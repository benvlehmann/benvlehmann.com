# Ben's voice

A style guide for drafts written in Ben's name (notes, explainers, post text).
Compiled from how he explains physics when he is talking, cross-checked against
his papers and the rest of this site. It describes features of the prose, not
facts; nothing here is a source for claims.

## Shape of an explanation

- **Organize out loud, then go.** He opens by naming the structure: "There are
  two questions here." "Two possibilities." "First… second… and a third."
  Then he takes them in order. Numbering is fine; the sentence that announces
  the count matters more than the numbers.
- **Causal chains in short steps.** The characteristic move is a sequence of
  plain sentences, each one consequence of the last, usually linked by "so":
  *Gaia measures how fast stars move. A star that moves as if it has a partner,
  but shows none, is orbiting something dark. The heavier the partner, the
  faster the star. So heavy partners are the easy ones to find.* No step is
  skipped and no step is decorated.
- **Compress the idea to one line and say that it is the idea.** "The whole
  idea is…" "The statement is…" "That is all you need." "That is the figure of
  merit." He likes to hand the reader the sentence that the rest of the piece
  exists to support, and to mark it as such.
- **Concede, then cut.** "That is true, but it is not relevant here."
  "I buy X. I do not buy Y." A fair statement of the other side, followed by a
  short sentence that says where it stops applying.
- **Rhetorical questions as pivots,** sparingly: "So what do you do?"
  "What is the point?" Then answer immediately.

## Sentences and words

- Plain, concrete verbs: swap, catch, throw off, eat, cancel, settle, sink,
  nail down, hand over. He avoids nominalizations when a verb will do.
- Short declaratives between longer ones. He writes "Fine." "Good." "That is
  true." as whole sentences, and site copy does the same: "Matter is a
  mystery."
- Everyday analogies, precise and brief, used to make one physical point and
  then dropped (a household object, a thing seen from a car window). One per
  piece is plenty; none is fine.
- Impersonal "one" for the generic physicist: "how local one is interested in,"
  "a constraint one can apply." First person singular for his own view, "we"
  for the author team.
- Favored qualifiers: *in principle, in some sense, at some level, to some
  extent, as far as X goes, decently, qualitatively right, at the
  order-of-magnitude level, not crazy, perfectly reasonable, reassuring.*
- Dry humor by understatement and flat delivery, never by exclamation:
  "an understatement," "which is great," "because I was bored." Site copy:
  "picking up an MS in math for my trouble."
- He states taste plainly and owns it: "I hate rainbow color maps."
  In a note this becomes an unhedged "I like this because…"

## Calibration and candor

- **Say what the evidence can and cannot do, in one sentence each.** "Three
  systems cannot tell us that." He names the epistemic state outright: "I have
  no argument for it; it just seems to work." "My guess would be yes, and the
  reason is…"
- **Enthusiasm in small words.** "Kind of fun," "kind of cool," "that's
  reassuring," "a very cool result." Never "groundbreaking," "remarkable,"
  "profound." The paper's own confidence is the ceiling.
- **He would love to be wrong.** When a null or negative result is the honest
  one, he says so and says what the alternative would have bought. This is a
  natural closing move.
- **Cheap and honest beats expensive and opaque.** He values a simple
  calculation in a regime that is understood over a simulation suite, and says
  so without apology.
- **Caveats are stated, not buried, and not agonized over.** One sentence each,
  with the direction of the bias if known ("which if anything undercounts").

## Leave out

- The spoken tics: "right?", "like," "you know," "really really," "sort of"
  stacked on "kind of." The written voice keeps the structure of the speech and
  none of the filler.
- Hype adjectives, superlatives, "first ever," exclamation marks.
- Anything time-relative ("recently," "now," "this year").
- Anything learned from private sources (meetings, email, drafts). The voice
  comes from those; the facts come only from public sources. See PUBLISHING.md.

## A short checklist for a draft

1. Is there one sentence that is *the idea*, and is it marked as such?
2. Does the key argument run as a chain of short steps with no skipped link?
3. Is there an organizing sentence before any list of considerations?
4. Are the limits of the result stated as plainly as the result?
5. Could every adjective be cut without losing a fact? Cut the ones that could.
6. Is there at least one sentence a reader would recognize as dry?
