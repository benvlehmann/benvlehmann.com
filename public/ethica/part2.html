Ethices Pars Secunda
De Natura et Origine Mentis

Second Part of the Ethics
Of the Nature and Origin of the Mind

Introduction to the Second Part

Part I deduced God and the necessity with which everything follows from him. Part II narrows the field at once. Spinoza will not explain everything that follows from God’s essence, for infinitely many things follow in infinitely many ways (Part I, Prop. 16), “but only those that can lead us, as if by the hand, to the knowledge of the human mind and its highest blessedness” (Preface). The word beatitudo points to the end of the book; the promise is redeemed only in Part V (Part V, Prop. 42). Part II is the first step toward it: a theory of the mind, of knowledge and of error, built on the metaphysics of Part I.

It is also the Part in which the reader first sees what the metaphysics of Part I is for. Substance, attribute and mode were abstract terms; in Part II they become a theory of what a human being is. A human being is a mode of God, and so is the mind, and so is the body, and the relation between the mind and the body is the relation between a mode of thought and a mode of extension. Much of what Part II says about perception, memory, error and knowledge follows from this one thesis, that the mind is the idea of the body. The rest of this introduction explains the problem Spinoza inherited, the doctrine he put in its place, and the theory of knowledge that grows out of it, and points to the places where readers most often go astray.

The problem it inherits

The problem is Descartes’. For Descartes the mind and the body are two created substances, a thinking and an extended one, each with its own principal attribute (Principles I.53), really distinct and yet united, so that the mind moves the body and is affected by it through a small gland in the brain. Spinoza returns to this view in the Preface to Part V, where he expresses his astonishment that a philosopher who had resolved to deduce nothing except from self-evident principles should have assumed a hypothesis “more occult than any occult quality.”

Part I has already removed the two substances: thought and extension are attributes of the one substance, God (Part I, Prop. 14, Cor. 2). Part II begins by stating this in form. Prop. 1 argues that “thought is an attribute of God, or God is a thinking thing,” from the existence of particular thoughts which must be conceived through some attribute, and adds in a scholium that we can conceive an infinite thinking being, since “the more things a thinking being can think, the more reality or perfection we conceive it to contain” (Prop. 1, Schol.). Prop. 2 does the same for extension. A reader who has followed Part I will not be surprised. What is new is the use to which these propositions are put. Mind and body are not two things that need to be joined; they are two modes, of two attributes, of one substance.

The definitions and axioms

The definitions and axioms bring in the vocabulary the rest of the Part needs, and several of them repay attention. A body is “a mode which expresses God’s essence, insofar as he is considered as an extended thing, in a certain and determinate way” (Def. 1): not a substance, and not a thing that could exist without God. The definition of essence (Def. 2) adds a reciprocal clause to the traditional one: the essence of a thing is that without which the thing can neither be nor be conceived, “and which in turn without the thing” can neither be nor be conceived. The addition matters, because without it God, without whom nothing can be or be conceived, would belong to the essence of everything; Spinoza explains the point later (Prop. 10, Cor., Schol.).

An idea is “a concept of the mind, which the mind forms because it is a thinking thing” (Def. 3), and the explanation adds: “I say concept rather than perception, because the word perception seems to indicate that the mind is acted on by the object. But concept seems to express an action of the mind.” Spinoza will insist on this throughout. An idea is not “something mute, like a picture on a panel” (Prop. 43, Schol.), an image waiting to be judged, but an act of thinking that already affirms or denies something. An adequate idea is one “which, insofar as it is considered in itself, without relation to its object, has all the properties, or intrinsic denominations, of a true idea” (Def. 4). The definition separates two things that ordinary usage runs together: truth, which is the agreement of an idea with its object (Part I, Ax. 6), and adequacy, which is a property the idea has in itself. Every adequate idea is true, but the converse is the burden of the second half of the Part.

Duration is “an indefinite continuation of existing” (Def. 5), indefinite because it cannot be determined by the nature of the existing thing itself. Reality and perfection are the same (Def. 6), which means that perfection is not a moral or aesthetic grade but a measure of being. And singular things are “things that are finite and have a determinate existence,” to which Spinoza adds that several individuals which so concur in one action that all are together the cause of one effect may be considered “as one singular thing” (Def. 7). The clause prepares the account of composite bodies in the physical digression, and with it the possibility that a human being, a society or the whole of nature may count as one individual.

The axioms include the only empirical premises of the Part. “Man thinks” (Ax. 2). “We feel that a certain body is affected in many ways” (Ax. 4). “We neither feel nor perceive any singular things except bodies and modes of thinking” (Ax. 5). Spinoza is sparing with appeals to experience, and these are the ones he allows himself. Another axiom will matter later: modes of thinking such as love and desire are not given unless there is in the same individual an idea of the thing loved or desired, though an idea can be given without any other mode of thinking (Ax. 3). The idea is the basic mode of thought; the affects of Part III will be ideas of a certain kind.

Ideas in God

Props. 3–6 work out the place of ideas in God. “In God there is necessarily an idea both of his essence and of all things that necessarily follow from his essence” (Prop. 3), and this idea is unique (Prop. 4). Ideas, considered in their own being as modes of thinking, their “formal being,” are caused by God only insofar as he is a thinking thing, and not by the things they are ideas of (Prop. 5); and in general the modes of each attribute have God for their cause only insofar as he is considered under that attribute (Prop. 6). Causation does not cross from one attribute to another.

The corollary to Prop. 6 draws a consequence that cuts against a long tradition: “the formal being of things which are not modes of thinking does not follow from the divine nature because God first knew the things; rather, the things that are the objects of ideas follow and are inferred from their own attributes in the same way and with the same necessity as we have shown ideas to follow from the attribute of thought” (Prop. 6, Cor.). On the traditional picture, God creates the world according to the ideas in his intellect, as an architect builds according to a plan. Spinoza denies it. Bodies do not follow from God’s ideas of them; they follow from extension, as ideas follow from thought. Long before human minds appear, the ground has been taken away from any interaction between mind and body. Part III will say so outright: “The body cannot determine the mind to thinking, nor can the mind determine the body to motion, or to rest, or to anything else (if there is anything else)” (Part III, Prop. 2).

Parallelism and identity

Prop. 7 states the doctrine for which Part II is best known: “The order and connection of ideas is the same as the order and connection of things.” Commentators call it Spinoza’s “parallelism”; the word is not his (M. Della Rocca, Representation and the Mind-Body Problem in Spinoza, 1996, p. 19). The demonstration is a single line, an appeal to the axiom that the knowledge of an effect depends on the knowledge of its cause (Part I, Ax. 4). As Della Rocca points out, the axiom by itself does not guarantee that every thing has an idea; for that Spinoza needs Prop. 3 (pp. 22–23). The corollary puts the doctrine in terms of power: “God’s power of thinking is equal to his actual power of acting; that is, whatever follows formally from God’s infinite nature, all of it follows objectively in God, in the same order and with the same connection, from the idea of God” (Prop. 7, Cor.). The scholastic pair of “formal” and “objective” being, which Descartes had used in the third Meditation (AT VII 40–41), here names the two sides of one order: a thing as it is in itself, and the same thing as it is represented in an idea.

The scholium goes further than the proposition. It recalls that “the thinking substance and the extended substance are one and the same substance, which is comprehended now under this attribute, now under that,” and concludes: “So also a mode of extension and the idea of that mode are one and the same thing, but expressed in two ways” (Prop. 7, Schol.). The claim is no longer that two series correspond, but that each pair of corresponding items is one thing. Martial Gueroult describes the step as a passage from the identity of the chains to the identity of the things chained, and notes that Prop. 7 is proved by an axiom about knowledge, while the scholium explains by ontological truths why it holds (Spinoza II: L’âme, 1974, pp. 85–86). The second half of the scholium then guards the distinction of the attributes: although a circle existing in nature and the idea of that circle are one and the same thing, the idea is to be explained only through other modes of thinking, the circle only through other modes of extension. One thing, two explanations, and no explanation that passes from one attribute to the other.

Prop. 8 adds a doctrine that will be needed in Part V. The ideas of singular things that do not exist are comprehended in God’s infinite idea in the same way as the formal essences of those things are contained in God’s attributes. The scholium gives an illustration, which Spinoza admits cannot be adequate, since the case is unique: in a circle there are infinitely many rectangles formed from the segments of intersecting chords, all equal to one another, and none of them can be said to exist except insofar as the circle exists; but if two of them are actually drawn, their ideas now exist not only as comprehended in the idea of the circle but also as involving the existence of those rectangles (Prop. 8, Schol.). The distinction between a thing’s essence as contained in God’s attributes and its existence in duration is the distinction on which Part V’s account of the eternity of the mind will turn.

The mind as the idea of the body

Applied to the human being, all this becomes the thesis of Props. 11–13. “The first thing that constitutes the actual being of a human mind is nothing but the idea of some singular thing actually existing” (Prop. 11). The mind is therefore “part of the infinite intellect of God,” and when we say that the human mind perceives something, “we are saying nothing but that God, not insofar as he is infinite, but insofar as he is explained through the nature of the human mind, or insofar as he constitutes the essence of the human mind, has this or that idea” (Prop. 11, Cor.). Spinoza expects resistance at this point: readers “will come to a halt and think of many things that give them pause,” and he asks them “not to pass judgment on these matters until they have read through everything” (Prop. 11, Schol.). Whatever happens in the object of the idea constituting the mind must be perceived by the mind (Prop. 12), and that object is “a body, or a certain mode of extension actually existing, and nothing else” (Prop. 13). The corollary draws the conclusion “that man consists of a mind and a body, and that the human body exists as we feel it” (Prop. 13, Cor.).

The scholium to Prop. 13 makes two remarks that every reader should keep in view. The first is that “the things we have shown so far are very general and pertain no more to men than to other individuals, all of which, though in different degrees, are nevertheless animate” (Prop. 13, Schol.). There is an idea in God of every body, and so, in the sense defined, a mind of every body. Jonathan Bennett calls the resulting position panpsychism, and argues that Spinoza is driven to it because, once mind and body are conceptually independent, no line drawn across nature between things that have minds and things that do not could be anything but arbitrary; he adds that this “is not to say that every physical thing has a mind properly so-called, or indeed that it has anything we would recognize as mental” (A Study of Spinoza’s Ethics, § 33, pp. 137–138). The second remark is that minds differ as their bodies do: “the more a body is more capable than others of doing many things at once, or of being acted on in many ways at once, the more its mind is more capable than others of perceiving many things at once.” To know what is distinctive about the human mind, then, we must know what is distinctive about the human body, and Spinoza interrupts the propositions at this point to give a brief account of bodies, the physical digression introduced in the essay that follows the scholium.

Imagination, memory and inadequate knowledge

The propositions that follow the physical digression build an account of perception on this foundation. The idea of any way in which the human body is affected by external bodies “must involve the nature of the human body and at the same time the nature of the external body” (Prop. 16), and so “the ideas we have of external bodies indicate the constitution of our own body more than the nature of the external bodies” (Prop. 16, Cor. 2). When the body is affected in a way that involves the nature of an external body, the mind will regard that body as present until the body is affected in a way that excludes its presence (Prop. 17), which explains how we can regard as present things that no longer exist. Spinoza calls the affections of the body whose ideas represent external bodies as present to us “images of things,” “even though they do not reproduce the figures of things,” and when the mind regards bodies in this way he says that it “imagines” (Prop. 17, Schol.).

The word “imagination” therefore has a much wider sense in the Ethics than in English. It covers sense perception as well as fantasy, and it means every way of perceiving things through the affections of one’s own body. Spinoza adds at once that imagination is not in itself an error: “the imaginations of the mind, considered in themselves, contain no error,” and the mind errs only insofar as it lacks an idea that excludes the existence of the things it imagines as present. Memory, likewise, is “a certain concatenation of ideas involving the nature of things which are outside the human body,” which takes place “according to the order and concatenation of the affections of the human body,” and not according to the order of the intellect (Prop. 18, Schol.). The example is memorable. A soldier who sees the tracks of a horse in the sand passes at once from the thought of a horse to that of a horseman, and from there to the thought of war; a farmer passes from the thought of a horse to that of a plough and a field. Each follows the order in which the images were joined in his own body.

The consequences for self-knowledge are severe. The mind does not know the human body, nor that it exists, except through the ideas of the body’s affections (Prop. 19); it does not know itself except insofar as it perceives these ideas (Prop. 23); it has no adequate knowledge of the parts of its body (Prop. 24), of external bodies (Prop. 25), or of the duration of its body or of other things (Props. 30–31). The conclusion is drawn in the corollary to Prop. 29: whenever the mind “perceives things from the common order of nature,” it has “neither of itself, nor of its own body, nor of external bodies an adequate knowledge, but only a confused and mutilated one” (Prop. 29, Cor.). The scholium adds the qualification on which the rest of the book depends. This holds whenever the mind is determined externally, “from the fortuitous encounter with things,” to regard this or that, and not whenever it is determined internally, “from the fact that it contemplates several things at once, to understanding their agreements, differences and oppositions” (Prop. 29, Schol.). There is another way of thinking, and the second half of Part II describes it.

Truth, error and the three kinds of knowledge

The account of truth and error begins with a surprising claim: “All ideas, insofar as they are related to God, are true” (Prop. 32), and “there is nothing positive in ideas on account of which they are called false” (Prop. 33). Falsity “consists in the privation of knowledge which inadequate, or mutilated and confused, ideas involve” (Prop. 35). The scholium gives two examples. Men think themselves free because they are conscious of their actions and ignorant of their causes. And when we look at the sun, “we imagine it to be about two hundred feet away from us; an error which does not consist in this imagining alone, but in the fact that while we imagine it so, we are ignorant of its true distance and of the cause of this imagining” (Prop. 35, Schol.). Even when we learn its true distance, we still imagine it near, since the image expresses the way our body is affected by the sun. Error is not a positive thing added to an idea, such as an act of misjudgment; it is an idea that is true as far as it goes but incomplete, taken for complete.

The escape from inadequacy begins with what Spinoza calls common notions. Some things are common to all bodies and are equally in the part and in the whole, and these “can only be conceived adequately” (Prop. 38), since whatever idea we have of them, from whatever part of our body, is complete. From such notions, and from the adequate ideas of the properties of things that follow from them, reason proceeds. The second scholium to Prop. 40 then sets out the doctrine of the three kinds of knowledge. The first kind, “opinion, or imagination,” comprises knowledge “from random experience,” built up from singular things perceived through the senses “in a mutilated, confused way and without order for the intellect,” and knowledge from signs, as when we hear or read words and recollect the things they signify. The second kind, “reason,” proceeds from common notions and adequate ideas of the properties of things. The third, “intuitive knowledge,” “proceeds from an adequate idea of the formal essence of certain attributes of God to the adequate knowledge of the essence of things.”

Spinoza illustrates the three with a single example. Given three numbers, one is to find a fourth that is to the third as the second is to the first. Merchants multiply the second by the third and divide by the first, either because they remember what they were told without demonstration, or because they have often found it so with simple numbers, or because they know the demonstration in Euclid. “But in the simplest numbers none of this is needed. For example, given the numbers 1, 2, 3, no one fails to see that the fourth proportional number is 6,” and this more clearly, because from the ratio we see at a glance between the first and the second, we infer the fourth itself. The first kind of knowledge is the only cause of falsity; the second and third are necessarily true (Prop. 41), and they alone teach us to distinguish the true from the false (Prop. 42).

Two further theses complete the account. The first is that truth is its own standard. “He who has a true idea knows at the same time that he has a true idea, and cannot doubt the truth of the thing” (Prop. 43), for “just as light makes manifest both itself and darkness, so truth is the standard both of itself and of the false” (Prop. 43, Schol.). Spinoza is answering the sceptic’s demand for a criterion of truth outside the true idea, which would need a further criterion, and so to infinity. The second is that “it is of the nature of reason to perceive things under a certain species of eternity” (Prop. 44, Cor. 2): reason regards things as necessary, not as contingent, and so as following from the eternal nature of God. Part II ends this line of argument with a claim that may seem breathtaking: “The human mind has an adequate knowledge of the eternal and infinite essence of God” (Prop. 47). Why then do men not have so clear a knowledge of God as of the common notions? Because they cannot imagine God as they imagine bodies, and because “they have joined the name God to the images of things which they are accustomed to see.” “And indeed most errors consist in this alone, that we do not apply names to things correctly” (Prop. 47, Schol.).

Will and intellect

Part II ends with two propositions about the will and the longest scholium of the Part. They deny that there is in the mind any absolute or free will, identify each volition with the affirmation or negation that an idea involves, and conclude that “will and intellect are one and the same.” The polemical target is Descartes’ theory of judgment and error, and the uses Spinoza draws from the doctrine at the end of the scholium sketch the program of the rest of the book. An essay before Prop. 48 introduces them.

Questions the reader will meet

Several features of Part II regularly mislead readers, and it may help to name them in advance.

The first is the word “idea.” For Spinoza an idea is an act of thinking that involves affirmation, not a picture or a mental image, and it is not necessarily conscious. The idea that constitutes the human mind is the idea of the body, but we are not aware of most of what happens in our bodies; Spinoza’s claim that whatever happens in the body is “perceived” by the mind (Prop. 12) uses “perceive” in a sense in which having an idea of something does not mean noticing it. The theory of consciousness that would make this intelligible is one of the least developed parts of the Ethics, and the reader should not expect to find it worked out.

The second concerns the other attributes. If each thing is expressed in infinitely many attributes, and there is an idea of each expression, why does the human mind know only the body? Spinoza’s reply to Tschirnhaus, which refers back to the scholium to Prop. 7, is that those infinitely many ideas do not constitute one mind but infinitely many, having no connection with one another (Letter 66). The human mind is the idea of the body, and of nothing else (Prop. 13).

The third concerns the identity of mind and body. Part III states the outcome of Part II in the words of the scholium to Prop. 7: “the mind and the body are one and the same thing, which is conceived now under the attribute of thought, now under the attribute of extension” (Part III, Prop. 2, Schol.). But how can a mode of extension and a mode of thought be one thing, if neither can be conceived or explained through the other? Della Rocca has argued that the answer lies in the “referential opacity” of explanatory contexts in Spinoza, which lets one and the same thing be explained under one attribute and not under another (1996, ch. 7). Others take the identity less strictly, as a correspondence between two series of modes. The question is important because Spinoza’s whole account of the affects depends on it. Appendix C takes up both this question and that of the other attributes.

The fourth is the relation between adequacy and truth. Every idea is true insofar as it is related to God, since in God’s intellect it is part of a complete idea; it is inadequate in us because our mind has only a part of that idea. Inadequacy is a matter of what a mind contains, and the same idea can be adequate in God and inadequate in us. This is why Spinoza can say both that all ideas are true and that most of ours are confused.

A last difficulty is the scope of Part II’s claims about the human mind. The propositions about imagination and memory describe the mind insofar as it is determined from outside, and they paint a bleak picture. The propositions about common notions and the third kind of knowledge describe the mind insofar as it is determined from within, and they paint a hopeful one. Both are true of every human mind; the question the rest of the Ethics asks is how the proportion between them can be changed.

The place of Part II in the whole

The doctrines of Part II carry the rest of the book. Part III will define the affects as affections of the body together with the ideas of those affections, and its account of the passions will rest on the theory of inadequate ideas worked out here. The denial of mind-body interaction allows Spinoza to treat human emotions as natural things, to be understood by their causes like lines, planes and bodies. Part IV’s distinction between being led by reason and being led by the affects is the practical form of the distinction between the second kind of knowledge and the first. And Part V’s account of the mind’s freedom and eternity returns to three propositions of this Part: to Prop. 8 and its corollary on the ideas of things insofar as they are contained in God’s attributes, to the third kind of knowledge of Prop. 40, Schol. 2, and to the doctrine that reason perceives things under a species of eternity (Prop. 44, Cor. 2).

The text begins with the Preface, a single sentence that narrows the field from everything that follows from God to what concerns the human mind, and then with the definitions. As in Part I, they are short and worth reading twice; several of them, above all those of idea and of adequate idea, are not what the reader expects.

Propositio I

Proposition I

Cogitatio attributum Dei est, sive Deus est res cogitans.
Thought is an attribute of God, or God is a thinking thing.

Cited in 2P2D, 2P3D, 2P20D

Demonstratio. Singulares cogitationes sive haec et illa cogitatio modi sunt, qui Dei naturam certo et determinato modo exprimunt (per coroll. prop. 25. P. 1.). Competit ergo Deo (per defin. 5. P. 1.) attributum, cuius conceptum singulares omnes cogitationes involvunt, per quod etiam concipiuntur. Est igitur cogitatio unum ex infinitis Dei attributis, quod Dei aeternam et infinitam essentiam exprimit (vide defin. 6. P. 1.), sive Deus est res cogitans. Q. E. D.
Demonstration. Singular thoughts, or this and that thought, are modes which express God’s nature in a certain and determinate way (by Cor. of Prop. 25, Part I). There pertains to God, therefore (by Def. 5, Part I), an attribute whose concept all singular thoughts involve, and through which they are also conceived. Thought, therefore, is one of God’s infinite attributes, which expresses God’s eternal and infinite essence (see Def. 6, Part I), or God is a thinking thing. Q.E.D.
Scholium. Patet etiam haec propositio ex hoc, quod nos possumus ens cogitans infinitum concipere. Nam quo plura ens cogitans potest cogitare, eo plus realitatis sive perfectionis idem continere concipimus. Ergo ens, quod infinita infinitis modis cogitare potest, est necessario virtute cogitandi infinitum. Cum itaque ad solam cogitationem attendendo ens infinitum concipiamus, est necessario (per defin. 4.et 6. P. 1.) cogitatio unum ex infinitis Dei attributis, ut volebamus.
Scholium. This proposition is also evident from the fact that we can conceive an infinite thinking being. For the more things a thinking being can think, the more reality or perfection we conceive it to contain. Therefore a being that can think infinitely many things in infinitely many ways is necessarily infinite in its power of thinking. Since, therefore, attending only to thought, we conceive an infinite being, thought is necessarily (by Defs. 4 and 6, Part I) one of God’s infinite attributes, as we wished.

Propositio III

Proposition III

In Deo datur necessario idea tam eius essentiae, quam omnium, quae ex ipsius essentia necessario sequuntur.
In God there is necessarily an idea both of his essence and of all things that necessarily follow from his essence.1
  1. 1 The idea of God was Spinoza’s example of an infinite mode of thought in Part I, Prop. 21, Dem.; Prop. 4 shows that it is unique.

Cited in 2P5D, 2P9CD, 2P20D, 2P24D, 5P22D, 5P35D

Demonstratio. Deus enim (per prop. 1. huius) infinita infinitis modis cogitare, sive (quod idem est per prop. 16. P. 1.) ideam suae essentiae et omnium, quae necessario ex ea sequuntur, formare potest. Atqui omne id, quod in Dei potestate est, necessario est (per prop. 35. P. 1.). Ergo datur necessario talis idea, et (per prop. 15. P. 1.) non nisi in Deo. Q. E. D.
Demonstration. For God (by Prop. 1 of this Part) can think infinitely many things in infinitely many ways, or (what is the same, by Prop. 16, Part I) can form the idea of his essence and of all things that necessarily follow from it. But all that is in God’s power1 necessarily is (by Prop. 35, Part I). Therefore there is necessarily such an idea, and (by Prop. 15, Part I) only in God. Q.E.D.
  1. 1 in Dei potestate: on potestas, see the note on Part I, Prop. 17, Schol..
Scholium. Vulgus per Dei potentiam intelligit Dei liberam voluntatem et ius in omnia quae sunt, quaeque propterea communiter ut contingentia considerantur. Deum enim potestatem omnia destruendi habere dicunt et in nihilum redigendi. Dei porro potentiam cum potentia regum saepissime comparant.
Scholium. By God’s power the common people understand God’s free will and his right over all things that are, which are therefore commonly considered as contingent. For they say that God has the power to destroy all things and reduce them to nothing. Further, they very often compare God’s power with the power of kings.
Sed hoc in coroll. 1. et 2. prop. 32. P. 1. refutavimus; et prop. 16. P. 1. ostendimus, Deum eadem necessitate agere, qua se ipsum intelligit; hoc est, sicuti ex necessitate divinae naturae sequitur (sicut omnes uno ore statuunt), ut Deus se ipsum intelligat, eadem etiam necessitate sequitur, ut Deus infinita infinitis modis agat. Deinde prop. 34. P. 1. ostendimus, Dei potentiam nihil esse, praeterquam Dei actuosam essentiam; adeoque tam nobis impossibile est concipere, Deum non agere, quam Deum non esse.
But we have refuted this in Cors. 1 and 2 of Prop. 32, Part I; and in Prop. 16, Part I we have shown that God acts with the same necessity by which he understands himself; that is, just as it follows from the necessity of the divine nature (as all maintain with one voice) that God understands himself, with the same necessity it also follows that God does infinitely many things in infinitely many ways. Next, in Prop. 34, Part I we have shown that God’s power is nothing but God’s active essence;1 and so it is as impossible for us to conceive that God does not act as that God does not exist.
  1. 1 actuosam essentiam: actuosus, ‘full of activity’, a rare word, chosen to say that God’s essence is itself activity, not a capacity that may or may not be exercised.
Porro si haec ulterius persequi liberet, possem hic etiam ostendere,a potentiam illam, quam vulgus Deo affingit, non tantum humanam esse (quod ostendit Deum hominem, vel instar hominis a vulgo concipi), sed etiam impotentiam involvere. Sed nolo de eadem re toties sermonem instituere.
Further, if it pleased me to pursue this matter further, I could also show here that the power which the common people ascribe to God is not only human (which shows that God is conceived by the common people as a man, or like a man), but also involves lack of power. But I do not wish to speak about the same thing so often.
  1. a The 1677 edition (OP p. 43) and Bartuschat repeat ulterius here. Gebhardt (G II 87; Textgestaltung II 357) corrects the second ulterius to etiam, from the Dutch version (ik zou hier ook konnen tonen); Meijer, Baensch and Appuhn delete it, which the Dutch ook (‘also’) forbids.
Lectorem solummodo iterum atque iterum rogo, ut quae in P. 1. ex prop. 16. usque ad finem de hac re dicta sunt, semel atque iterum perpendat. Nam nemo ea quae volo percipere recte poterit, nisi magnopere caveat, ne Dei potentiam cum humana regum potentia vel iure confundat.
I only ask the reader again and again to weigh, once and again, what has been said on this matter in Part I from Prop. 16 to the end. For no one will be able to perceive rightly the things I intend unless he takes great care not to confuse God’s power with the human power or right of kings.

Propositio IV

Proposition IV

[G II 88] Idea Dei, ex qua infinita infinitis modis sequuntur, unica tantum esse potest.
The idea of God, from which infinitely many things follow in infinitely many ways, can only be unique.
Demonstratio. Intellectus infinitus nihil praeter Dei attributa eiusque affectiones comprehendit (per prop. 30. P. 1.). Atqui Deus est unicus (per coroll. 1. prop. 14. P. 1.). Ergo idea Dei, ex qua infinita infinitis modis sequuntur, unica tantum esse potest. Q. E. D.
Demonstration. The infinite intellect comprehends nothing except God’s attributes and his affections (by Prop. 30, Part I). But God is unique (by Cor. 1 of Prop. 14, Part I). Therefore the idea of God, from which infinitely many things follow in infinitely many ways, can only be unique. Q.E.D.

Propositio V

Proposition V

Esse formale idearum Deum, quatenus tantum ut res cogitans consideratur, pro causa agnoscit et non, quatenus alio attributo explicatur; hoc est, tam Dei attributorum, quam rerum singularium ideae non ipsa ideata sive res perceptas pro causa efficiente agnoscunt, sed ipsum Deum, quatenus est res cogitans.a
The formal being of ideas1 admits God as a cause only insofar as he is considered as a thinking thing, and not insofar as he is explained by any other attribute; that is, the ideas both of God’s attributes and of singular things admit as their efficient cause not the objects themselves, or the things perceived, but God himself, insofar as he is a thinking thing.
  1. a The Dutch version of 1677 gives the proposition in an earlier form: ‘God, voor zo veel hy alleenlijk als een denkende zaak aangemerkt word, is oorzaak van ’t vormelijk wezen der denkbeelden; maar niet voor zo veel hy door een andere toeëigening uitgedrukt en verklaart word: dat is, God alleen, voor zo veel hy een denkende zaak is, en niet de voorwerpen der denkbeelden, zijn de werkende oorzaak van alle denkbeelden, zo wel die Gods toeëigeningen, als die de bezondere dingen uitdrukken’ (‘God, insofar as he is considered only as a thinking thing, is the cause of the formal being of ideas, but not insofar as he is expressed and explained by another attribute; that is, God alone, insofar as he is a thinking thing, and not the objects of the ideas, is the efficient cause of all ideas, both those that express God’s attributes and those that express singular things’). Gebhardt gives it in his Textgestaltung (II 357–358), not in his text.
  1. 1 esse formale idearum: the being an idea has in its own right, as a mode of thinking, as distinct from its objective being, the being of what it represents (see the note on formaliter and obiective at Part I, Prop. 17, Schol.). The proposition denies that the object of an idea is its cause.
Demonstratio. Patet quidem ex prop. 3. huius. Ibi enim concludebamus, Deum ideam suae essentiae et omnium, quae ex ea necessario sequuntur, formare posse ex hoc solo, nempe quod Deus est res cogitans, et non ex eo, quod sit suae ideae obiectum. Quare esse formale idearum Deum, quatenus est res cogitans, pro causa agnoscit.
Demonstration. This is evident from Prop. 3 of this Part. For there we concluded that God can form the idea of his essence and of all things that necessarily follow from it from this alone, namely that God is a thinking thing, and not from his being the object of his idea. So the formal being of ideas admits God as a cause insofar as he is a thinking thing.
Sed aliter hoc modo demonstratur. Esse formale idearum modus est cogitandi (ut per se notum), hoc est (per coroll. prop. 25. P. 1.) modus, qui Dei naturam, quatenus est res cogitans, certo modo exprimit, adeoque (per prop. 10. P. 1.) nullius alterius attributi Dei conceptum involvit, et consequenter (per axiom. 4. P. 1.) nullius alterius attributi, nisi cogitationis, est effectus. Adeoque esse formale idearum Deum, quatenus tantum ut res cogitans consideratur etc. Q. E. D.
But it is demonstrated otherwise in this way. The formal being of ideas is a mode of thinking (as is self-evident), that is (by Cor. of Prop. 25, Part I), a mode which expresses God’s nature, insofar as he is a thinking thing, in a certain way, and so (by Prop. 10, Part I) involves the concept of no other attribute of God, and consequently (by Ax. 4, Part I) is the effect of no other attribute except thought. And so the formal being of ideas admits God as a cause insofar as he is considered only as a thinking thing, etc. Q.E.D.

Proposition VI

[G II 89] Cuiuscumque attributi modi Deum, quatenus tantum sub illo attributo, cuius modi sunt, et non quatenus sub ullo alio consideratur, pro causa habent.
The modes of each attribute have God for their cause only insofar as he is considered under the attribute of which they are modes, and not insofar as he is considered under any other.

Demonstratio. Unumquodque enim attributum per se absque alio concipitur (per prop. 10. P. 1.). Quare uniuscuiusque attributi modi conceptum sui attributi, non autem alterius involvunt; adeoque (per axiom. 4. P. 1.) Deum, quatenus tantum sub illo attributo, cuius modi sunt, et non, quatenus sub ullo alio consideratur, pro causa habent. Q. E. D.
Demonstration. For each attribute is conceived through itself without any other (by Prop. 10, Part I). So the modes of each attribute involve the concept of their own attribute, but not of another; and so (by Ax. 4, Part I) they have God for their cause only insofar as he is considered under the attribute of which they are modes, and not insofar as he is considered under any other. Q.E.D.
Corollarium. Hinc sequitur, quod esse formale rerum, quae modi non sunt cogitandi, non sequitur ideo ex divina natura, quia res prius cognovit; sed eodem modo eademque necessitate res ideatae ex suis attributis consequuntur et concluduntur, ac ideas ex attributo cogitationis consequi ostendimus.
Corollary. From this it follows that the formal being of things which are not modes of thinking does not follow from the divine nature because God first knew the things;1 rather, the things that are the objects of ideas follow and are inferred from their own attributes in the same way and with the same necessity as we have shown ideas to follow from the attribute of thought.

Propositio VII

Proposition VII

Ordo et connexio idearum idem est, ac ordo et connexio rerum.
The order and connection of ideas is the same as the order and connection of things.1
  1. 1 The doctrine is usually called Spinoza’s ‘parallelism’; the word is the commentators’, not his. M.   1. 1 As Della Rocca observes, the axiom gives only that wherever there is an idea of an effect there is an idea of its cause on which it depends; that there is an idea of every thing at all needs Prop. 3 (1996, pp. 22–23).

Corollary. From this it follows that God’s power of thinking is equal to his actual power of acting; that is, whatever follows formally from God’s infinite nature, all of it follows objectively in God, in the same order and with the same connection, from the idea of God.

Propositio VIII

Proposition VIII

Ideae rerum singularium sive modorum non existentium ita debent comprehendi in Dei infinita idea, ac rerum singularium sive modorum essentiae formales in Dei attributis continentur.
The ideas of singular things, or of modes, that do not exist must be comprehended in God’s infinite idea in the same way as the formal essences of singular things, or modes, are contained in God’s attributes.1
  1. 1 The essences of things that do not now exist are contained in the attributes, and their ideas in the idea of God. The doctrine returns in Part V, where there is in God ‘an idea which expresses the essence of this or that human body under a species of eternity’ (Part V, Prop. 22).

Proposition IX

Proposition X

Propositio XI

Proposition XI

[G II 94] Primum, quod actuale mentis humanae esse constituit, nihil aliud est, quam idea rei alicuius singularis actu existentis.
The first thing that constitutes the actual being of a human mind is nothing but the idea of some singular thing actually existing.

Demonstratio. Essentia hominis (per coroll. prop. praeced.) a certis Dei attributorum modis constituitur; nempe (per axiom. 2 huius) a modis cogitandi, quorum omnium (per axiom. 3. huius) idea natura prior est, et ea data reliqui modi (quibus scilicet idea natura prior est) in eodem debent esse individuo (per axiom. 3. huius).a Atque adeo idea primum est, quod humanae mentis esse constituit.
Demonstration. The essence of man (by the Cor. of the preceding Prop.) is constituted by certain modes of God’s attributes, namely (by Ax. 2 of this Part) by modes of thinking, of all of which (by Ax. 3 of this Part) the idea is prior in nature, and, the idea being given, the other modes (to which, that is, the idea is prior in nature) must be in the same individual (by Ax. 3 of this Part). And so an idea is the first thing that constitutes the being of a human mind.

At non idea rei non existentis; nam tum (per coroll. prop. 8. huius) ipsa idea non posset dici existere. Erit ergo idea rei actu existentis. At non rei infinitae. Res namque infinita (per prop. 21. et 22. P. 1.)b debet semper necessario existere. Atqui hoc (per axiom. 1. huius) est absurdum. Ergo primum, quod esse humanae mentis actuale constituit, est idea rei singularis actu existentis. Q. E. D.
But not the idea of a thing that does not exist; for then (by Cor. of Prop. 8 of this Part) the idea itself could not be said to exist. It will therefore be the idea of a thing actually existing. But not of an infinite thing. For an infinite thing (by Props. 21 and 22, Part I) must always exist necessarily. But this (by Ax. 1 of this Part) is absurd. Therefore the first thing that constitutes the actual being of a human mind is the idea of a singular thing actually existing. Q.E.D.

From this it follows that the human mind is part of the infinite intellect of God.1 And therefore, when we say that the human mind perceives this or that, we are saying nothing but that God, not insofar as he is infinite, but insofar as he is explained through the nature of the human mind, or insofar as he constitutes the essence of the human mind, has this or that idea; and when we say that God has this or that idea not only insofar as he constitutes the nature of the human mind, but insofar as he also has, together with the human mind, the idea of another thing, then we say that the human mind perceives the thing partially, or inadequately.2
  1. 1 Compare the end of Part V: our mind, insofar as it understands, ‘is an eternal mode of thinking, which is determined by another eternal mode of thinking, and this again by another, and so on to infinity; so that all together constitute God’s eternal and infinite intellect’ (Part V, Prop. 40, Schol.).
  2. 2 The first account of inadequate knowledge: an idea in the human mind is inadequate when God has it not only insofar as he constitutes this mind but insofar as he also has the idea of something else, which this mind does not contain. The account is applied in Props. 24–31.

Proposition XII

Propositio XIII

Proposition XIII

[G II 96] Obiectum ideae humanam mentem constituentis est corpus, sive certus extensionis modus actu existens, et nihil aliud.

The Physical Digression

At this point the numbered propositions of Part II stop, and a short treatise on bodies takes their place: axioms, lemmas and postulates about motion and rest, about composite bodies, and about the human body. A reader may well wonder why a Part on the mind should turn to physics. The answer lies in the scholium to Prop. 13, which has just drawn the consequence of the preceding propositions: the human mind is the idea of the human body, and what has been shown of it holds of the idea of any thing, for all individuals are “animate,” though in different degrees. What distinguishes one mind from another can therefore be learned only from what distinguishes its body: “the more a body is more capable than others of doing many things at once, or of being acted on in many ways at once, the more its mind is more capable than others of perceiving many things at once.” To make use of this, Spinoza needs some account of bodies, and he gives one, briefly and, as he says, not as a treatise on body for its own sake (Lemma 7, Schol.). The pages that follow are compressed and unadorned, and their purpose is easy to lose sight of; it may help to see in advance what they contain and what the rest of the Ethics takes from them.

The plan of the digression

The section has two axioms, three lemmas with a corollary, three more axioms and a definition, four more lemmas with a scholium, and six postulates about the human body. It has no propositions of its own and is often called the “physical digression,” though it is not a digression in the argument; the propositions that follow it depend on it throughout. It falls into three parts. The first concerns the simplest bodies, which differ from one another only in motion and rest; the second, composite bodies, or individuals, and the conditions under which they keep their nature; the third, the human body in particular. The order is the order of the Ethics as a whole, which proceeds from what is general to what concerns human beings, and from simple things to complex ones.

The simplest bodies

“All bodies are either in motion or at rest,” and “each body moves now more slowly, now more quickly” (Axioms 1–2). On these two axioms Spinoza builds three lemmas. “Bodies are distinguished from one another by reason of motion and rest, speed and slowness, and not by reason of substance” (Lemma 1): there is only one substance, and extension is indivisible, so whatever makes one body this body and not another must be a difference in its modes. “All bodies agree in certain things” (Lemma 2), namely that they involve the concept of one attribute and that they can move more slowly or more quickly, or be at rest. And a body in motion or at rest “must have been determined to motion or rest by another body, which was also determined to motion or rest by another, and that again by another, and so on to infinity” (Lemma 3), the physical counterpart of Part I, Prop. 28.

The corollary to Lemma 3 states a principle of persistence: “a body in motion moves until it is determined to rest by another body, and ... a body at rest also remains at rest until it is determined to motion by another” (Lemma 3, Cor.). Descartes had made the same principle his first law of nature: each thing, “quantum in se est, semper in eodem statu perseveret” (“as far as is in it, always perseveres in the same state,” Principles II.37). The phrase quantum in se est will return, applied to all things, in the proposition of Part III that states the striving of each thing to persevere in its being (Part III, Prop. 6). The principle of inertia, in other words, is the physical form of the doctrine on which Spinoza’s whole psychology rests.

A second group of axioms follows. The first states that “all the ways in which a body is affected by another body follow from the nature of the body affected and at the same time from the nature of the affecting body,” so that the same body is moved differently by different bodies, and different bodies are moved differently by the same body (Axiom 1). This axiom will be cited more often than anything else in the digression, since it is the ground of the doctrine that our ideas of external bodies involve the nature of our own body as well as theirs (Prop. 16). The second is the law of reflection, with a diagram in the first edition: a body in motion that strikes another at rest which it cannot move is reflected, and the angle of reflection equals the angle of incidence (Axiom 2).

Spinoza took a serious interest in the physics of his day. In a letter to Henry Oldenburg, the secretary of the Royal Society, he corrected a misunderstanding: he had not said that almost all of Descartes’ rules of motion were false; it was Christiaan Huygens who thought so, and he himself held only the sixth of Descartes’ rules to be false, adding that he thought Huygens mistaken about it too (Letter 32; VVL³ III 122). The digression does not enter into such questions. It takes from physics only what the theory of the mind will need.

Composite bodies

The second part concerns composite bodies, and its central notion is the definition placed after the second group of axioms. “When a number of bodies, of the same or of different size, are so constrained by other bodies that they lie upon one another, or, if they move with the same or with different degrees of speed, so that they communicate their motions to one another in a certain fixed ratio, we shall say that those bodies are united with one another, and that all together compose one body, or individual, which is distinguished from the others by this union of bodies” (Definition). The form of an individual is thus not a substantial form, as in the scholastic philosophy, nor a mere aggregate of parts, but a pattern: a fixed ratio of motion and rest among the parts. An axiom adds that parts which lie upon one another over large surfaces are harder to displace, and on this Spinoza bases the distinction between hard, soft and fluid bodies (Axiom 3).

Lemmas 4–7 draw the consequences for identity through change. If some of the bodies composing an individual are separated from it, and as many others of the same nature take their place, the individual “will retain its nature as before, without any change of its form” (Lemma 4). It keeps its nature if its parts grow or shrink in proportion, so that they keep the same ratio of motion and rest to one another (Lemma 5); if they turn their motions in another direction while continuing to communicate them in the same ratio (Lemma 6); and whether the whole moves or is at rest, “provided that each part retains its motion and communicates it to the others as before” (Lemma 7). What makes a body the same body over time is not the persistence of its matter, which is continually replaced, but the persistence of the ratio by which its parts are united.

The doctrine has a famous application in Part IV. There Spinoza defines the good and the bad for the human body by the same ratio: “Those things which bring it about that the ratio of motion and rest which the parts of the human body have to one another is preserved are good; and on the contrary, those are evil which bring it about that the parts of the human body have a different ratio of motion and rest to one another” (Part IV, Prop. 39). The scholium draws a startling consequence. A body dies when its parts take on a different ratio of motion and rest, and this can happen while the blood still circulates. “For no reason compels me to maintain that the body does not die unless it is changed into a corpse.” Spinoza tells of a Spanish poet who, after an illness, so forgot his past life that he did not believe the plays he had written were his own, and asks what we should say of infants, whose nature an old man can hardly believe was ever his (Part IV, Prop. 39, Schol.). The identity of a human being is the identity of a pattern, and patterns can be lost before matter falls apart.

The whole of nature as one individual

The scholium to Lemma 7 builds a hierarchy on these foundations. An individual composed of the simplest bodies can be affected in many ways and keep its nature. An individual composed of individuals of different natures can be affected in many more ways, since each of its parts can move more slowly or more quickly without change of its nature. A third kind of individual, composed of these, can be affected in still more ways; “and if we proceed in this way to infinity, we shall easily conceive that the whole of nature is one individual, whose parts, that is, all bodies, vary in infinitely many ways, without any change of the whole individual” (Lemma 7, Schol.).

The same picture appears in a letter to Oldenburg of 1665 (Letter 32). A worm living in the blood would take each particle of blood for a whole and could not know how all the parts are governed by the nature of the blood as a whole; we are in the same position with respect to the universe, and “every body, insofar as it exists modified in a certain way, must be considered as a part of the whole universe” (VVL³ III 120–121). Letter 64 names, as the mediate infinite mode of extension, “the face of the whole universe, which, although it varies in infinite ways, nevertheless remains always the same” (VVL³ III 206), and refers the reader to this very scholium. The end of the scholium to Lemma 7 thus connects the physics of Part II with the metaphysics of Part I: the whole of nature, considered under the attribute of extension, is one individual whose form is preserved through all the changes of its parts, and this individual is the mediate infinite mode of extension.

The human body

The postulates bring the account down to the human body. “The human body is composed of very many individuals (of different natures), each of which is highly composite” (Postulate 1). Some of these are fluid, some soft and some hard (Postulate 2). The human body is affected by external bodies in very many ways (Postulate 3); it “needs, in order to be preserved, very many other bodies, by which it is continually, as it were, regenerated” (Postulate 4); and it “can move external bodies in very many ways, and arrange them in very many ways” (Postulate 6).

Each of these postulates has work to do. The complexity of the human body explains why its mind is capable of perceiving many things at once, and why it can form ideas of what many bodies have in common. The need for other bodies is the physical root of the doctrine of Part IV that nothing is more useful to a human being than other human beings. And the fifth postulate prepares the theory of memory and imagination: “When a fluid part of the human body is determined by an external body so that it frequently strikes another, soft part, it changes the surface of that part and impresses on it, as it were, certain traces of the external body that drives it” (Postulate 5). These traces explain how the mind can imagine as present things that are absent (Prop. 17), and how one image recalls another with which it was once joined (Prop. 18). Spinoza’s physiology was that of his time, and the details do not matter. What matters is the principle: memory and imagination are the mind’s awareness of changes in its body, and they follow the order of those changes, not the order of the intellect.

What the digression makes possible

On these few premises Props. 14–31 build an account of sense perception, imagination, memory and the association of ideas, and of why the human mind’s knowledge of its own body, of external bodies and of itself is, in the common order of nature, inadequate. But the digression also contains the seed of the remedy. Lemma 2 says that all bodies agree in certain things, and these common properties, which are “equally in the part and in the whole,” can only be conceived adequately (Prop. 38). The same holds of what is common and proper to the human body and to the external bodies by which it is usually affected (Prop. 39). These are the common notions on which reason is built. The physics of the digression thus supplies both the explanation of our confusion and the foundation of our knowledge.

Later Parts return to the digression more than once. Part III restates two of its postulates in its own: the human body can be affected in many ways by which its power of acting is increased or diminished, a postulate which, Spinoza says, “rests on Post. 1 and Lemmas 5 and 7” (Part III, Post. 1); and it can undergo many changes and still retain the traces of objects (Part III, Post. 2). Part IV defines good and evil for the body by the ratio of motion and rest, as we have seen. Part V’s account of the eternity of the mind ends with the thesis that “he who has a body capable of a great many things has a mind whose greatest part is eternal” (Part V, Prop. 39), which returns to the principle stated in the scholium to Prop. 13: minds differ as their bodies differ in what they can do and suffer.

How to read it

The digression should be read as a sketch. Spinoza says so himself: if it had been his intention to treat of body expressly, he would have had to explain and demonstrate these matters more fully, but he brings them forward only because he can easily deduce from them what he has decided to demonstrate (Lemma 7, Schol.). The reader need not decide whether the physics is correct, and should not expect from it the precision of Descartes’ or Huygens’ laws of collision. Its function is to supply a notion of the individual that does not depend on substance, a principle of persistence, and a theory of bodily traces, and these the later Parts use.

The Dutch version of 1677 preserves a slightly fuller form of these pages. In the scholium to Lemma 7 Spinoza says that he would have had to explain and demonstrate these matters more fully had it been his intention to treat expressly “of matter, or” of body, “and in particular”; the Latin drops the words. In the demonstration of Lemma 6 the Dutch adds a cross-reference to the definition before Lemma 4, which the Latin gives instead in the demonstration of Lemma 7, where the Dutch has “this also is evident.” Gebhardt takes these to be the traces of Spinoza’s later revision.

When the text resumes, the axioms, lemmas and postulates follow one another with little comment, and a first reading need not master them all. The propositions that follow draw above all on the postulates about the human body and on the first axiom after Lemma 3: all the ways in which a body is affected by another follow from the nature of the affected body and at the same time from the nature of the affecting body. The account of composite individuals returns later. Part III restates two of the postulates in its own (Part III, Post. 1), and in Part IV the preservation of the ratio of motion and rest among the parts of the human body becomes the measure of what is good or evil for it (Part IV, Prop. 39).

Proposition XV

Propositio XVII

Proposition XVII

Proposition XVIII

[G II 106] Si corpus humanum a duobus, vel pluribus corporibus simul affectum fuerit semel, ubi mens postea eorum aliquod imaginabitur, statim et aliorum recordabitur.

Demonstratio. Mens (per coroll. praeced.) corpus aliquod ea de causa imaginatur, quia scilicet humanum corpus a corporis externi vestigiis eodem modo afficitur disponiturque, ac affectum est, cum quaedam eius partes ab ipso corpore externo fuerunt impulsae. Sed (per hypothesin) corpus tum ita fuit dispositum, ut mens duo simul corpora imaginaretur. Ergo iam etiam duo simul imaginabitur, atque mens ubi alterutrum imaginabitur, statim et alterius recordabitur. Q. E. D.
Demonstration. The mind (by the preceding Cor.) imagines some body for this reason, that the human body is affected and disposed by the traces of an external body in the same way as it was affected when certain of its parts were driven by the external body itself. But (by hypothesis) the body was then so disposed that the mind imagined two bodies at once. Therefore it will now also imagine two at once, and when the mind imagines either of them, it will immediately recollect the other too. Q.E.D.
Scholium. Hinc clare intelligimus, quid sit memoria. Est enim nihil aliud, quam quaedam concatenatio idearum, naturam rerum, quae extra corpus humanum sunt, involventium, quae in mente fit secundum ordinem et concatenationem affectionum corporis humani.
Scholium. From this we understand clearly what memory is. For it is nothing other than a certain concatenation of ideas involving the nature of things which are outside the human body, a concatenation which takes place in the mind according to the order and concatenation of the affections of the human body.
Dico primo concatenationem esse illarum tantum idearum, quae naturam rerum, quae extra corpus humanum sunt, involvunt; non autem idearum, quae earundem rerum naturam explicant. Sunt enim revera (per prop. 16. huius) ideae affectionum corporis humani, quae tam huius, quam corporum externorum naturam involvunt. I say, first, that it is a concatenation only of those ideas which involve the nature of things which are outside the human body, not of ideas which explain the nature of those same things. For they are in fact (by Prop. 16 of this Part) ideas of affections of the human body, which involve the nature both of this body and of the external bodies. I say, second, that this concatenation takes place according to the order and concatenation of the affections of the human body, in order to distinguish it from the concatenation of ideas which takes place according to the order of the intellect, by which the mind perceives things through their first causes, and which is the same in all men.1

Proposition XIX

Deus itaque ideam corporis humani habet, sive corpus humanum cognoscit, quatenus plurimis aliis ideis affectus est, et non quatenus naturam humanae mentis constituit, hoc est (per coroll. prop. 11. huius) mens humana corpus humanum non cognoscit.
Demonstration. For the human mind is the very idea, or knowledge, of the human body (by Prop. 13 of this Part), which (by Prop. 9 of this Part) is indeed in God insofar as he is considered to be affected by another idea of a singular thing; or, since (by Post. 4) the human body needs very many bodies by which it is continually, as it were, regenerated, and the order and connection of ideas is the same (by Prop. 7 of this Part) as the order and connection of causes, this idea will be in God insofar as he is considered to be affected by the ideas of very many singular things. God, therefore, has the idea of the human body, or knows the human body, insofar as he is affected by very many other ideas, and not insofar as he constitutes the nature of the human mind; that is (by Cor. of Prop. 11 of this Part), the human mind does not know the human body.
At ideae affectionum corporis in Deo sunt, quatenus humanae mentis naturam constituit, sive mens humana easdem affectiones percipit (per prop. 12. huius), et consequenter (per prop. 16. huius) ipsum corpus humanum, idque (per prop. 17. huius) ut actu existens. Percipit ergo eatenus tantum mens humana ipsum humanum corpus. Q. E. D.
But the ideas of the affections of the body are in God insofar as he constitutes the nature of the human mind, or the human mind perceives those affections (by Prop. 12 of this Part), and consequently (by Prop. 16 of this Part) the human body itself, and (by Prop. 17 of this Part) as actually existing. Only to that extent, therefore, does the human mind perceive the human body itself. Q.E.D.

Propositio XX

Proposition XX

Mentis humanae datur etiam in Deo idea, sive cognitio, quae in Deo eodem modo sequitur et ad Deum eodem modo refertur, ac idea sive cognitio corporis humani.
There is also in God an idea, or knowledge, of the human mind, which follows in God in the same way, and is related to God in the same way, as the idea, or knowledge, of the human body.

Cited in 2P22D, 2P23D, 2P43D

Demonstratio. Cogitatio attributum Dei est (per prop. 1. huius); adeoque (per prop. 3. huius) tam eius, quam omnium eius affectionum, et consequenter (per prop. 11. huius) mentis etiam humanae debet necessario in Deo dari idea. Deinde haec mentis idea sive cognitio non sequitur in Deo dari, quatenus infinitus; sed quatenus alia rei singularis idea affectus est (per prop. 9. huius). Sed ordo et connexio idearum idem est, ac ordo et connexio causarum (per prop. 7. huius). Sequitur ergo haec mentis idea, sive cognitio in Deo, et ad Deum eodem modo refertur, ac idea sive cognitio corporis. Q. E. D.
Demonstration. Thought is an attribute of God (by Prop. 1 of this Part); and so (by Prop. 3 of this Part) there must necessarily be in God an idea both of thought and of all its affections, and consequently (by Prop. 11 of this Part) of the human mind too. Next, this idea, or knowledge, of the mind does not follow to be in God insofar as he is infinite, but insofar as he is affected by another idea of a singular thing (by Prop. 9 of this Part). But the order and connection of ideas is the same as the order and connection of causes (by Prop. 7 of this Part). This idea, or knowledge, of the mind therefore follows in God, and is related to God, in the same way as the idea, or knowledge, of the body. Q.E.D.

Propositio XXI

Proposition XXI

[G II 109] Haec mentis idea eodem modo unita est menti, ac ipsa mens unita est corpori.
This idea of the mind is united to the mind in the same way as the mind itself is united to the body.

Cited in 2P22D, 4P8D, 5P3D

Demonstratio. Mentem unitam esse corpori ex eo ostendimus, quod scilicet corpus mentis sit obiectum (vide prop. 12. et 13. huius); adeoque per eandem illam rationem idea mentis cum suo obiecto, hoc est, cum ipsa mente eodem modo unita esse debet, ac ipsa mens unita est corpori. Q. E. D.
Demonstration. We have shown that the mind is united to the body from the fact that the body is the object of the mind (see Props. 12 and 13 of this Part); and so, by that same reasoning, the idea of the mind must be united with its object, that is, with the mind itself, in the same way as the mind itself is united to the body. Q.E.D.
Scholium. Haec prop. longe clarius intelligitur ex dictis in schol. prop. 7. huius. Ibi enim ostendimus corporis ideam, et corpus, hoc est (per prop. 13. huius) mentem et corpus unum et idem esse individuum, quod iam sub cogitationis, iam sub extensionis attributo concipitur. Quare mentis idea et ipsa mens una eademque est res, quae sub uno eodemque attributo, nempe cogitationis, concipitur. Mentis, inquam, idea et ipsa mens in Deo eadem necessitate ex eadem cogitandi potentia sequuntur dari.
Scholium. This proposition is understood far more clearly from what was said in Schol. of Prop. 7 of this Part. For there we showed that the idea of the body and the body, that is (by Prop. 13 of this Part), the mind and the body, are one and the same individual, which is conceived now under the attribute of thought, now under the attribute of extension. So the idea of the mind and the mind itself are one and the same thing, which is conceived under one and the same attribute, namely thought. The idea of the mind, I say, and the mind itself follow to be in God from the same power of thinking by the same necessity.
Nam revera idea mentis, hoc est, idea ideae nihil aliud est, quam forma ideae, quatenus haec ut modus cogitandi absque relatione ad obiectum consideratur. Simulac enim quis aliquid scit, eo ipso scit se id scire, et simul scit se scire quod scit, et sic in infinitum. Sed de his postea.
For in fact the idea of the mind, that is, the idea of the idea, is nothing other than the form of the idea1 insofar as this is considered as a mode of thinking without relation to its object. For as soon as someone knows something, by that very fact he knows that he knows it, and at the same time he knows that he knows that he knows, and so on to infinity. But of these matters later.

Propositio XXII

Proposition XXII

Mens humana non tantum corporis affectiones, sed etiam harum affectionum ideas percipit.
The human mind perceives not only the affections of the body, but also the ideas of these affections.

Cited in 2P23D, 2P47D, 4P8D

Demonstratio. Affectionum idearum ideae in Deo eodem modo sequuntur et ad Deum eodem modo referuntur, ac ipsae affectionum ideae; quod eodem modo demonstratur, ac prop. 20. huius. At ideae affectionum corporis in mente humana sunt (per prop. 12. huius), hoc est (per coroll. prop. 11. huius) in Deo, quatenus humanae mentis essentiam constituit. Ergo harum idearum ideae in Deo erunt, quatenus humanae mentis cognitionem, sive ideam habet, hoc est (per prop. 21. huius) in ipsa mente humana, quae propterea non tantum corporis affectiones, sed earum etiam ideas percipit. Q. E. D.
Demonstration. The ideas of the ideas of the affections follow in God in the same way, and are related to God in the same way, as the ideas of the affections themselves; which is demonstrated in the same way as Prop. 20 of this Part. But the ideas of the affections of the body are in the human mind (by Prop. 12 of this Part), that is (by Cor. of Prop. 11 of this Part), in God insofar as he constitutes the essence of the human mind. Therefore the ideas of these ideas will be in God insofar as he has the knowledge, or idea, of the human mind, that is (by Prop. 21 of this Part), in the human mind itself, which therefore perceives not only the affections of the body, but also their ideas. Q.E.D.

Propositio XXIII

Proposition XXIII

[G II 110] Mens se ipsam non cognoscit, nisi quatenus corporis affectionum ideas percipit.
The mind does not know itself except insofar as it perceives the ideas of the affections of the body.1
  1. 1 A reversal of Descartes, whose Second Meditation is titled ‘De natura mentis humanae: quod ipsa sit notior quam corpus’ (‘Of the nature of the human mind: that it is better known than the body’; AT VII 23). For Spinoza the mind knows itself only through the ideas of its body’s affections, and so no better than it knows the body (Prop. 19).

Cited in 2P29C, 2P47D, 3P9D, 3P30D, 3P53D, 3DA1Ex

Demonstratio. Mentis idea sive cognitio (per prop. 20. huius) in Deo eodem modo sequitur et ad Deum eodem modo refertur, ac corporis idea sive cognitio. At quoniam (per prop. 19. huius) mens humana ipsum humanum corpus non cognoscit, hoc est (per coroll. prop. 11. huius) quoniam cognitio corporis humani ad Deum non refertur, quatenus humanae mentis naturam constituit; ergo nec cognitio mentis ad Deum refertur, quatenus essentiam mentis humanae constituit; atque adeo (per idem coroll. prop. 11. huius.) mens humana eatenus se ipsam non cognoscit.
Demonstration. The idea, or knowledge, of the mind (by Prop. 20 of this Part) follows in God in the same way, and is related to God in the same way, as the idea, or knowledge, of the body. But since (by Prop. 19 of this Part) the human mind does not know the human body itself, that is (by Cor. of Prop. 11 of this Part), since the knowledge of the human body is not related to God insofar as he constitutes the nature of the human mind, neither is the knowledge of the mind related to God insofar as he constitutes the essence of the human mind; and so (by the same Cor. of Prop. 11 of this Part) the human mind to that extent does not know itself.
Deinde affectionum, quibus corpus afficitur, ideae naturam ipsius corporis humani involvunt (per prop. 16. huius), hoc est (per prop. 13. huius) cum natura mentis conveniunt. Quare harum idearum cognitio cognitionem mentis necessario involvet. At (per prop. praeced.) harum idearum cognitio in ipsa humana mente est. Ergo mens humana eatenus tantum se ipsam novit. Q. E. D.
Next, the ideas of the affections by which the body is affected involve the nature of the human body itself (by Prop. 16 of this Part), that is (by Prop. 13 of this Part), they agree with the nature of the mind. So the knowledge of these ideas will necessarily involve the knowledge of the mind. But (by the preceding Prop.) the knowledge of these ideas is in the human mind itself. Therefore the human mind knows itself only to that extent. Q.E.D.

Propositio XXIV

Proposition XXIV

Mens humana partium corpus humanum componentium adaequatam cognitionem non involvit.
The human mind does not involve adequate knowledge of the parts composing the human body.

Cited in 2P28D, 2P36D

Demonstratio. Partes corpus humanum componentes ad essentiam ipsius corporis non pertinent, nisi quatenus motus suos certa quadam ratione invicem communicant (vide defin. post coroll. lem. 3.), et non quatenus ut individua absque relatione ad humanum corpus considerari possunt. Sunt enim partes humani corporis (per postul. 1.) valde composita individua, quorum partes (per lem. 4.) a corpore humano servata omnino eiusdem natura et forma segregari possunt, motusque suos (vide axiom. 1. post lem. 3.) aliis corporibus alia ratione communicare.
Demonstration. The parts composing the human body do not pertain to the essence of the body itself except insofar as they communicate their motions to one another in a certain fixed ratio (see the Def. after the Cor. of Lemma 3), and not insofar as they can be considered as individuals without relation to the human body. For the parts of the human body (by Post. 1) are highly composite individuals, whose parts (by Lemma 4) can be separated from the human body while it entirely keeps its nature and form, and can communicate their motions (see Ax. 1 after Lemma 3) to other bodies in another ratio.
Adeoque (per prop. 3. huius) cuiuscumque partis idea sive cognitio in Deo erit, et quidem (per prop. 9. huius) quatenus affectus consideratur alia idea rei singularis, quae res singularis ipsa parte ordine naturae prior est (per prop. 7. huius). Quod idem praeterea etiam de quacumque parte ipsius individui corpus humanum componentis est dicendum.
And so (by Prop. 3 of this Part) the idea, or knowledge, of each part will be in God, and indeed (by Prop. 9 of this Part) insofar as he is considered to be affected by another idea of a singular thing, which singular thing is prior in the order of nature to the part itself (by Prop. 7 of this Part). The same, moreover, is to be said of any part of the individual itself composing the human body.
Adeoque cuiuscumque partis corpus humanum componentis cognitio in Deo est, quatenus plurimis rerum ideis affectus est, et non quatenus corporis humani tantum habet ideam, hoc est (per prop. 13. huius) ideam, quae humanae mentis naturam constituit. Atque adeo (per coroll. prop. 11. huius) humana mens partium corpus humanum componentium adaequatam cognitionem non involvit. Q. E. D.
And so the knowledge of each part composing the human body is in God insofar as he is affected by very many ideas of things, and not insofar as he has only the idea of the human body, that is (by Prop. 13 of this Part), the idea which constitutes the nature of the human mind. And therefore (by Cor. of Prop. 11 of this Part) the human mind does not involve adequate knowledge of the parts composing the human body. Q.E.D.

Propositio XXV

Proposition XXV

[G II 111] Idea cuiuscumque affectionis corporis humani adaequatam corporis externi cognitionem non involvit.
The idea of any affection of the human body does not involve adequate knowledge of the external body.

Cited in 2P26CD, 2P27D, 2P28D, 2P29C, 2P38D

Demonstratio. Ideam affectionis corporis humani eatenus corporis externi naturam involvere ostendimus (vide prop.16. huius), quatenus externum ipsum humanum corpus certo quodam modo determinat. At quatenus externum corpus individuum est, quod ad corpus humanum non refertur, eius idea sive cognitio in Deo est (per prop. 9. huius), quatenus Deus affectus consideratur alterius rei idea, quae (per prop. 7. huius) ipso corpore externo prior est natura. Quare corporis externi adaequata cognitio in Deo non est, quatenus ideam affectionis humani corporis habet, sive idea affectionis corporis humani adaequatam corporis externi cognitionem non involvit. Q. E. D.
Demonstration. We have shown that the idea of an affection of the human body involves the nature of an external body insofar as the external body determines the human body itself in a certain way (see Prop. 16 of this Part). But insofar as the external body is an individual that is not related to the human body, its idea, or knowledge, is in God (by Prop. 9 of this Part) insofar as God is considered to be affected by the idea of another thing, which (by Prop. 7 of this Part) is prior in nature to the external body itself. So adequate knowledge of the external body is not in God insofar as he has the idea of the affection of the human body; or, the idea of an affection of the human body does not involve adequate knowledge of the external body. Q.E.D.

Propositio XXVI

Proposition XXVI

[G II 112] Mens humana nullum corpus externum ut actu existens percipit, nisi per ideas affectionum sui corporis.
The human mind perceives no external body as actually existing except through the ideas of the affections of its own body.

Cited in 2P26CD, 2P29C, 5P21D, 5P29D

Demonstratio. Si a corpore aliquo externo corpus humanum nullo modo affectum est, ergo (per prop. 7. huius) nec idea corporis humani, hoc est (per prop. 13. huius) nec mens humana idea existentiae illius corporis ullo etiam modo affecta est, sive existentiam illius corporis externi ullo modo percipit. At quatenus corpus humanum a corpore aliquo externo aliquo modo afficitur, eatenus (per prop. 16. huius cum coroll. 1. eiusdem) corpus externum percipit. Q. E. D.
Demonstration. If the human body is in no way affected by some external body, then (by Prop. 7 of this Part) neither is the idea of the human body, that is (by Prop. 13 of this Part), neither is the human mind, affected in any way by the idea of the existence of that body; or, it does not perceive the existence of that external body in any way. But insofar as the human body is affected in some way by some external body, to that extent (by Prop. 16 of this Part with its Cor. 1) the mind perceives the external body. Q.E.D.
Corollarium. Quatenus mens humana corpus externum imaginatur, eatenus adaequatam eius cognitionem non habet.
Corollary. Insofar as the human mind imagines an external body, it does not have adequate knowledge of it.
Demonstratio. Cum mens humana per ideas affectionum sui corporis corpora externa contemplatur, eandem tum imaginari dicimus (vide schol. prop. 17. huius); nec mens alia ratione (per prop. praeced.) corpora externa ut actu existentia imaginari potest. Atque adeo (per prop. 25. huius) quatenus mens corpora externa imaginatur, eorum adaequatam cognitionem non habet. Q. E. D.
Demonstration. When the human mind contemplates external bodies through the ideas of the affections of its own body, we then say that it imagines (see Schol. of Prop. 17 of this Part); nor can the mind (by the preceding Prop.) imagine external bodies as actually existing in any other way. And so (by Prop. 25 of this Part), insofar as the mind imagines external bodies, it does not have adequate knowledge of them. Q.E.D.

Propositio XXVII

Proposition XXVII

Idea cuiuscumque affectionis corporis humani adaequatam ipsius humani corporis cognitionem non involvit.
The idea of any affection of the human body does not involve adequate knowledge of the human body itself.

Cited in 2P29D, 2P29C, 2P38D

Demonstratio. Quaelibet idea cuiuscumque affectionis humani corporis eatenus naturam corporis humani involvit, quatenus ipsum humanum corpus certo quodam modo affici consideratur (vide prop. 16. huius). At quatenus corpus humanum individuum est, quod multis aliis modis affici potest, eius idea etc. Vide demonstr. prop. 25. huius.
Demonstration. Any idea of any affection of the human body involves the nature of the human body to the extent that the human body itself is considered to be affected in a certain way (see Prop. 16 of this Part). But insofar as the human body is an individual that can be affected in many other ways, its idea etc. See the demonstration of Prop. 25 of this Part.

Propositio XXVIII

Proposition XXVIII

[G II 113] Ideae affectionum corporis humani, quatenus ad humanam mentem tantum referuntur, non sunt clarae et distinctae, sed confusae.
The ideas of the affections of the human body, insofar as they are related only to the human mind, are not clear and distinct, but confused.

Cited in 2P29C, 2P36D

Demonstratio. Ideae enim affectionum corporis humani, tam corporum externorum, quam ipsius humani corporis naturam involvunt (per prop. 16. huius); nec tantum corporis humani, sed eius etiam partium naturam involvere debent. Affectiones namque modi sunt (per postul. 3.), quibus partes corporis humani, et consequenter totum corpus afficitur.
Demonstration. For the ideas of the affections of the human body involve the nature both of external bodies and of the human body itself (by Prop. 16 of this Part); and they must involve the nature not only of the human body but also of its parts. For the affections are modes (by Post. 3) in which the parts of the human body, and consequently the whole body, are affected.
At (per prop. 24. et 25. huius) corporum externorum adaequata cognitio, ut et partium corpus humanum componentium in Deo non est, quatenus humana mente, sed quatenus aliis ideis affectus consideratur.a Sunt ergo hae affectionum ideae, quatenus ad solam humanam mentem referuntur, veluti consequentiae absque praemissis, hoc est (ut per se notum) ideae confusae. Q. E. D.
But (by Props. 24 and 25 of this Part) adequate knowledge of external bodies, as also of the parts composing the human body, is not in God insofar as he is considered to be affected by the human mind, but insofar as he is considered to be affected by other ideas. These ideas of affections, therefore, insofar as they are related only to the human mind, are like consequences without premises,1 that is (as is self-evident), confused ideas. Q.E.D.

Propositio XXIX

Proposition XXIX

Idea ideae cuiuscumque affectionis corporis humani adaequatam humanae mentis cognitionem non involvit.
The idea of the idea of any affection of the human body does not involve adequate knowledge of the human mind.

Propositio XXX

Proposition XXX

Nos de duratione nostri corporis nullam nisi admodum inadaequatam cognitionem habere possumus.
We can have only a very inadequate knowledge of the duration of our body.

Cited in 2P31D

Demonstratio. Nostri corporis duratio ab eius essentia non dependet (per axiom. 1. huius), nec etiam ab absoluta Dei natura (per prop. 21. P. 1.). Sed (per prop. 28. P. 1.) ad existendum et operandum determinatur a talibus causis,a quae etiam ab aliis determinatae sunt ad existendum et operandum certa ac determinata ratione, et hae iterum ab aliis, et sic in infinitum. Nostri igitur corporis duratio a communi naturae ordine et rerum constitutione pendet.
Demonstration. The duration of our body does not depend on its essence (by Ax. 1 of this Part), nor on the absolute nature of God either (by Prop. 21, Part I). But (by Prop. 28, Part I) it is determined to exist and to operate by such causes as are also determined by others to exist and to operate in a certain and determinate manner, and these again by others, and so on to infinity. The duration of our body therefore depends on the common order of nature and on the constitution of things.

Proposition XXXI

Propositio XXXIII

Proposition XXXIII

Propositio XXXIV

Proposition XXXIV

Propositio XXXV

Proposition XXXV

Falsitas consistit in cognitionis privatione, quam ideae inadaequatae sive mutilatae et confusae involvunt.

Q. E. D.

For when they say that human actions depend on the will, these are words of which they have no idea. For what the will is, and how it moves the body, they are all ignorant; those who boast otherwise, and feign seats and dwelling places of the soul,2 usually provoke either laughter or disgust.

Nam tametsi postea cognoscamus, eundem ultra sescentos terrae diametros a nobis distare, ipsum nihilominus prope adesse imaginabimur; non enim solem adeo propinquum imaginamur, propterea quod veram eius distantiam ignoramus, sed propterea, quod affectio nostri corporis essentiam solis involvit, quatenus ipsum corpus ab eodem afficitur.

Propositio XXXVI

Proposition XXXVI

Ideae inadaequatae et confusae eadem necessitate consequuntur, ac adaequatae sive clarae ac distinctae ideae.
Inadequate and confused ideas follow with the same necessity as adequate, or clear and distinct, ideas.
[G II 118] Demonstratio. Ideae omnes in Deo sunt (per prop. 15. P. 1.) et quatenus ad Deum referuntur, sunt verae (per prop. 32. huius) et (per coroll. prop. 7. huius) adaequatae; adeoque nullae inadaequatae nec confusae sunt, nisi quatenus ad singularem alicuius mentem referuntur. Demonstration. All ideas are in God (by Prop. 15, Part I), and insofar as they are related to God they are true (by Prop. 32 of this Part) and (by Cor. of Prop. 7 of this Part) adequate; and so there are no inadequate or confused ideas except insofar as they are related to the singular mind of someone. Propositio XXXVIII

Proposition XXXVIII

Illa quae omnibus communia, quaeque aeque in parte ac in toto sunt, non possunt concipi nisi adaequate.

Spinoza had used the term for axioms in the old sense (see the note on Part I, Prop. 8, Schol. 2); here they become ideas of what is common to all bodies, or to the human body and those that affect it (Prop. 39), and are adequate in every mind because nothing in them depends on what the mind lacks.

Nam eius idea (per coroll. prop. 7. huius) erit necessario in Deo adaequata, tam quatenus ideam corporis humani, quam quatenus ideas habet eiusdem affectionum, quae (per prop. 16., 25. et 27. huius) tam corporis humani, quam corporum externorum naturam ex parte involvunt, hoc est (per prop. 12. et 13. huius) haec idea erit necessario in Deo adaequata, quatenus mentem humanam constituit, sive quatenus ideas habet, quae in mente humana sunt. Demonstration. Let A be something that is common to all bodies, and that is equally in the part of each body and in the whole. I say that A can only be conceived adequately. For its idea (by Cor. of Prop. 7 of this Part) will necessarily be adequate in God, both insofar as he has the idea of the human body and insofar as he has the ideas of its affections, which (by Props. 16, 25 and 27 of this Part) involve in part the nature both of the human body and of external bodies; that is (by Props. 12 and 13 of this Part), this idea will necessarily be adequate in God insofar as he constitutes the human mind, or insofar as he has the ideas which are in the human mind. The mind therefore (by Cor. of Prop. 11 of this Part) necessarily perceives A adequately, and that both insofar as it perceives itself and insofar as it perceives its own or any external body; nor can A be conceived in any other way. Q.E.D.

Propositio XL

Proposition XL

[G II 120] Quaecumque ideae in mente sequuntur ex ideis, quae in ipsa sunt adaequatae, sunt etiam adaequatae.
Whatever ideas follow in the mind from ideas which are adequate in it are also adequate.

Proposition XLI

Cognitio primi generis unica est falsitatis causa, secundi autem et tertii est necessario vera.
Knowledge of the first kind is the only cause of falsity, but knowledge of the second and third kinds is necessarily true.

Ad primi generis cognitionem illas omnes ideas diximus in praeced. schol. pertinere, quae sunt inadaequatae, et confusae; atque adeo (per prop. 35. huius) haec cognitio unica est falsitatis causa. Proposition XLII

Demonstratio. Haec prop. per se patet. Qui enim inter verum, et falsum scit distinguere, debet adaequatam veri, et falsi habere ideam, hoc est (per schol. 2. prop. 40. huius) verum, et falsum secundo, aut tertio cognitionis genere cognoscere.
Demonstration. This Proposition is evident through itself. Will and Intellect

Part II ends with two propositions about the will and the longest scholium of the Part. They draw the consequence of everything said about ideas. They are also polemical, directed at a doctrine of the will which Spinoza expects his reader to know and does not set out, and they are easier to follow with that doctrine in view. Prop. 48 denies that there is in the mind any absolute or free will: the mind is a determinate mode of thinking, and so (Part I, Prop. 17, Cor. 2; Prop. 28) each volition is determined by a cause, which is determined by another, and so to infinity. Prop. 49 says what a volition is: nothing but the affirmation or negation that an idea, insofar as it is an idea, involves. Its corollary is the thesis the whole scholium defends: “will and intellect are one and the same.”

Descartes’ theory of judgment

The opponent is Descartes, though Spinoza does not name him here. In the fourth Meditation Descartes explains error by the relation of two faculties. The intellect is finite; the will is not. “Sola est voluntas, sive arbitrii libertas, quam tantam in me experior, ut nullius maioris ideam apprehendam” (“It is only the will, or freedom of choice, that I experience in myself as so great that I apprehend the idea of none greater”), and it is chiefly by the will that I bear the image and likeness of God (AT VII 57). Error comes from this disproportion: “cum latius pateat voluntas quam intellectus, illam non intra eosdem limites contineo, sed etiam ad illa quae non intelligo extendo” (“since the will extends more widely than the intellect, I do not keep it within the same limits, but extend it also to things I do not understand”, AT VII 58). The Principles make the doctrine systematic. There are only two modes of thinking, the perception of the intellect and the operation of the will, and affirming and denying belong to the second (I.32); judgment needs the will as well as the intellect, for the intellect only presents and the will assents (I.34); the will “can be called in a certain way infinite” (I.35); and its freedom is so evident that it must be counted among the first and most common notions innate in us, as we found when, even supposing an all-powerful deceiver, we could still refrain from believing what was not certain (I.39; AT VIII-1 17–20). The remedy for error follows: withhold assent wherever perception is not clear and distinct.

The theory did more than explain error. It allowed Descartes to hold that God, who gave us both faculties, is not the cause of our mistakes. The intellect is finite but contains no falsity; the will is as perfect as a will can be; error arises only from our use of the will, when we affirm or deny what we do not clearly perceive, and then “libertate arbitrii non recte utor” (“I do not use my freedom of choice rightly”), and am at fault even if I happen to hit on the truth (AT VII 59–60). The responsibility for error, and with it the possibility of avoiding error, rests on the freedom of the will. Spinoza’s denial of that freedom therefore had to supply another account both of what error is and of how it is overcome.

Spinoza’s reply

Spinoza’s reply has two parts. The first is that there is no faculty of willing over and above singular volitions. Faculties of understanding, desiring, loving and willing are either wholly fictitious or “metaphysical beings, or universals, which we are accustomed to form from particulars”, so that the will is related to this or that volition as stoneness to this or that stone, or man to Peter and Paul (Prop. 48, Schol.). He had put it the same way to Oldenburg years before: “voluntas differt ab hac et illa volitione, eodem modo ac albedo ab hoc et illo albo, sive humanitas ab hoc et illo homine” (“the will differs from this or that volition in the same way as whiteness from this or that white thing, or humanity from this or that man”), and it is as impossible to conceive the will as the cause of a volition as humanity as the cause of Peter and Paul (Letter 2; VVL³ III 6). The second part is that a singular volition is not something added to an idea. The demonstration of Prop. 49 takes the affirmation that the three angles of a triangle are equal to two right angles. It cannot be or be conceived without the idea of the triangle, and the idea of the triangle cannot be or be conceived without it; by the definition of essence (Def. 2) the affirmation belongs to the essence of the idea and is nothing besides it. The idea is not “something mute, like a picture on a panel” (Prop. 43, Schol.) waiting for the will to say yes to it. It is itself an act of affirming. The Cartesian division of judgment between a presenting intellect and an assenting will disappears.

Images, words and ideas

Before answering objections, the scholium to Prop. 49 asks the reader to make two distinctions, which Spinoza thinks most people neglect: between an idea and the images of things that we imagine, and between ideas and the words by which we signify things. “Because these three, namely images, words and ideas, are by many either altogether confused, or not distinguished accurately enough, or finally not cautiously enough, they have been altogether ignorant of this doctrine of the will, which it is absolutely necessary to know, both for speculation and for arranging life wisely” (Prop. 49, Schol.).

Each confusion produces a characteristic mistake. Those who think that ideas consist in images formed in us by the encounter of bodies conclude that where we can form no image there is no idea, only a fiction made by the free choice of the will; “they look on ideas, therefore, as mute pictures on a panel,” and do not see that an idea, insofar as it is an idea, involves affirmation or negation. Those who confuse words with ideas think they can will contrary to what they perceive, because by words alone they can affirm or deny the contrary. But to affirm something in words is not to affirm it in thought. Words and images are bodily motions, and “do not at all involve the concept of thought”; an idea, which is a mode of thinking, “consists neither in the image of some thing nor in words.” The appearance of a will that can assent or refuse at pleasure comes, on this account, from treating the verbal expression of a judgment, or the image that accompanies it, as if it were the judgment itself.

Two senses of “will”

“Will” in this sense means the faculty of affirming and denying, “not desire”, as the scholium to Prop. 48 is careful to say. In Part III, Prop. 9, Schol. the word returns with another sense: the striving by which each thing perseveres in its being is called will “when it is related to the mind alone”, and appetite when it is related to mind and body together. The two uses do not conflict, but the reader should keep them apart.

The four objections

The scholium to Prop. 49 then answers four objections. That the will extends further than the intellect is true, Spinoza replies, if “intellect” means only clear and distinct ideas; it does not extend further than our perceptions, and the “infinity” of the will is only the universality of an abstract idea, which is said equally of one, of many and of infinitely many individuals. That we can suspend judgment is denied: to suspend judgment is to perceive that one does not perceive the thing adequately, so that suspension is itself a perception. A boy who imagined a winged horse and perceived nothing else would contemplate the horse as present and could not doubt its existence; to perceive a winged horse is to affirm wings of a horse. That all affirmations seem equal in reality, while ideas differ in perfection, confuses the affirmation abstractly conceived, which is the same in all ideas as a universal is the same in its instances, with singular affirmations, which differ as their ideas differ; and a true and a false affirmation are related as being to not-being. The third reply ends with the warning that we are easily deceived “when we confuse universals with singulars, and beings of reason and abstractions with real beings.”

Buridan’s ass

The fourth objection is the ass of Buridan. If man does not act from freedom of the will, what will happen when he is in equilibrium, like Buridan’s ass? Will he perish of hunger and thirst? The dilemma is older than the name attached to it. Aristotle, discussing why the earth stays at the centre, mentions the argument about “τοῦ πεινῶντος καὶ διψῶντος σφόδρα μὲν ὁμοίως δὲ καὶ τῶν ἐδωδίμων καὶ ποτῶν ἴσον ἀπέχοντος (καὶ γὰρ τοῦτον ἠρεμεῖν ἀναγκαῖον)” (“the man who is very hungry and thirsty, and equally so, and is equally distant from food and drink, for he too must stay where he is”, De caelo II.13, 295b31–34). Spinoza’s answer is to grant the conclusion: such a man, perceiving nothing but hunger and thirst and food and drink equally distant, will die of hunger and thirst; and whether he should be counted a man or an ass, he does not know, any more than he knows how to count a man who hangs himself, or children, fools and madmen. The answer is the reverse of the one given under Spinoza’s name in 1663, in the Metaphysical Thoughts appended to his exposition of Descartes’ Principles: “si enim hominem loco asinae ponamus in tali aequilibrio positum, homo non pro re cogitante, sed pro turpissimo asino erit habendus, si fame et siti pereat” (“for if we put a man in place of the ass, placed in such an equilibrium, the man would be considered not a thinking thing but the most shameful ass, if he perished of hunger and thirst”, II.12; VVL² III 230). But that book expounded Descartes, and Lodewijk Meyer’s preface had warned the reader that its author did not share the Cartesian view of the will: he thought he could demonstrate “voluntatem ab intellectu non distingui, multo minus ea, quam illi Cartesius adscribit, pollere libertate; quin imo ipsam affirmandi et negandi facultatem prorsus fictitiam” (“that the will is not distinct from the intellect, much less endowed with the freedom Descartes ascribes to it; indeed, that the faculty of affirming and denying is wholly fictitious”, VVL² III 112). Prop. 49 and its scholium are that demonstration.

Error and responsibility without free will

What replaces Descartes’ remedy for error? If we cannot suspend judgment at will, we cannot avoid error by restraining the will; and Spinoza does not think we can. To suspend judgment is itself to perceive that we do not perceive the thing adequately. What we can do is have better ideas. A false idea is not corrected by an act of refusal but by an idea that excludes it, as the boy’s image of a winged horse is undone only when he perceives something that excludes its existence. The improvement of the intellect, which Spinoza had made the subject of an early work, the Treatise on the Emendation of the Intellect, takes the place of the discipline of the will, and the Ethics itself is meant to be such an improvement. The same structure returns in Part V, where “the power of the mind is defined by knowledge alone” (Part V, Prop. 20, Schol.).

A natural objection is that without free will there can be neither praise nor blame, neither merit nor sin, and Spinoza’s correspondents pressed it on him. His answer, in a late letter to Oldenburg, is that men can be excusable and still lack blessedness and suffer in many ways. “A horse is excusable for being a horse and not a man; but nonetheless it must be a horse and not a man. He who goes mad from the bite of a dog is indeed to be excused, and yet he is rightly strangled. And he, finally, who cannot govern his desires and restrain them by fear of the laws, although he too is to be excused on account of his weakness, cannot enjoy peace of mind and the knowledge and love of God, but necessarily perishes” (Letter 78; VVL³ III 237). The denial of free will does not abolish the difference between the wise and the foolish, or between what is good for us and what is bad; it changes the way we regard it. That change of regard is the subject of the last section of the scholium.

The uses of the doctrine

The last section of the scholium turns to the uses of the doctrine. It teaches that we act by God’s decree alone and share in the divine nature, the more so the more perfect our actions and the more we understand God, and that our highest happiness consists in the knowledge of God alone; so that those who expect to be rewarded for virtue, as if for the utmost servitude, are far from a true estimate of it, since virtue and the service of God are themselves happiness and the highest freedom. It teaches us to bear both faces of fortune with an equal mind, since everything follows from God’s eternal decree as necessarily as the properties of a triangle from its essence. It teaches us to hate, despise, mock and envy no one, and to help others from the guidance of reason alone, not from womanish compassion, partiality or superstition. And it teaches how citizens are to be governed: not so that they may be slaves, but so that they may freely do what is best. Each of these is the program of a later Part: the ethics of reason in Part IV, the freedom and blessedness of the mind in Part V, the state in Part IV, Prop. 37, Schol. 2 and, at length, in the unfinished Political Treatise. The cross-reference for the third read “in the third Part” in the 1677 edition; Gebhardt corrects it to the fourth, taking it for a survival from the time when the work was divided into three parts.

The text resumes with Prop. 48 and its scholium on faculties. The scholium to Prop. 49 announces its own plan: first, some points needed for a fuller explanation of the proposition, chiefly the distinction between ideas on the one hand and images and words on the other; then the replies to the objections, taken in order and numbered; and last the advantages of the doctrine, also numbered. With it Part II ends, and Part III begins its account of the affects.

Proposition XLVIII

[G II 129] In mente nulla est absoluta sive libera voluntas, sed mens ad hoc vel illud volendum determinatur a causa, quae etiam ab alia determinata est, et haec iterum ab alia, et sic in infinitum.
In the mind there is no absolute, or free, will; but the mind is determined to will this or that by a cause which is also determined by another, and this again by another, and so to infinity.

Cited in 2P49D, 2P49CD, 3DA6Ex

Demonstratio. Mens certus et determinatus modus cogitandi est (per prop. 11. huius), adeoque (per coroll. 2. prop. 17. P. 1.) suarum actionum non potest esse causa libera, sive absolutam facultatem volendi et nolendi habere non potest; sed ad hoc vel illud volendum (per prop. 28. P. 1.) determinari debet a causa, quae etiam ab alia determinata est, et haec iterum ab alia, etc. Q. E. D.
Demonstration. The mind is a certain and determinate mode of thinking (by Prop. 11 of this Part), and so (by Cor. 2 of Prop. 17 of Part I) it cannot be the free cause of its own actions, or cannot have an absolute faculty of willing and not willing; but it must be determined to will this or that (by Prop. 28 of Part I) by a cause which is also determined by another, and this again by another, etc. Q.E.D.
Scholium. Eodem hoc modo demonstratur in mente nullam dari facultatem absolutam intelligendi, cupiendi, amandi, etc. Unde sequitur, has et similes facultates vel prorsus fictitias, vel nihil esse praeter entia metaphysica sive universalia, quae ex particularibus formare solemus; adeo ut intellectus, et voluntas ad hanc et illam ideam, vel ad hanc et illam volitionem eodem modo sese habeant, ac lapideitas ad hunc et illum lapidem, vel ut homo ad Petrum et Paulum. Causam autem, cur homines se liberos esse putent, explicuimus in app. P. 1.
Scholium. In this same way it is demonstrated that there is in the mind no absolute faculty of understanding, desiring, loving, etc. From this it follows that these and similar faculties are either altogether fictitious, or nothing but metaphysical beings, or universals, which we are accustomed to form from particulars; so that intellect and will are related to this or that idea, or to this or that volition, in the same way as stoneness is related to this or that stone,1 or man to Peter and Paul. The cause why men think themselves free, however, we have explained in the Appendix of Part I.
  1. 1 Universals formed from particulars were explained in Prop. 40, Schol. 1. Spinoza had made the same point to Oldenburg: ‘voluntas differt ab hac et illa volitione, eodem modo ac albedo ab hoc et illo albo, sive humanitas ab hoc et illo homine’ (‘the will differs from this or that volition in the same way as whiteness from this or that white thing, or humanity from this or that man’), so that it is as impossible to conceive the will as the cause of this or that volition as to conceive humanity as the cause of Peter and Paul (Letter 2). The faculties of the scholastic and Cartesian psychology are, on this account, beings of reason.

But before I go further, it should be noted here that by will I understand the faculty of affirming and denying, and not desire;2 I understand, I say, the faculty by which the mind affirms or denies what is true or what is false, and not the desire by which the mind wants things or is averse to them.
  1. 2 Descartes counted affirming and denying, together with desiring and being averse, among the ‘modes of willing’ (Principles I.32). Spinoza separates them: the will of Props. 48–49 is the faculty of judgment. In Part III, Prop. 9, Schol. the word has another use, for the striving of each thing to persevere in its being ‘when it is related to the mind alone’, and desire is there defined as appetite together with consciousness of it. Gebhardt (Textgestaltung II 365–366) records W. Meijer’s proposal to emend this sentence and Baensch’s view that it preserves the distinction of will and desire from the Short Treatise; he rejects both, observing that the Dutch version has the same text and that Part III itself distinguishes voluntas from cupiditas.
At postquam demonstravimus, has facultates notiones esse universales, quae a singularibus, ex quibus easdem formamus, non distinguuntur, inquirendum iam est, an ipsae volitiones aliquid sint praeter ipsas rerum ideas. Inquirendum, inquam, est, an in mente alia affirmatio et negatio detur praeter illam, quam idea, quatenus idea est, involvit, qua de re vide seq. prop., ut et defin. 3. huius, ne cogitatio in picturas incidat. Non enim per ideas imagines, quales in fundo oculi, et, si placet, in medio cerebro formantur, sed cogitationis conceptus intelligo.a
But now that we have demonstrated that these faculties are universal notions, which are not distinguished from the singulars from which we form them, we must inquire whether the volitions themselves are anything besides the very ideas of things. We must inquire, I say, whether there is in the mind any other affirmation and negation besides that which an idea, insofar as it is an idea, involves (on which see the following Proposition, and also Def. 3 of this Part, so that thought does not fall into pictures3). For by ideas I do not understand images such as are formed at the back of the eye and, if you like, in the middle of the brain,4 but concepts of thought.

Propositio XLIX

Proposition XLIX

[G II 130] In mente nulla datur volitio sive affirmatio et negatio praeter illam, quam idea, quatenus idea est, involvit.
There is in the mind no volition, or affirmation and negation, besides that which an idea, insofar as it is an idea, involves.

Cited in 2P48S, 2P49CD, 2P49S, 3P2S

Demonstratio. In mente (per prop. praeced.) nulla datur absoluta facultas volendi et nolendi, sed tantum singulares volitiones, nempe haec et illa affirmatio, et haec et illa negatio. Concipiamus itaque singularem aliquam volitionem, nempe modum cogitandi, quo mens affirmat, tres angulos trianguli aequales esse duobus rectis. Haec affirmatio conceptum, sive ideam trianguli involvit, hoc est, sine idea trianguli non potest concipi. Idem enim est, si dicam, quod A conceptum B debeat involvere, ac quod A sine B non possit concipi. Deinde haec affirmatio (per axiom. 3. huius) non potest etiam sine idea trianguli esse. Haec ergo affirmatio sine idea trianguli nec esse, nec concipi potest. Porro haec trianguli idea hanc eandem affirmationem involvere debet, nempe, quod tres eius anguli aequentur duobus rectis. Quare et vice versa haec trianguli idea sine hac affirmatione nec esse nec concipi potest; adeoque (per defin. 2. huius) haec affirmatio ad essentiam ideae trianguli pertinet, nec aliud praeter ipsam est. Et quod de hac volitione diximus (quandoquidem eam ad libitum sumpsimus), dicendum etiam est de quacumque volitione, nempe, quod praeter ideam nihil sit. Q. E. D.
Demonstration. There is in the mind (by the preceding Prop.) no absolute faculty of willing and not willing, but only singular volitions, namely this and that affirmation, and this and that negation. Let us conceive, then, some singular volition, namely the mode of thinking by which the mind affirms that the three angles of a triangle are equal to two right angles. This affirmation involves the concept, or idea, of the triangle, that is, it cannot be conceived without the idea of the triangle. For it is the same whether I say that A must involve the concept of B, or that A cannot be conceived without B. Next, this affirmation (by Ax. 3 of this Part) also cannot be without the idea of the triangle. This affirmation, therefore, can neither be nor be conceived without the idea of the triangle. Further, this idea of the triangle must involve this same affirmation, namely that its three angles are equal to two right angles. Therefore, conversely, this idea of the triangle can neither be nor be conceived without this affirmation; and so (by Def. 2 of this Part) this affirmation pertains to the essence of the idea of the triangle,1 and is nothing besides it. And what we have said of this volition (since we took it at random) must also be said of any volition, namely that it is nothing besides the idea. Q.E.D.
  1. 1 The demonstration establishes both halves of the reciprocal definition of essence in Def. 2: the affirmation can neither be nor be conceived without the idea, and the idea can neither be nor be conceived without the affirmation. The fourth sentence states the equivalence, used throughout, between involving a concept and not being conceivable without it (compare Part I, Ax. 4).

Corollary. Will and intellect are one and the same.1

Demonstratio. Voluntas et intellectus nihil praeter ipsas singulares volitiones et ideas sunt (per prop. 48. huius et eiusdem schol.). At singularis volitio et idea (per prop. praeced.) unum et idem sunt. Ergo voluntas et intellectus unum et idem sunt. Q. E. D.
Demonstration. Will and intellect are nothing besides the singular volitions and ideas themselves (by Prop. 48 of this Part and its Scholium). But a singular volition and idea (by the preceding Prop.) are one and the same. Therefore will and intellect are one and the same. Q.E.D.
Scholium. His causam, quae communiter erroris esse statuitur, sustulimus. Supra autem ostendimus, falsitatem in sola privatione, quam ideae mutilatae, et confusae involvunt, consistere. Quare idea falsa, quatenus falsa est, certitudinem non involvit. Cum itaque dicimus, hominem in falsis acquiescere, nec de iis dubitare, non ideo ipsum certum esse, sed tantum non dubitare dicimus, vel quod in falsis acquiescit, quia nullae causae dantur, quae efficiant, ut ipsius imaginatio fluctuetur.a Qua de re vide schol. prop. 44. huius. Quantumvis igitur homo falsis adhaerere supponatur,b nunquam tamen ipsum certum esse dicemus. Nam per certitudinem quid positivum intelligimus (vide prop. 43. huius cum eiusdem schol.), non vero dubitationis privationem. At per certitudinis privationem falsitatem intelligimus.
Scholium. With this we have removed what is commonly held to be the cause of error.1 We have shown above, moreover, that falsity consists only in the privation which mutilated and confused ideas involve.2 Therefore a false idea, insofar as it is false, does not involve certainty. So when we say that a man acquiesces in what is false and does not doubt it, we do not on that account say that he is certain, but only that he does not doubt, or that he acquiesces in what is false because there are no causes which bring it about that his imagination fluctuates. On this see the Scholium of Prop. 44 of this Part. However much, therefore, a man may be supposed to adhere to what is false, we shall never say that he is certain. For by certainty we understand something positive (see Prop. 43 of this Part with its Scholium), not the privation of doubt. But by the privation of certainty we understand falsity.

But for a fuller explanation of the preceding Proposition some things remain to be pointed out. Incipio igitur a primo, lectoresque moneo, ut accurate distinguant inter ideam sive mentis conceptum, et inter imagines rerum, quas imaginamur. Deinde necesse est, ut distinguant inter ideas, et verba, quibus res significamus. Nam quia haec tria, imagines scilicet, verba et ideae a multis vel plane confunduntur, vel non satis accurate, vel denique non satis caute distinguuntur, ideo hanc de voluntate doctrinam, scitu prorsus necessariam, tam ad speculationem,a quam ad vitam sapienter instituendam, plane ignorarunt.
I begin, then, with the first, and I warn my readers to distinguish accurately between an idea, or concept of the mind, and the images of things which we imagine. Next, it is necessary that they distinguish between ideas and the words by which we signify things. For because these three, namely images, words and ideas, are by many either altogether confused, or not distinguished accurately enough, or finally not cautiously enough, they have been altogether ignorant of this doctrine of the will, which it is absolutely necessary to know, both for speculation and for arranging life wisely.

Quippe qui putant ideas consistere in imaginibus, quae in nobis ex corporum occursu formantur, sibi persuadent, ideas illas rerum,b quarum similem nullam imaginem formare possumus, non esse ideas, sed tantum figmenta, quae ex libero voluntatis arbitrio fingimus; ideas igitur veluti picturas in tabula mutas, aspiciunt, et hoc praeiudicio praeoccupati non vident, ideam, quatenus idea est, affirmationem aut negationem involvere. Deinde qui verba confundunt cum idea, vel cum ipsa affirmatione, quam idea involvit, putant se posse contra id, quod sentiunt, velle; quando aliquid solis verbis contra id, quod sentiunt, affirmant aut negant. Haec autem praeiudicia exuere facile is poterit, qui ad naturam cogitationis attendit, quae extensionis conceptum minime involvit; atque adeo clare intelliget, ideam (quandoquidem modus cogitandi est) neque in rei alicuius imagine, neque in verbis consistere. Verborum namque et imaginum essentia a solis motibus corporeis constituitur, qui cogitationis conceptum minime involvunt.
Indeed, those who think that ideas consist in images which are formed in us from the encounter of bodies persuade themselves that those ideas of things of which we can form no similar image are not ideas, but only fictions which we feign from the free choice of the will; they look on ideas, therefore, as mute pictures on a panel, and, preoccupied by this prejudice, do not see that an idea, insofar as it is an idea, involves affirmation or negation. Next, those who confuse words with the idea, or with the very affirmation which the idea involves, think that they can will contrary to what they feel, when by words alone they affirm or deny something contrary to what they feel. But anyone who attends to the nature of thought, which does not at all involve the concept of extension, will easily be able to put off these prejudices; and so he will understand clearly that an idea (since it is a mode of thinking) consists neither in the image of some thing nor in words. For the essence of words and of images is constituted by bodily motions alone,1 which do not at all involve the concept of thought.

Let it suffice to have pointed out these few things about these matters; so I pass to the objections mentioned above. The first of them is that they think it established that the will extends more widely than the intellect, and is therefore different from it. And the reason why they think that the will extends more widely than the intellect is that they say they experience that they do not need a greater faculty of assenting, or of affirming and denying, than we already have, in order to assent to infinitely many other things which we do not perceive; but that we do need a greater faculty of understanding. The will is therefore distinguished from the intellect in that the latter is finite and the former infinite.1

Second, it can be objected to us that experience seems to teach nothing more clearly than that we can suspend our judgment2 so as not to assent to things which we perceive; and this is confirmed also by the fact that no one is said to be deceived insofar as he perceives something, but only insofar as he assents or dissents. For example, he who feigns a winged horse does not on that account grant that there is a winged horse, that is, he is not on that account deceived, unless at the same time he grants that there is a winged horse. Experience seems, therefore, to teach nothing more clearly than that the will, or faculty of assenting, is free, and different from the faculty of understanding.

Ata unam ideam plus realitatis sive perfectionis, quam aliam habere percipimus; quantum enim obiecta alia aliis praestantiora, tantum etiam eorum ideae aliae aliis perfectiores sunt; ex quibus etiam constare videtur differentia inter voluntatem et intellectum.
Third, it can be objected that one affirmation does not seem to contain more reality than another, that is, we do not seem to need greater power to affirm that what is true is true than to affirm that something which is false is true. But we perceive that one idea has more reality, or perfection, than another; for in proportion as some objects are more excellent than others, so also some of their ideas are more perfect than others; and from this also the difference between will and intellect seems to be established.

Nec sane video, cur facultas volendi potius dicenda est infinita, quam sentiendi facultas; sicut enim infinita (unum tamen post aliud; nam infinita simul affirmare non possumus) eadem volendi facultate possumus affirmare, sic etiam infinita corpora (unum nempe post aliudb) eadem sentiendi facultate possumus sentire sive percipere. Quod si dicant, infinita dari, quae percipere non possumus? regero, nos ea ipsa nulla cogitatione, et consequenter nulla volendi facultate posse assequi. At dicunt, si Deus vellet efficere, ut ea etiam perciperemus, maiorem quidem facultatem percipiendi deberet nobis dare, sed non maiorem, quam dedit, volendi facultatem; quod idem est, ac si dicerent, quod si Deus velit efficere, ut infinita alia entia intelligeremus, necesse quidem esset, ut nobis daret maiorem intellectum; sed non universaliorem entis ideam, quam dedit, ad eadem infinita entia amplectendum. Ostendimus enim voluntatem ens esse universale sive ideam, qua omnes singulares volitiones, hoc est, id quod iis omnibus commune est, explicamus. Cum itaque hanc omnium volitionum communem sive universalem ideam facultatemc esse credant, minime mirum, si hanc facultatem ultra limites intellectus in infinitum se extendere dicant. Universale enim aeque de uno, ac de pluribus ac de infinitis individuis dicitur.
And indeed to the first I say that I grant that the will extends more widely than the intellect, if by intellect they understand only clear and distinct ideas; but I deny that the will extends more widely than perceptions, or the faculty of conceiving. Nor indeed do I see why the faculty of willing should be called infinite rather than the faculty of sensing; for just as we can affirm infinitely many things by the same faculty of willing (one after another, however, for we cannot affirm infinitely many things at once), so also we can sense, or perceive, infinitely many bodies (one after another, that is) by the same faculty of sensing. If they say that there are infinitely many things which we cannot perceive, I reply that we can reach them by no thought, and consequently by no faculty of willing. But, they say, if God willed to bring it about that we perceived these too, he would indeed have to give us a greater faculty of perceiving, but not a greater faculty of willing than he has given. This is the same as if they said that if God willed to bring it about that we understood infinitely many other beings, it would indeed be necessary that he give us a greater intellect, but not a more universal idea of being than he has given, in order to embrace those same infinitely many beings. For we have shown that the will is a universal being, or idea, by which we explain all singular volitions, that is, that which is common to them all. Since, therefore, they believe that this common, or universal, idea of all volitions is a faculty, it is not at all surprising if they say that this faculty extends itself beyond the limits of the intellect to infinity. For a universal is said equally of one, of many and of infinitely many individuals.

Ad secundam obiectionem respondeo negando, nos liberam habere potestatem iudicium suspendendi. Nam cum dicimus, aliquem iudicium suspendere, nihil aliud dicimus, quam quod videt, se rem non adaequate percipere. Est igitur iudicii suspensio revera perceptio, et non libera voluntas. Quod ut clare intelligatur, concipiamus puerum equum alatum imaginantem,d nec aliud quicquam percipientem. Quandoquidem haec imaginatio equi existentiam involvit (per coroll. prop. 17. huius) nec puer quicquam percipit, quod equi existentiam tollat (ille necessario equum ut praesentem contemplabitur), nec de eius existentia poterit dubitare, quamvis de eadem non sit certus. Atque hoc quotidie in somnis experimur, nec credo aliquem esse, qui putet, se, dum somniat, liberam habere potestatem suspendendi de iis, quae somniat, iudicium, efficiendique ut ea, quae se videre somniat, non somniet; et nihilominus contingit, ut etiam in somnis iudicium suspendamus, nempe cum somniamus, nos somniare.
To the second objection I reply by denying that we have a free power of suspending judgment. For when we say that someone suspends judgment, we say nothing other than that he sees that he does not perceive the thing adequately. Suspension of judgment, therefore, is in truth a perception, and not free will. That this may be clearly understood, let us conceive a boy imagining a winged horse and perceiving nothing else. Since this imagination involves the existence of the horse (by Cor. of Prop. 17 of this Part), and the boy perceives nothing which excludes the existence of the horse, he will necessarily contemplate the horse as present, nor will he be able to doubt its existence, although he is not certain of it.4 And this we experience daily in dreams; nor do I believe that there is anyone who thinks that, while he is dreaming, he has a free power of suspending judgment about what he dreams, and of bringing it about that he does not dream what he dreams that he sees; and nevertheless it happens that even in dreams we suspend judgment, namely when we dream that we are dreaming.

Porro concedo neminem decipi, quatenus percipit, hoc est, mentis imaginationes in se consideratas nihil erroris involvere concedo (vide schol. prop. 17. huius); sed nego, hominem nihil affirmare, quatenus percipit. Nam quid aliud est equum alatum percipere, quam alas de equo affirmare? Si enim mens praeter equum alatum nihil aliud perciperet, eundem sibi praesentem contemplaretur, nec causam haberet ullam dubitandi de eiusdem existentia, nec ullam dissentiendi facultatem, nisi imaginatio equi alati iuncta sit ideae, quae existentiam eiusdem equi tollit, vel quod percipit, ideam equi alati, quam habet, esse inadaequatam, atque tum vel eiusdem equi existentiam necessario negabit, vel de eadem necessario dubitabit.
Further, I grant that no one is deceived insofar as he perceives, that is, I grant that the imaginations of the mind, considered in themselves, involve no error (see Schol. of Prop. 17 of this Part); but I deny that a man affirms nothing insofar as he perceives. For what else is perceiving a winged horse than affirming wings of a horse? For if the mind perceived nothing else besides the winged horse, it would contemplate it as present to itself, and would have no cause to doubt its existence, nor any faculty of dissenting, unless the imagination of the winged horse is joined to an idea which excludes the existence of the same horse, or it perceives that the idea of the winged horse which it has is inadequate; and then it will either necessarily deny the existence of the horse, or necessarily doubt it.
Atque his puto me ad tertiam etiam obiectionem respondisse, nempe quod voluntas universale quid sit, quod de omnibus ideis praedicatur, quodque id tantum significat, quod omnibus ideis commune est, nempe affirmationem, cuius propterea adaequata essentia,f quatenus sic abstracte concipitur, debet esse in unaquaque idea, et hac ratione tantum in omnibus eadem;e sed non quatenus consideratur essentiam ideae constituere; nam eatenus singulares affirmationes aeque inter se differunt, ac ipsae ideae. Ex. gr. affirmatio, quam idea circuli ab illa, quam idea trianguli involvit, aeque differt, ac idea circuli ab idea trianguli. Deinde absolute nego, nos aequali cogitandi potentia indigere ad affirmandum, verum esse id, quod verum est, quam ad affirmandum, verum esse id, quod falsum est. Nam hae duae affirmationes, si mentem spectes,g se habent ad invicem, ut ens ad non-ens; nihil enim in ideis positivum est, quod falsitatis formam constituit. Vide prop. 35. huius cum eius schol. et schol. prop. 47. huius. Quare hic apprime venit notandum, quam facile decipimur, quando universalia cum singularibus et entia rationis et abstracta cum realibus confundimus.
And with this I think I have also replied to the third objection: namely, that the will is something universal, which is predicated of all ideas, and which signifies only what is common to all ideas, namely affirmation, whose adequate essence, therefore, insofar as it is thus conceived abstractly, must be in each idea, and only in this respect the same in all; but not insofar as it is considered to constitute the essence of the idea, for in that respect singular affirmations differ from one another as much as the ideas themselves do. For example, the affirmation which the idea of a circle involves differs from that which the idea of a triangle involves as much as the idea of the circle differs from the idea of the triangle. Next, I absolutely deny that we need an equal power of thinking to affirm that what is true is true as to affirm that what is false is true. For these two affirmations, if you look to the mind,5 are related to one another as being to not-being; for there is nothing positive in ideas which constitutes the form of falsity. See Prop. 35 of this Part with its Scholium, and the Scholium of Prop. 47 of this Part. Therefore it is especially to be noted here how easily we are deceived when we confuse universals with singulars, and beings of reason and abstractions with real beings.

Si me rogant, an talis homo non potius asinus, quam homo sit aestimandus? dico me nescire, ut etiam nescio, quanti aestimandus sit ille, qui se pensilem facit, et quanti aestimandi sint pueri, stulti, vesani etc.
Finally, as regards the fourth objection, I say that I entirely grant that a man placed in such an equilibrium (namely, one who perceives nothing else but thirst and hunger, and such food and such drink, which are equally distant from him) will perish of hunger and thirst.6 If they ask me whether such a man is not to be reckoned an ass rather than a man, I say that I do not know; just as I also do not know at what to reckon one who hangs himself, or at what to reckon children, fools, madmen, etc.

[G II 135] Superest tandem indicare, quantum huius doctrinae cognitio ad usum vitae conferat, quod facile ex his animadvertemus. Nempe I. Quatenus docet nos ex solo Dei nutu agere, divinaeque naturae esse participes, et eo magis quo perfectiores actiones agimus et quo magis magisque Deum intelligimus. Haec ergo doctrina, praeterquam quod animum omnimode quietum reddit, hoc etiam habet, quod nos docet, in quo nostra summa felicitas sive beatitudo consistit, nempe in sola Dei cognitione, ex qua ad ea tantum agenda inducimur, quae amor et pietas suadent. It remains, finally, to indicate how much the knowledge of this doctrine contributes to the conduct of life, which we shall easily see from the following. Namely: I. Insofar as it teaches that we act by God’s bidding alone, and are sharers in the divine nature, and the more so, the more perfect the actions we do and the more and more we understand God. This doctrine, then, besides making the mind entirely at peace, has this also, that it teaches us in what our highest happiness, or blessedness, consists: namely in the knowledge of God alone, by which we are led to do only those things which love and piety urge.1 From this we understand clearly how far they stray from the true estimate of virtue who, for virtue and the best actions, as if for the utmost servitude, expect to be honoured by God with the highest rewards, as if virtue itself and servitude to God were not happiness itself and the highest freedom.2
