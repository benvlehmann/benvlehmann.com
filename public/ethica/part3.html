Ethices Pars Tertia
De Origine et Natura Affectuum

Third Part of the Ethics
Of the Origin and Nature of the Affects

Introduction to the Third Part

The third Part bears the title De origine et natura affectuum, “Of the Origin and Nature of the Affects.” It is the hinge of the Ethics. Parts I and II have established what God is and what the human mind is; Parts IV and V will treat human bondage and human freedom. Between them Spinoza needs an account of what moves us: why we love and hate, hope and fear, envy, pity and despise, and why we so often see the better and follow the worse. Part III supplies it. It is the longest Part of the book after Part IV, the richest in observation of ordinary life, and the one in which Spinoza’s naturalism is most consistently and most provocatively applied. A reader who has found the first two Parts remote from human concerns will find in the third the reason for them: the metaphysics of substance and the theory of ideas were built so that the passions could be understood as natural things.

A dominion within a dominion

The method is announced in the Preface, with deliberate provocation. “Most of those who have written about the affects and about men’s way of living seem to treat not of natural things, which follow the common laws of nature, but of things which are outside nature. Indeed, they seem to conceive man in nature as a dominion within a dominion” (Preface). They believe that man disturbs the order of nature rather than follows it, that he has absolute power over his actions, and that he is determined by nothing but himself. They attribute human weakness and inconstancy not to the common power of nature but to some vice of human nature, which they “weep over, laugh at, despise, or, as usually happens, detest,” and the one who can censure the weakness of the human mind most eloquently or most cleverly “is regarded as if he were divine.”

Against them Spinoza sets a single principle. “Nothing happens in nature which can be attributed to a vice of nature; for nature is always the same, and its virtue and power of acting are everywhere one and the same; that is, the laws and rules of nature, according to which all things happen and change from one form into another, are always and everywhere the same.” There must therefore be one way of understanding things of every kind, through the universal laws of nature. Hatred, anger and envy “follow from the same necessity and virtue of nature as other singular things,” and have causes through which they are understood and properties “as worthy of our knowledge as the properties of any other thing.” And so: “I shall consider human actions and appetites just as if the question were of lines, planes or bodies.” The Political Treatise puts the same resolve more personally: “I have taken pains not to laugh at, nor weep over, nor detest human actions, but to understand them” (sedulo curavi, humanas actiones non ridere, non lugere, neque detestari, sed intelligere; TP I.4).

The geometrical treatment of the passions is easily misunderstood. Spinoza does not mean that feelings are to be measured, or that human beings are machines. He means that the affects are effects of definite causes, related to one another by definite laws, and that a knowledge of these causes and laws is possible and is worth having for its own sake. The phrase “as worthy of our knowledge as the properties of any other thing by the mere contemplation of which we are delighted” is the key. A mathematician is not indignant at the properties of a triangle; a student of the affects should not be indignant at the properties of envy. The attitude is not indifference, and it is not cynicism. It is the condition of any remedy.

Descartes and the passions

One predecessor is named, and only to be dismissed. Descartes, “although he too believed that the mind has absolute power over its actions,” had tried to explain the passions through their first causes and to show the way by which the mind can have absolute dominion over them; “but in my opinion he showed nothing but the acumen of his great ingenuity, as I shall demonstrate in its place” (Preface). The Passions of the Soul (1649) had in fact claimed this in the heading of one of its articles: there is no soul so weak that it cannot, if well directed, acquire an absolute power over its passions (art. 50). The “place” in which Spinoza demonstrates the contrary turns out to be the Preface to Part V.

Part III owes Descartes more than the Preface admits. Its procedure, a small number of primary affects from which all the others are derived by combination and by the variety of their objects, is Descartes’ procedure, though the list is different. Descartes recognized six primitive passions: wonder, love, hatred, desire, joy and sadness (Passions, art. 69). Spinoza recognizes three, desire, joy and sadness (Prop. 11, Schol.); love and hatred are joy and sadness accompanied by the idea of an external cause (Prop. 13, Schol.), and wonder is not an affect at all, but a distraction of the mind that has no positive cause (Def. Aff. 4, Expl.).

The difference lies deeper than the list. For Descartes a passion is something the soul undergoes because of the body, an effect in the soul of motions in the body transmitted through the pineal gland, and the remedy is for the will, which is free, to take control of the passions by joining them to other thoughts and by forming “firm and determinate judgments concerning the knowledge of good and evil” (Passions, arts. 45, 48). Spinoza has already removed both terms of that remedy. The mind and the body are one thing conceived under two attributes (Part II, Prop. 7, Schol.), so neither can act on the other (Prop. 2); and there is no free will, only volitions that are the affirmations involved in ideas (Part II, Prop. 49). If the passions are to be mastered, it cannot be by the will acting on the body; it must be in some other way.

Mind and body once more

The long scholium to Prop. 2 argues against mind-body interaction from experience as well as from reason, and it is one of the most vivid passages in the book. Spinoza doubts that his readers will weigh the matter with an equal mind unless he confirms it by experience, “so firmly are they persuaded that the body now moves, now is at rest, at the mind’s bidding alone.” But experience teaches nothing of the kind. “No one has so far known the structure of the body so accurately as to be able to explain all its functions,” not to mention that many things are observed in animals which far surpass human ingenuity, “and that sleepwalkers do a great many things in their sleep which they would not dare to do awake; which shows well enough that the body itself, from the laws of its own nature alone, can do many things at which its mind wonders.” No one knows how the mind could move the body, or how much motion it could give it. When men say that this or that action of the body arises from the mind, which has dominion over the body, “they do not know what they are saying, and do nothing but confess, in fine-sounding words, that they are ignorant of the true cause of that action.”

The objection from speech is answered in the same way. People think that it is in the mind’s power alone to speak or to keep silent. But if experience had not taught us that we do many things we afterwards regret, and that “when we are torn by contrary affects, we see the better and follow the worse,” nothing would prevent us from believing that we do everything freely. “So the infant believes that it freely wants milk, the angry boy that he wants vengeance, and the timid one flight. The drunk, again, believes that he says from a free decree of the mind what afterwards, when sober, he would wish he had kept silent about.” The conclusion returns to Part II: “the decree of the mind and the appetite and determination of the body are simultaneous in nature, or rather are one and the same thing, which we call a decree when it is considered under the attribute of thought and explained through it, and a determination when it is considered under the attribute of extension.” And so those “who believe that they speak or are silent or do anything from a free decree of the mind dream with their eyes open” (Prop. 2, Schol.).

The phrase “no one has so far known the structure of the body” is sometimes quoted as a general remark about human ignorance, but in context it is an argument. If we do not know what the body can do by its own laws, we have no reason to suppose that anything the body does requires the intervention of the mind. The body’s achievements, from the instincts of animals to the acts of sleepwalkers, show how far mechanism can go without any mental command.

Action and passion

Since action and passion cannot be defined by the will, they have to be redefined, and the definitions at the head of the Part do this without mentioning the will at all. An adequate cause is one “whose effect can be perceived clearly and distinctly through it,” an inadequate or partial cause one “whose effect cannot be understood through it alone” (Def. 1). “I say that we act when something happens, in us or outside us, of which we are the adequate cause,” and that we are acted on when something happens in us, or follows from our nature, of which we are only a partial cause (Def. 2). An affect is defined as “affections of the body by which the body’s power of acting is increased or diminished, aided or restrained, and at the same time the ideas of these affections”; if we can be the adequate cause of one of these affections, the affect is an action, otherwise a passion (Def. 3).

The first three propositions draw the consequences. “Our mind acts in some things and is acted on in others; namely, insofar as it has adequate ideas, it necessarily acts in some things, and insofar as it has inadequate ideas, it is necessarily acted on in some things” (Prop. 1), and “the actions of the mind arise from adequate ideas alone; but the passions depend on inadequate ideas alone” (Prop. 3). Activity and passivity are therefore matters of knowledge. To be active is to be the complete explanation of what happens in one; to be passive is to be only part of the explanation, the rest lying in external causes. This is the foundation of everything Part V will say about the power of the intellect over the affects, and it is perhaps the most important single idea of the Part. Freedom will not consist in a will that escapes causes, but in being oneself the adequate cause of what one does, and that will be a matter of understanding.

The striving to persevere

The centre of the Part is the doctrine of striving, worked out in Props. 4–9. “No thing can be destroyed except by an external cause” (Prop. 4), since “the definition of any thing affirms the essence of the thing itself, but does not deny it” (Prop. 4, Dem.): attending only to the thing itself, we find nothing in it that could destroy it. Things are of contrary natures insofar as one can destroy the other (Prop. 5). Then comes the central proposition: “Each thing, as far as it is in itself, strives to persevere in its being” (Prop. 6). The demonstration ties the doctrine to Part I. Singular things are modes by which God’s attributes are expressed in a certain and determinate way, “that is ... things which express in a certain and determinate way the power of God by which God is and acts”; nothing has in itself anything by which it could be destroyed; “and so, as far as it can and is in itself, it strives to persevere in its being” (Prop. 6, Dem.). The striving is “nothing but the actual essence of the thing itself” (Prop. 7), and it involves no finite time, but an indefinite one (Prop. 8).

The Latin word is conatus, and its sense should be understood carefully. It is not a separate drive that a thing has in addition to its nature, nor a desire for some future state, but the thing’s own power of acting, considered as resisting whatever would destroy it. A stone at rest, a body in motion, a plant, an animal and a human being all strive in this sense. The phrase “as far as it is in itself” (quantum in se est) repeats the language in which Descartes had stated the principle of inertia (Principles II.37), and the physical digression of Part II had given the same principle for bodies (Part II, Lemma 3, Cor.). What Part III adds is the application of this principle to the mind, and so to the whole of human life.

In a human being the striving takes several names. “This striving, when it is related to the mind alone, is called will; but when it is related to the mind and body together, it is called appetite, which is therefore nothing but the very essence of man, from whose nature there necessarily follow those things which serve his preservation” (Prop. 9, Schol.). Between appetite and desire there is no difference, except that desire is appetite together with consciousness of it. And then comes a sentence that reverses the order assumed by most moral philosophy: “we strive for, will, want and desire nothing because we judge it to be good; but on the contrary, we judge something to be good because we strive for it, will it, want it and desire it.” Values are not perceived and then pursued. They are the shadows cast by our desires.

Jonathan Bennett remarks that Spinoza’s “substantive morality will be based on a strong individual egoism,” and that the argument for it, running through Props. 4–6, “deserves to be tracked patiently” (A Study of Spinoza’s Ethics, § 54, p. 231). Whether the argument succeeds, and whether it shows that everything strives to persevere in its being or only that nothing destroys itself, has been much discussed. The reader should notice at least that the doctrine is meant to apply to everything, not only to living things, and that it is derived from the metaphysics of Part I, not from observation of human behaviour.

Joy, sadness and desire

From the doctrine of striving Spinoza derives the primary affects. “Whatever increases or diminishes, aids or restrains, the power of acting of our body, the idea of that same thing increases or diminishes, aids or restrains, the power of thinking of our mind” (Prop. 11). The mind can pass to a greater or a lesser perfection, and these passages are the affects of joy and sadness: “By joy, therefore, I shall understand in what follows the passion by which the mind passes to a greater perfection. By sadness, however, the passion by which it passes to a lesser perfection” (Prop. 11, Schol.). Joy related to mind and body together is called pleasure or cheerfulness, sadness pain or melancholy, according as one part of the body or the whole is affected. Desire has been defined in the scholium to Prop. 9, “and apart from these three I acknowledge no other primary affect; for I shall show in what follows that the rest arise from these three.”

Joy and sadness, so defined, are transitions, not states. A man who was born with a greater perfection would not feel joy at it; joy is the feeling of becoming more powerful. This dynamic conception explains much of what follows. Since the mind strives to persevere and to increase its power of acting, it “strives to imagine those things which increase or aid the body’s power of acting” (Prop. 12), and when it imagines what diminishes the body’s power, it strives to recollect things that exclude their existence (Prop. 13). From this follow love and hatred: “love is nothing but joy accompanied by the idea of an external cause, and hatred nothing but sadness accompanied by the idea of an external cause” (Prop. 13, Schol.). A lover strives to have present and to preserve what he loves, a hater to remove and destroy what he hates.

The mechanics of the passions

The rest of the Part, Props. 14–57, derives the variety of the affects from these few principles, and it does so with a combination of rigour and observation that has no parallel in earlier philosophy. Three mechanisms do most of the work.

The first is association. If the mind has once been affected by two affects at the same time, then when it is afterwards affected by one, it will also be affected by the other (Prop. 14), and so anything can be the accidental cause of joy, sadness or desire (Prop. 15). We love or hate a thing merely because it resembles something that usually affects us with joy or sadness, “even though that in which the thing is similar to the object is not the efficient cause of these affects” (Prop. 16). When one thing affects us with both joy and sadness, we love and hate it at once, and this state is called “fluctuation of mind,” which “is therefore related to the affect as doubt is to the imagination” (Prop. 17, Schol.). Hope and fear arise from the image of a thing of whose outcome we are in doubt: “hope is nothing but an inconstant joy arising from the image of a future or past thing of whose outcome we are in doubt; fear, on the other hand, an inconstant sadness, arising also from the image of a doubtful thing” (Prop. 18, Schol. 2).

The second mechanism is the transmission of affects through the objects of love and hatred. We rejoice when we imagine that what we love is preserved and are saddened when we imagine it destroyed; we love whatever affects what we love with joy, and hate whatever affects it with sadness; and the reverse holds for what we hate (Props. 19–26). The affects thus spread through a network of relations, and much of the misery Spinoza describes arises from the fact that the objects of our love can be possessed by others, lost, or changed.

The third and most original mechanism is the imitation of affects. “From the fact that we imagine a thing like us, towards which we have had no affect, to be affected with some affect, we are thereby affected with a like affect” (Prop. 27). The proposition is derived from the theory of Part II: the image of an external body like ours involves the nature of our body as well as its, and so an affection like the one we imagine in the other. From imitation Spinoza derives pity, which is the imitation of sadness, and emulation, the imitation of desire (Prop. 27, Schol.); the striving to please others, which is called ambition when it is pursued to our own harm or another’s, and otherwise courtesy (Prop. 29, Schol.); and, by a further step, the desire that everyone should love what we love and hate what we hate. “So we see that each by nature wants the others to live according to his temperament; and while all want this alike, they are alike an obstacle to one another, and while all want to be praised, or loved, by all, they hate one another” (Prop. 31, Schol.). From the same property of human nature that makes men compassionate, Spinoza adds, it follows that they are envious and ambitious; and experience confirms it, especially in children, who “laugh or cry simply because they see others laugh or cry,” and desire whatever they see others delight in (Prop. 32, Schol.).

Several of the later propositions are among the most quoted in the book. “Hatred is increased by reciprocal hatred, and can, on the other hand, be destroyed by love” (Prop. 43), and hatred completely conquered by love passes into a love greater than if hatred had not preceded it (Prop. 44). “Good” and “evil” are defined, in the scholium to Prop. 39, by reference to joy and sadness: by good Spinoza understands here “every kind of joy, and whatever, further, leads to it,” and by evil every kind of sadness, so that “each judges, or evaluates, from his own affect what is good, what evil, what better, what worse.” Different men can be affected differently by the same object, and the same man differently at different times (Prop. 51). There are as many species of each affect as there are species of objects by which we are affected (Prop. 56), and “each affect of each individual differs from the affect of another as much as the essence of the one differs from the essence of the other” (Prop. 57). The scholium to this last proposition notes that the affects of the animals differ from ours as their natures differ, since “we can by no means doubt that the brutes feel, now that we know the origin of the mind,” and that “there is no small difference between the gladness by which a drunkard, for example, is led, and the gladness which a philosopher possesses” (Prop. 57, Schol.).

The active affects

The last two propositions turn from the passions to the affects that are actions. “Apart from the joy and desire which are passions, there are other affects of joy and desire which are related to us insofar as we act” (Prop. 58), and among the affects related to the mind insofar as it acts there are none but those related to joy or desire (Prop. 59); sadness, which diminishes the mind’s power of acting, can never be an action. The actions that follow from the affects related to the mind insofar as it understands Spinoza relates to strength of character, fortitudo, which he divides into tenacity (animositas) and nobility (generositas): “by tenacity I understand the desire by which each strives, from the dictate of reason alone, to preserve his being. By nobility, however, I understand the desire by which each strives, from the dictate of reason alone, to aid other men and join them to himself in friendship” (Prop. 59, Schol.). Temperance, sobriety and presence of mind in dangers are species of tenacity; modesty and clemency are species of nobility. These two words will carry much of the ethics of Part IV.

The same scholium sums up the condition that the Part has described. From what has been shown, “it is evident that we are driven about in many ways by external causes, and that, like waves of the sea driven by contrary winds, we fluctuate, ignorant of our outcome and fate.” The image is the most memorable in the Part, and it states the problem to which Parts IV and V are the answer.

The definitions of the affects

The Part ends with a recapitulation: forty-eight definitions of the affects, with explanations, and a general definition of the affect that is a passion of the mind. The definitions follow a classification of their own, and several of them correct what the scholia had said; an essay before them introduces their plan.

Questions the reader will meet

Several features of Part III need care.

The first is the vocabulary. “Affect” (affectus) is not the same as emotion. It includes desire, which is not a feeling in the ordinary sense, and it is defined by a change in the body’s power of acting together with the idea of that change; so it is both bodily and mental. “Passion” is not intense feeling but being acted on, being only the partial cause of what happens in one. “Joy” (laetitia) and “sadness” (tristitia) are broader than their English names suggest; they cover every increase or decrease of power that the mind undergoes, from physical pleasure to intellectual delight, and from a toothache to grief.

The second concerns perfection. Joy is the passage to a greater perfection, and perfection is the same as reality (Part II, Def. 6). To be more perfect is to be more powerful, to be able to do and to undergo more, and not to be better in a moral sense. The doctrine that we judge things good because we desire them should be read in the same light. It does not say that whatever anyone desires is good. Part IV will define the good as what we certainly know to be useful to us, and will distinguish between what the passions lead us to desire and what reason does.

The third concerns egoism. If every thing strives to persevere in its being, and the human mind seeks only what increases its power, it may seem that Spinoza’s psychology leaves no room for concern for others. But the imitation of the affects, which makes us share the joys and sorrows of those we take to be like us, is derived from the same principles, and Part III already names nobility, the desire to aid others and join them to oneself in friendship, as one of the two species of strength of character. Part IV will argue that nothing is more useful to a human being than other human beings who live by reason. Whether this amounts to a genuine concern for others or an enlightened self-interest is a question that readers have answered in different ways.

The fourth concerns teleology. The Appendix to Part I attacked final causes, and Part III speaks constantly of what we strive for and desire. The striving of Prop. 6 is not a striving toward a goal in the sense the Appendix rejects; it is the actual essence of the thing, and the things it leads us to do follow from it as effects from a cause. Bennett describes the Part as an attempt to move from a Spinozist “appetite,” a push, to something that functions like genuine teleology, a pull (§ 54, p. 231). How far Spinoza succeeds, and how far he needs to, is a question for the reader to keep in mind.

The place of Part III in the whole

The account of the affects is what makes the rest of the Ethics possible. Part IV will ask how strong the affects are and why they so often overcome our knowledge, and it will rest its answer on the principle that “an affect can be neither restrained nor removed except by an affect contrary to it and stronger than the affect to be restrained” (Part IV, Prop. 7). It will also ask which affects agree with reason and which are opposed to it, using the definitions at the end of this Part as its terms. Part V will propose remedies for the passions, and every one of them depends on the theory worked out here: that an affect which is a passion ceases to be a passion as soon as we form a clear and distinct idea of it, that the mind can order and connect the affections of the body according to the order of the intellect, and that the intellectual love of God arises from the third kind of knowledge as other loves arise from their causes. The derivations of Part III were offered as explanations of human life as it is. They are also the material from which Spinoza will build an account of how it can be changed.

The text begins with the Preface, the declaration of method quoted above. The definitions and postulates that follow are few, and the first propositions are abstract; the scholia, from the second onwards, are where the Part’s observation of human life begins.

To them it will doubtless seem strange that I undertake to treat men’s vices and follies in the geometrical manner, and that I wish to demonstrate by sure reasoning things which they proclaim to be contrary to reason, vain, absurd and horrible. But my reasoning is this. Nothing happens in nature which can be attributed to a vice of nature; for nature is always the same, and its virtue and power of acting4 are everywhere one and the same; that is, the laws and rules of nature, according to which all things happen and change from one form into another, are always and everywhere the same; and so there must also be one and the same way of understanding the nature of things of any kind whatever, namely through the universal laws and rules of nature. Proposition I

P. 2.), et quae deinde inadaequatae sunt in mente, sunt etiam in Deo (per idem coroll.) adaequatae, non quatenus eiusdem solummodo mentis essentiam, sed etiam quatenus aliarum rerum mentes in se simul continet. Deinde ex data quacumque idea aliquis effectus sequi necessario debet (per prop. 36. P. 1.), cuius effectus Deus causa est adaequata (vide defin. 1. huius), non quatenus infinitus est, sed quatenus data illa idea affectus consideratur (vide prop. 9. P. 2.). At eius effectus, cuius Deus est causa, quatenus affectus est idea, quae in alicuius mente est adaequata, illa eadem mens est causa adaequata (per coroll. prop. 11. P. 2.). Ergo mens nostra (per defin. 2. huius) quatenus ideas habet adaequatas, quaedam necessario agit; quod erat primum. Deinde quicquid necessario sequitur ex idea, quae in Deo est adaequata, non quatenus mentem unius hominis tantum, sed quatenus aliarum rerum mentes simul cum eiusdem hominis mente in se habet, eius (per idem coroll. prop. 11 P. 2.) illius hominis mens non est causa adaequata, sed partialis. Ac proinde (per defin. 2. huius) mens quatenus ideas inadaequatas habet, quaedam necessario patitur; quod erat secundum. Ergo mens nostra etc. Q. E. D.
Demonstration. Of each human mind some ideas are adequate, others mutilated and confused (by the Schol. of Prop. 40 of Part II). But the ideas which are adequate in someone’s mind are adequate in God insofar as he constitutes the essence of that mind (by Cor. of Prop. 11 of Part II); and those which are inadequate in the mind are also adequate in God (by the same Corollary), not insofar as he contains in himself the essence of that mind only, but also insofar as he contains in himself the minds of other things at the same time. Next, from any given idea some effect must necessarily follow (by Prop. 36 of Part I), of which effect God is the adequate cause (see Def. 1 of this Part), not insofar as he is infinite, but insofar as he is considered as affected by that given idea (see Prop. 9 of Part II). But of that effect of which God is the cause insofar as he is affected by an idea which is adequate in someone’s mind, that same mind is the adequate cause (by Cor. of Prop. 11 of Part II). Therefore our mind (by Def. 2 of this Part), insofar as it has adequate ideas, necessarily acts in some things; which was the first point. Next, whatever necessarily follows from an idea which is adequate in God, not insofar as he has in himself the mind of one man only, but insofar as he has in himself the minds of other things together with the mind of that man, of that (by the same Cor. of Prop. 11 of Part II) the man’s mind is not the adequate cause, but a partial cause. And therefore (by Def. 2 of this Part) the mind, insofar as it has inadequate ideas, is necessarily acted on in some things; which was the second point. Therefore our mind, etc. Q.E.D.

Propositio II

Proposition II

Nec corpus mentem ad cogitandum, nec mens corpus ad motum, neque ad quietem, nec ad aliquid (si quid est) aliud determinare potest.
The body cannot determine the mind to thinking, nor can the mind determine the body to motion,1 or to rest, or to anything else (if there is anything else).
  1. 1 The denial of interaction between mind and body. It follows from the causal independence of the attributes (Part II, Prop. 6) and, as the Scholium says, is better understood from their identity: the mind and the body are one and the same thing conceived under two attributes (Part II, Prop. 7, Schol.). Descartes’ account of their interaction through the pineal gland is examined in the Preface to Part V.

Cited in 5P1D

Demonstratio. Omnes cogitandi modi Deum, quatenus res est cogitans et non quatenus alio attributo explicatur, pro causa habent (per prop. 6. P. 2.). Id ergo, quod mentem ad cogitandum determinat, modus cogitandi est, et non extensionis, hoc est (per defin. 1. P. 2.) non est corpus; quod erat primum. Corporis deinde motus et quies ab alio oriri debet corpore, quod etiam ad motum vel quietem determinatum fuit ab alio, et absolute quicquid in corpore oritur, id a Deo oriri debuit, quatenus aliquo extensionis modo et non quatenus aliquo cogitandi modo affectus consideratur (per eandem prop. 6. P. 2.), hoc est, a mente, quae (per prop. 11. P. 2.) modus cogitandi est, oriri non potest; quod erat secundum. Ergo nec corpus mentem etc. Q. E. D.
Demonstration. All modes of thinking have God for their cause insofar as he is a thinking thing, and not insofar as he is explained by another attribute (by Prop. 6 of Part II). What determines the mind to thinking, therefore, is a mode of thinking and not of extension, that is (by Def. 1 of Part II), it is not a body; which was the first point. Next, the motion and rest of a body must arise from another body, which was also determined to motion or rest by another; and absolutely, whatever arises in a body must have arisen from God insofar as he is considered as affected by some mode of extension, and not insofar as he is considered as affected by some mode of thinking (by the same Prop. 6 of Part II); that is, it cannot arise from the mind, which (by Prop. 11 of Part II) is a mode of thinking; which was the second point. Therefore neither can the body determine the mind, etc. Q.E.D.

Nam nemo hucusque corporis fabricam tam accurate novit, ut omnes eius functiones potuerit explicare, ut iam taceam quod in brutis plura observentur, quae humanam sagacitatem longe superant, et quod somnambuli in somnis plurima agant, quae vigilando non auderent; quod satis ostendit, ipsum corpus ex solis suae naturae legibus multa posse, quae ipsius mens admiratur. Unde sequitur, cum homines dicunt, hanc vel illam actionem corporis oriri a mente, quae imperium in corpus habet, eos nescire, quid dicant, nec aliud agere, quam speciosis verbis fateri, se veram illius actionis causam absque admiratione ignorare.
But although these things are so, so that no reason for doubting remains, I hardly believe that men can be induced to weigh them with an equal mind unless I confirm the matter by experience; so firmly are they persuaded that the body now moves, now is at rest, at the mind’s bidding alone, and does a great many things which depend only on the will of the mind and its art of thinking things out. For what the body can do, no one has so far determined;1 that is, experience has so far taught no one what the body can do from the laws of nature alone, insofar as nature is considered only as corporeal, and what it cannot do unless it is determined by the mind. For no one has so far known the structure of the body so accurately as to be able to explain all its functions, not to mention that many things are observed in brutes which far surpass human ingenuity, and that sleepwalkers do a great many things in their sleep which they would not dare to do awake; which shows well enough that the body itself, from the laws of its own nature alone, can do many things at which its mind wonders. Next, no one knows in what way, or by what means, the mind moves the body, nor how many degrees of motion it can give to the body, and with what speed it can move it. From this it follows that when men say that this or that action of the body arises from the mind, which has dominion over the body, they do not know what they are saying, and do nothing but confess, in fine-sounding words, that they are ignorant of the true cause of that action, and do not wonder at it.

All these things certainly show clearly that both the decree of the mind and the appetite and determination of the body are simultaneous in nature, or rather are one and the same thing, which we call a decree when it is considered under the attribute of thought and explained through it, and a determination when it is considered under the attribute of extension5 and deduced from the laws of motion and rest; which will be still more clearly evident from what is to be said now. For there is something else which I should like to be especially noted here, namely that we can do nothing from a decree of the mind unless we recollect it. For example, we cannot speak a word unless we recollect it. Next, it is not in the free power of the mind to recollect a thing or to forget it. That is why it is believed that only this is in the power of the mind: that we can, from the mind’s decree alone, either be silent about or speak of a thing which we recollect. But when we dream that we speak, we believe that we speak from a free decree of the mind, and yet we do not speak, or, if we do speak, it happens from a spontaneous motion of the body. We dream, next, that we conceal certain things from men, and that by the same decree of the mind by which, while awake, we are silent about the things we know. We dream, finally, that we do from a decree of the mind certain things which we do not dare to do while awake; and so I should very much like to know whether there are in the mind two kinds of decrees, the fantastic and the free. But if one does not wish to go so far in madness, it must necessarily be granted that this decree of the mind which is believed to be free is not distinguished from the imagination itself, or memory, and is nothing but that affirmation which an idea, insofar as it is an idea, necessarily involves. See Prop. 49 of Part II. And so these decrees of the mind arise in the mind with the same necessity as the ideas of things actually existing. Those, therefore, who believe that they speak or are silent or do anything from a free decree of the mind dream with their eyes open.

Proposition III

Propositio IV

Proposition IV

Nulla res nisi a causa externa potest destrui.
No thing can be destroyed except by an external cause.1
  1. 1 The argument of the Demonstration moves from what the definition of a thing contains to what the thing itself can do. Part IV draws the consequence for suicide: no one neglects to preserve his being, or kills himself, ‘from the necessity of his own nature’, but only ‘compelled by external causes’ (Part IV, Prop. 20, Schol.).

Cited in 3P5D, 3P6D, 3P8D, 3P11S, 4P1D, 4P4D, 4P18S, 4P20D, 4P30D

Demonstratio. Haec propositio per se patet. Definitio enim cuiuscumque rei ipsius rei essentiam affirmat, sed non negat; sive rei essentiam ponit, sed non tollit. Dum itaque ad rem ipsam tantum, non autem ad causas externas attendimus, nihil in eadem poterimus invenire, quod ipsam possit destruere. Q. E. D.
Demonstration. This Proposition is evident through itself. For the definition of any thing affirms the essence of the thing itself, but does not deny it; or it posits the essence of the thing, but does not take it away. So while we attend only to the thing itself, and not to external causes, we shall be able to find nothing in it which could destroy it. Q.E.D.

Propositio V

Proposition V

Res eatenus contrariae sunt naturae, hoc est, eatenus in eodem subiecto esse nequeunt, quatenus una alteram potest destruere.
Things are of a contrary nature, that is, cannot be in the same subject, insofar as one can destroy the other.

Propositio VI

Proposition VI

Unaquaeque res, quantum in se est, in suo esse perseverare conatur.
Each thing, as far as it is in itself, strives to persevere in its being.1
  1. 1 The doctrine of the conatus, the centre of Part III. Quantum in se est is the phrase of Descartes’ first law of nature, that each thing ‘quantum in se est, semper in eodem statu perseveret’ (‘as far as is in it, always perseveres in the same state’, Principles II.37, AT VIII-1 62), which Spinoza had stated for bodies in Part II, Lemma 3, Cor.. Here it is extended to every thing under every attribute, and it was anticipated in Part II, Prop. 45, Schol.: the force by which each thing perseveres in existing follows from the eternal necessity of God’s nature.

Cited in 3P7D, 3P12D, 3P44S, 4P4D, 4P20D, 4P25D, 4P26D, 4P31D, 4P60D, 4P64D

Demonstratio. Res enim singulares modi sunt, quibus Dei attributa certo et determinato modo exprimuntur (per coroll. prop. 25. P. 1.), hoc est (per prop. 34. P. 1.) res, quae Dei potentiam qua Deus est et agit, certo et determinato modo exprimunt. Neque ulla res aliquid in se habet, a quo possit destrui, sive quod eius existentiam tollat (per prop. 4. huius); sed contra ei omni, quod eiusdem existentiam potest tollere, opponitur (per prop. praeced.). Adeoque quantum potest et in se est, in suo esse perseverare conatur. Q. E. D.
Demonstration. For singular things are modes by which God’s attributes are expressed in a certain and determinate way (by Cor. of Prop. 25 of Part I), that is (by Prop. 34 of Part I), things which express in a certain and determinate way the power of God by which God is and acts.1 And no thing has in itself anything by which it could be destroyed, or which takes its existence away (by Prop. 4 of this Part); on the contrary, it is opposed to everything which can take its existence away (by the preceding Prop.). And so, as far as it can and is in itself, it strives to persevere in its being. Q.E.D.
  1. 1 The first step grounds the striving of finite things in the power of God, which is his essence (Part I, Prop. 34): each thing is a certain and determinate portion of that power. The striving is thus not a tendency added to the thing but its share in the power by which God exists and acts.

Propositio VII

Proposition VII

Conatus, quo unaquaeque res in suo esse perseverare conatur, nihil est praeter ipsius rei actualem essentiam.
The striving by which each thing strives to persevere in its being is nothing but the actual essence of the thing itself.

Demonstratio. Ex data cuiuscumque rei essentia quaedam necessario sequuntur (per prop. 36. P. 1.), nec res aliud possunt, quam id quod ex determinata earum natura necessario sequitur (per prop. 29. P. 1.). Quare cuiuscumque rei potentia sive conatus, quo ipsa vel sola vel cum aliis quidquam agit, vel agere conatur, hoc est (per prop. 6. huius) potentia sive conatus, quo in suo esse perseverare conatur, nihil est praeter ipsius rei datam sive actualem essentiam. Q. E. D.
Demonstration. From the given essence of any thing certain things necessarily follow (by Prop. 36 of Part I), and things can do nothing but what necessarily follows from their determinate nature (by Prop. 29 of Part I). Therefore the power of any thing, or the striving by which it, either alone or with others, does anything or strives to do anything, that is (by Prop. 6 of this Part), the power, or striving, by which it strives to persevere in its being, is nothing but the given, or actual, essence of the thing itself.1 Q.E.D.

What a thing is and what it does are not two things: its essence is a power of producing effects, and its striving to persevere is that same power considered as it meets the things that oppose it.

Propositio VIII

Proposition VIII

[G II 147] Conatus, quo unaquaeque res in suo esse perseverare conatur, nullum tempus finitum, sed indefinitum involvit.
The striving by which each thing strives to persevere in its being involves no finite time, but an indefinite time.

Cited in 3P9D

Demonstratio. Si enim tempus limitatum involveret, quod rei durationem determinaret, tum ex sola ipsa potentia, qua res existit, sequeretur, quod res post limitatum illud tempus non posset existere, sed quod deberet destrui. Atqui hoc (per prop. 4. huius) est absurdum. Ergo conatus, quo res existit, nullum tempus definitum involvit, sed contra, quoniam (per eandem prop. 4. huius) si a nulla externa causa destruatur, eadem potentia, qua iam existit, existere perget semper, ergo hic conatus tempus indefinitum involvit. Q. E. D.
Demonstration. For if it involved a limited time, which would determine the duration of the thing, then it would follow from the very power alone by which the thing exists that the thing could not exist after that limited time, but would have to be destroyed. But this (by Prop. 4 of this Part) is absurd. Therefore the striving by which a thing exists involves no definite time; on the contrary, since (by the same Prop. 4 of this Part) if it is destroyed by no external cause, it will always continue to exist by the same power by which it now exists, this striving involves an indefinite time.1 Q.E.D.
  1. 1 Compare the definition of duration as ‘the indefinite continuation of existing’ (Part II, Def. 5). The limit of a thing’s duration comes from outside it.

Proposition IX

The mind, both insofar as it has clear and distinct ideas and insofar as it has confused ideas, strives to persevere in its being for an indefinite duration, and is conscious of this striving of its own.

Propositio X

Proposition X

[G II 148] Idea, quae corporis nostri existentiam secludit, in nostra mente dari nequit, sed eidem est contraria.
An idea which excludes the existence of our body cannot be in our mind, but is contrary to it.

Demonstratio. Quicquid corpus nostrum potest destruere, in eodem dari nequit (per prop. 5. huius). Demonstration. Whatever can destroy our body cannot be in it (by Prop. 5 of this Part). And so the idea of that thing cannot be in God insofar as he has the idea of our body (by Cor. of Prop. 9 of Part II); that is (by Props. 11 and 13 of Part II), the idea of that thing cannot be in our mind. On the contrary, since (by Props. 11 and 13 of Part II) the first thing that constitutes the essence of the mind is the idea of a body actually existing, the first and principal thing in the striving of our mind is (by Prop. 7 of this Part) to affirm the existence of our body. And so an idea which denies the existence of our body is contrary to our mind, etc. Q.E.D.

Proposition XI

Whatever increases or diminishes, aids or restrains, the power of acting of our body, the idea of that same thing increases or diminishes, aids or restrains, the power of thinking of our mind.

Proposition XIV

Propositio XV

Proposition XV

Res quaecumque potest esse per accidens causa laetitiae, tristitiae vel cupiditatis.
Any thing can be, accidentally, the cause of joy, sadness or desire.

Cited in 3P16D, 3P36D, 3P50D, 3P52S

Demonstratio. Ponatur mens duobus affectibus simul affici, uno scilicet, qui eius agendi potentiam neque auget neque minuit et altero, qui eandem vel auget vel minuit (vide postul. 1. huius). Demonstration. Let the mind be supposed to be affected by two affects at the same time, one which neither increases nor diminishes its power of acting, and the other which either increases or diminishes it (see Post. 1 of this Part). From the preceding Prop. it is evident that when the mind is afterwards affected by the former through its true cause, which (by hypothesis) of itself neither increases nor diminishes its power of thinking, it will immediately be affected also by the latter, which increases or diminishes its power of thinking, that is (by Schol. of Prop. 11 of this Part), it will be affected with joy or sadness; and so that thing will be the cause of joy or sadness not through itself but accidentally. Demonstration. Therefore the images of things which posit the existence of the thing loved aid the striving of the mind by which it strives to imagine the thing loved, that is (by Schol. of Prop. 11 of this Part), they affect the mind with joy; and those which, on the other hand, exclude the existence of the thing loved restrain that same striving of the mind, that is (by the same Scholium), they affect the mind with sadness. He, therefore, who imagines that what he loves is destroyed will be saddened, etc. Q.E.D.

Propositio XX

Proposition XX

[G II 156] Qui id quod odio habet destrui imaginatur, laetabitur.
He who imagines that what he hates is destroyed will rejoice.

Demonstratio. Mens (per prop. 13. huius) ea imaginari conatur, quae rerum existentiam, quibus corporis agendi potentia minuitur vel coercetur, secludunt, hoc est (per schol. eiusdem prop.) ea imaginari conatur; quae rerum quas odio habet, existentiam secludunt. Demonstration. The mind (by Prop. 13 of this Part) strives to imagine those things which exclude the existence of things by which the body’s power of acting is diminished or restrained, that is (by the Schol. of the same Prop.), it strives to imagine those things which exclude the existence of things it hates. And so the image of a thing which excludes the existence of what the mind hates aids this striving of the mind, that is (by Schol. of Prop. 11 of this Part), it affects the mind with joy. He, therefore, who imagines that what he hates is destroyed will rejoice. Q.E.D.

Propositio XXI

Proposition XXI

Qui id quod amat laetitia vel tristitia affectum imaginatur, laetitia etiam vel tristitia afficietur; et uterque hic affectus maior aut minor erit in amante, prout uterque maior aut minor est in re amata.
He who imagines that what he loves is affected with joy or sadness will also be affected with joy or sadness; and each of these affects will be greater or less in the lover as it is greater or less in the thing loved.

Cited in 3P22D, 3P22S, 3P25D, 3P26D, 3P27C1D, 3P38D, 3P45D

Demonstratio. Rerum imagines (ut in prop. 19. huius demonstravimus) quae rei amatae existentiam ponunt, mentis conatum, quo ipsam rem amatam imaginari conatur, iuvant. Sed laetitia existentiam rei laetae ponit, et eo magis, quo laetitiae affectus maior est; est enim (per schol. prop. 11. huius) transitio ad maiorem perfectionem. Ergo imago laetitiae rei amatae in amante ipsius mentis conatum iuvat, hoc est (per schol. prop. 11. huius) amantem laetitia afficit, et eo maiore quo maior hic affectus in re amata fuerit. Quod erat primum. Deinde quatenus res aliqua tristitia afficitur, eatenus destruitur, et eo magis, quo maiore afficitur tristitia (per idem schol. prop. 11. huius); adeoque (per prop. 19. huius) qui id quod amat tristitia affici imaginatur, tristitia etiam afficietur, et eo maiore quo maior hic affectus in re amata fuerit. Q. E. D.
Demonstration. The images of things which posit the existence of the thing loved (as we have demonstrated in Prop. 19 of this Part) aid the striving of the mind by which it strives to imagine the thing loved itself. But joy posits the existence of the thing that rejoices, and the more so, the greater the affect of joy is; for it is (by Schol. of Prop. 11 of this Part) a passage to a greater perfection. Therefore the image of the joy of the thing loved aids the striving of the lover’s mind, that is (by Schol. of Prop. 11 of this Part), it affects the lover with joy, and the more so, the greater this affect was in the thing loved. This was the first point. Next, insofar as a thing is affected with sadness, it is to that extent destroyed, and the more so, the greater the sadness with which it is affected (by the same Schol. of Prop. 11 of this Part); and so (by Prop. 19 of this Part) he who imagines that what he loves is affected with sadness will also be affected with sadness, and the more so, the greater this affect was in the thing loved. Q.E.D.

Propositio XXV

Proposition XXV

[G II 159] Id omne de nobis deque re amata affirmare conamur, quod nos vel rem amatam laetitia afficere imaginamur; et contra id omne negare, quod nos vel rem amatam tristitia afficere imaginamur.

Demonstratio. Quod rem amatam laetitia vel tristitia afficere imaginamur, id nos laetitia vel tristitia afficit (per prop. 21. huius). Scholium. Next, the joy which arises from a man’s thinking more highly of another than is just is called overestimation; and finally that which arises from his thinking less highly of another than is just, scorn.

Propositio XXVIII

Proposition XXVIII

Id omne, quod ad laetitiam conducere imaginamur, conamur promovere, ut fiat; quod vero eidem repugnare sive ad tristitiam conducere imaginamur, amovere vel destruere conamur.

Deinde si id, quod tristitiae causam esse credimus, hoc est (per schol. prop. 13. huius) si id, quod odio habemus destrui imaginamur, laetabimur (per prop. 20. huius). Adeoque idem (per primam huius partem) conabimur destruere sive (per prop. 13. huius) a nobis amovere, ne ipsum ut praesens contemplemur; quod erat secundum. Ergo id omne, quod ad laetitiam etc. Q. Demonstration. What we imagine leads to joy we strive, as far as we can, to imagine (by Prop. 12 of this Part), that is (by Prop. 17 of Part II), we shall strive, as far as we can, to contemplate it as present, or as actually existing. But the mind’s striving, or power in thinking, is equal to, and simultaneous in nature with, the body’s striving, or power in acting (as follows clearly from Cor. of Prop. 7 and Cor. of Prop. 11 of Part II). Therefore we strive absolutely that it should exist, or (which by Schol. of Prop. 9 of this Part is the same) we want it and aim at it; which was the first point. Next, if we imagine that what we believe to be the cause of sadness, that is (by Schol. of Prop. 13 of this Part), what we hate, is destroyed, we shall rejoice (by Prop. 20 of this Part). And so (by the first part of this Demonstration) we shall strive to destroy it, or (by Prop. 13 of this Part) to remove it from us, so that we do not contemplate it as present; which was the second point. Therefore whatever we imagine leads to joy, etc. Q.E.D.

Propositio XXX

Proposition XXX

[G II 163] Si quis aliquid egit, quod reliquos laetitia afficere imaginatur, is laetitia concomitante idea sui, tamquam causa, afficietur, sive se ipsum cum laetitia contemplabitur. Si contra aliquid egit, quod reliquos tristitia afficere imaginatur, se ipsum cum tristitia contra contemplabitur.
If someone has done something which he imagines affects others with joy, he will be affected with joy accompanied by the idea of himself as cause, or he will contemplate himself with joy. If, on the other hand, he has done something which he imagines affects others with sadness, he will, on the contrary, contemplate himself with sadness.

Demonstration. He who imagines that he affects others with joy or sadness will thereby (by Prop. 27 of this Part) be affected with joy or sadness. But since man (by Props. 19 and 23 of Part II) is conscious of himself through the affections by which he is determined to act, he who has done something which he imagines affects others with joy will be affected with joy together with the consciousness of himself as cause, or he will contemplate himself with joy; and the contrary. Q.E.D.
Scholium. Cum amor (per schol. prop. 13. huius) sit laetitia concomitante idea causae externae, et odium tristitia concomitante etiam idea causae externae, erit ergo haec laetitia et tristitia amoris et odii species. Sed quia amor et odium ad obiecta externa referuntur, ideo hos affectus aliis nominibus significabimus; nempe laetitiam concomitante idea causae externae gloriama et tristitiam huic contrariam pudorem appellabimus; intellige, quando laetitia vel tristitia ex eo oritur, quod homo se laudari vel vituperari credit. Otherwise, joy accompanied by the idea of an internal cause I shall call self-contentment,1 and the sadness contrary to it repentance. Next, because (by Cor. of Prop. 17 of Part II) it can happen that the joy with which someone imagines that he affects others is only imaginary, and (by Prop. 25 of this Part) each strives to imagine of himself whatever he imagines affects him with joy, it can easily happen that the vainglorious man is proud and imagines himself to be pleasing to all when he is a nuisance to all.

Proposition XXXI

Propositio XXXII

Proposition XXXII

[G II 165] Si aliquem re aliqua, qua unus solus potiri potest, gaudere imaginamur, conabimur efficere ne ille illa re potiatur.

Cited in 3P32S, 3DA33Ex, 4P34D

Demonstratio. Ex eo solo, quod aliquem re aliqua gaudere imaginamur, (per prop. 27. huius cum eiusdem coroll. 1.) rem illam amabimus, eaque gaudere cupiemus. At (per hypothesin) huic laetitiae obstare imaginamur, quod ille eadem hac re gaudeat. Ergo (per prop. 28. huius) ne ille eadem potiatur, conabimur. Q. E. D.
Demonstration. From the mere fact that we imagine someone to enjoy some thing (by Prop. 27 of this Part with its Cor. 1), we shall love that thing and desire to enjoy it. But (by hypothesis) we imagine that the fact that he enjoys this same thing stands in the way of this joy. Therefore (by Prop. 28 of this Part) we shall strive that he should not possess it. Q.E.D.
Scholium. Videmus itaque cum hominum natura plerumque ita comparatum, esse ut eorum, quibus male est, misereantur, et quibus bene est, invideant, et (per prop. praeced.) eo maiore odio, quo rem, qua alium potiri imaginantur, magis amant. Videmus deinde, ex eadem naturae humanae proprietate, ex qua sequitur homines esse misericordes, sequi etiam eosdem esse invidos et ambitiosos. We see, then, that the nature of men is generally so constituted that they pity those who fare badly and envy those who fare well, and (by the preceding Prop.) with a hatred the greater, the more they love the thing they imagine another to possess. We see, next, that from the same property of human nature from which it follows that men are compassionate, it also follows that they are envious and ambitious.1 Finally, if we are willing to consult experience itself, we shall find that it teaches all these things, especially if we attend to the first years of our life. For we find that children, because their body is continually, as it were, in equilibrium, laugh or cry simply because they see others laugh or cry; and whatever else they see others do, they immediately desire to imitate it; and finally they desire for themselves all the things in which they imagine others take delight; namely, because, as we have said, the images of things are the very affections of the human body, or modes by which the human body is affected by external causes and disposed to do this or that.

Proposition XXXIII

Cum rem nobis similem amamus, conamur, quantum possumus, efficere ut nos contra amet.

If, therefore, the thing is like us, we shall strive to affect it with joy before others (by Prop. 29 of this Part), or we shall strive, as far as we can, to bring it about that the thing loved is affected with joy accompanied by the idea of us, that is (by Schol. of Prop. 13 of this Part), that it loves us in return. Propositio XXXIV

Proposition XXXIV

[G II 166] Quo maiore affectu rem amatam erga nos affectam esse imaginamur, eo magis gloriabimur.
The greater the affect with which we imagine a thing we love to be affected towards us, the more we shall exult in glory.

Cited in 3P35D, 3P42D, 3P49S

Demonstratio. Nos (per prop. praeced.) conamur, quantum possumus, ut res amata nos contra amet hoc est (per schol. prop. 13. huius) ut res amata laetitia afficiatur concomitante idea nostri. Quo itaque rem amatam maiore laetitia nostra de causa affectam esse imaginamur, eo magis hic conatus iuvatur hoc est (per prop. 11. huius cum eius schol.) eo maiore laetitia afficimur. At cum ex eo laetemur, quod alium nobis similem laetitia affecimus, tum nosmet cum laetitia contemplamur (per prop. 30. huius). Ergo quo maiore affectu rem amatam erga nos affectam esse imaginamur, eo maiore laetitia nosmet contemplabimur, sive (per schol. prop. 30. huius) eo magis gloriabimur. Q. E. D.
Demonstration. We strive (by the preceding Prop.), as far as we can, that the thing loved should love us in return, that is (by Schol. of Prop. 13 of this Part), that the thing loved should be affected with joy accompanied by the idea of us. The greater, therefore, the joy with which we imagine the thing loved to be affected on our account, the more this striving is aided, that is (by Prop. 11 of this Part with its Schol.), the greater the joy with which we are affected. But when we rejoice that we have affected with joy another like us, then we contemplate ourselves with joy (by Prop. 30 of this Part). Therefore the greater the affect with which we imagine the thing loved to be affected towards us, the greater the joy with which we shall contemplate ourselves, or (by Schol. of Prop. 30 of this Part) the more we shall exult in glory. Q.E.D.

Propositio XXXV

Proposition XXXV

Therefore (by Schol. of Prop. 11 of this Part) he will thereby be affected with sadness, accompanied by the idea of the thing loved as cause and at the same time by the image of the other, that is (by Schol. of Prop. 13 of this Part), he will be affected with hatred towards the thing loved, and at the same time towards that other (by Cor. of Prop. 15 of this Part), whom (by Prop. 23 of this Part) he will envy because that other takes delight in the thing loved. Hoc odium erga rem amatam invidiae iunctum zelotypia vocatur, quae proinde nihil aliud est, quam animi fluctuatio orta ex amore et odio simul, concomitante idea alterius, cui invidetur. Praeterea hoc odium erga rem amatam maius erit pro ratione laetitiae, qua zelotypus ex reciproco rei amatae amore solebat affici, et etiam pro ratione affectus quo erga illum, quem sibi rem amatam iungere imaginatur, affectus erat. For if he hated him, he will thereby hate the thing loved (by Prop. 24 of this Part), because he imagines that it affects with joy what he himself hates; and also (by Cor. of Prop. 15 of this Part) from the fact that he is compelled to join the image of the thing loved to the image of him whom he hates; which reason generally has place in love towards a woman. For he who imagines that the woman he loves gives herself to another will not only be saddened because his own appetite is restrained, but will also be averse to her because he is compelled to join the image of the thing loved to the private parts and excretions of the other. To which, finally, is added that the jealous man is not received by the thing loved with the same countenance that it used to show him; for this reason also the lover is saddened, as I shall now show.

Propositio XXXVII

Proposition XXXVII

Cupiditas, quae prae tristitia vel laetitia praeque odio vel amore oritur, eo est maior, quo affectus maior est.
The desire which arises from sadness or joy, and from hatred or love, is the greater, the greater the affect is.

Cited in 3P38D, 3P39D, 3P43D, 3P44D, 4P15D, 4P37D2, 4P44D

Demonstratio. Tristitia hominis agendi potentiam (per schol. prop. 11. huius) minuit vel coercet, hoc est (per prop. 7. huius) conatum quo homo in suo esse perseverare conatur, minuit vel coercet; adeoque (per prop. 5. huius) huic conatui est contraria; et quicquid homo tristitia affectus conatur, est tristitiam amovere. At (per tristitiae defin.) quo tristitia maior est, eo maiori parti hominis agendi potentiae necesse est opponi. Ergo quo maior tristitia est, eo maiore agendi potentia conabitur homo contra tristitiam amovere, hoc est (per schol. prop. 9. huius) eo maiore cupiditate sive appetitu conabitur tristitiam amovere. Deinde quoniam laetitia (per idem schol. prop. 11. huius) hominis agendi potentiam auget vel iuvat, facile eadem via demonstratur, quod homo laetitia affectus nihil aliud cupit, quam eandem conservare, idque eo maiore cupiditate quo laetitia maior erit. Denique quoniam odium et amor sunt ipsi tristitiae vel laetitiae affectus,a sequitur eodem modo, quod conatus, appetitus sive cupiditas, quae prae odio vel amore oritur, maior erit pro ratione odii et amoris. Q. E. D.
Demonstration. Sadness diminishes or restrains a man’s power of acting (by Schol. of Prop. 11 of this Part), that is (by Prop. 7 of this Part), it diminishes or restrains the striving by which a man strives to persevere in his being; and so (by Prop. 5 of this Part) it is contrary to this striving; and whatever a man affected with sadness strives for is to remove the sadness. But (by the definition of sadness) the greater the sadness is, the greater the part of the man’s power of acting to which it is necessarily opposed. Therefore the greater the sadness is, the greater the power of acting with which the man will strive, on the other hand, to remove the sadness, that is (by Schol. of Prop. 9 of this Part), the greater the desire, or appetite, with which he will strive to remove the sadness. Next, since joy (by the same Schol. of Prop. 11 of this Part) increases or aids a man’s power of acting, it is easily demonstrated in the same way that a man affected with joy desires nothing but to preserve it, and that with a desire the greater, the greater the joy will be. Finally, since hatred and love are themselves affects of sadness or joy, it follows in the same way that the striving, appetite or desire which arises from hatred or love will be greater in proportion to the hatred and love. Q.E.D.

Propositio XXXIX

Proposition XXXIX

Scholium. Proposition LII

The Definitions of the Affects

Part III ends by going over its ground a second time. The affects have been defined one by one in the scholia, as the demonstrations reached them: joy and sadness in the scholium to Prop. 11, love and hatred in that to Prop. 13, hope and fear in the second scholium to Prop. 18, and so on through envy, ambition, glory and jealousy. Spinoza now repeats the definitions “in order”, forty-eight of them, and adds to each what needs to be observed about it. A General Definition of the affect that is a passion of the mind closes the Part. Because the definitions follow a classification of their own, and because several of them correct what the scholia said, they are best read with their plan and their changes in view.

The decision to do so is announced at the end of the scholium to Prop. 59: “Finally, some things must be noted about the definitions of the affects, which I shall therefore repeat here in order, inserting among them what is to be observed in each.” Two motives seem to be at work. The first is practical. The affects were defined in the order in which the demonstrations reached them, scattered through fifty-nine propositions, and a reader who wants to know what Spinoza means by envy or by humility needs to find the definition quickly; Part IV, which judges the affects one by one, needs a list of terms to refer to. The second is theoretical. The propositions derived the affects from their causes; the definitions now classify them by their nature, and the classification shows that the derivation is complete, that every affect the language names can be placed by its relation to desire, joy and sadness.

The order of the definitions

The order is not that of the propositions but of a classification. The three primary affects come first: desire, joy and sadness (Defs. 1–3). Wonder and contempt follow (4–5); wonder, Spinoza explains, is not an affect at all, and is mentioned only because usage gives other names to affects derived from the primary ones when they are related to objects we wonder at. Then come the affects of joy and sadness with an external cause: love and hatred, and their species (6–11); those that concern a doubtful or a decided outcome, hope, fear, confidence, despair, gladness and remorse (12–17); those that arise from the good or ill of others, pity, favour and indignation, overestimation and scorn, envy and compassion (18–24). Next are the joys and sadnesses whose idea is of an internal cause, oneself: self-contentment and humility, repentance, pride and self-abasement, glory and shame (25–31). The rest are kinds of desire, from longing and emulation to anger, vengeance and cruelty, timidity, daring and cowardice, and finally courtesy, ambition, gluttony, drunkenness, greed and lust (32–48). Each affect is placed by its relation to the three primary ones and by the kind of object or idea that accompanies it, as the scholium to Prop. 56 said it must be: the affects have as many species as there are species of objects, and a general definition of each is enough for the purpose of the work.

Earlier catalogues

A catalogue of the passions with short definitions was not a new genre. Descartes’ Passions of the Soul had derived a long list of particular passions from its six primitive ones, and Hobbes, in the sixth chapter of Leviathan (1651), had defined the passions one after another in a few words each: “For Appetite with an opinion of attaining, is called HOPE.” Spinoza’s definitions resemble Hobbes’s in their brevity and in their reduction of the passions to a few elementary motions; they differ from both predecessors in what the elements are and in the use made of the list. For Descartes, the classification of the passions served a doctrine of how the free will can govern them. For Spinoza it serves a doctrine of how understanding transforms them.

The difference shows clearly in a single word. The virtue Descartes valued above all he called générosité, which “makes a man esteem himself as highly as he can legitimately esteem himself,” and consists partly in knowing that nothing truly belongs to him “but this free disposition of his volitions,” and partly in a firm and constant resolution to use it well (Passions, art. 153). Spinoza takes over the Latin form of the word, generositas, and gives it an entirely different sense: “the desire by which each strives, from the dictate of reason alone, to aid other men and join them to himself in friendship” (Prop. 59, Schol.). The Cartesian virtue is a relation of the self to its own free will; the Spinozist one is a desire, born of reason, directed at others. The translation renders Spinoza’s word as “nobility”, which keeps the two apart.

Corrections and sharpenings

The repetition is not mechanical. Several definitions are corrected or sharpened. The first is the most important. The scholium to Prop. 9 had defined desire as “appetite together with consciousness of it”. The new definition reads: “Desire is the very essence of man insofar as it is conceived to be determined, from any given affection of it, to do something.” The explanation gives two reasons for the change. Since there is no real difference between appetite and desire, whether or not a man is conscious of his appetite, defining one by the other would be a tautology; and a definition was wanted that would comprehend all the strivings of human nature, which are called appetite, will, desire or impulse. And the phrase “from any given affection of it” is added so that the definition contains the cause of the consciousness: a man is conscious of himself only through the affections of his body (Part II, Prop. 23). In the same way, joy is now said to be the passage to a greater perfection, not the perfection itself: if a man were born with the perfection to which he passes, he would possess it without the affect of joy. Love is defined again as joy accompanied by the idea of an external cause, and Spinoza takes the opportunity to reject the definition of love as the lover’s will to join himself to the thing loved, which states a property of love and not its essence (Def. 6, Expl.).

New distinctions

Other explanations add distinctions that the propositions passed over. Repentance is now defined as sadness accompanied by the idea of some deed which we believe we have done from a free decree of the mind; its intensity comes from the belief in freedom. Pride is distinguished from overestimation, and the customary opposition of pride and humility is examined (Defs. 28–29). Pity (commiseratio) is said to differ from misericordia only in that one regards the single affect and the other the habit; misericordia is then redefined, ‘against the meaning of the word’, as the love that rejoices in another’s good, the opposite of envy (Defs. 18 and 24). Spinoza also admits where the Latin language fails him. Some affects have no names, others have names that are used loosely; and he warns, once for all, that his purpose is to explain not the meaning of words but the nature of things, and to indicate them by words whose ordinary meaning is not wholly at odds with the one he intends (Def. 20, Expl.).

What the explanations observe

The explanations are also where Spinoza allows himself remarks of a kind the propositions rarely contain, observations about how the affects behave and about how the words for them are used. Several are worth reading for their own sake.

Wonder is excluded from the affects because the imagination of a new thing, “considered in itself, is of the same nature as the others”; the mind is held in the contemplation of it only because it is not yet linked to other images, so that nothing leads the mind on to something else (Def. Aff. 4, Expl.). Wonder is thus a gap in the chain of association, not a positive feeling, and Spinoza keeps the word only because usage gives special names to the affects derived from the primary ones when their objects are things we wonder at, as devotion is “love towards one whom we wonder at” (Def. Aff. 10).

Hope and fear are shown to be inseparable. “There is no hope without fear, nor fear without hope.” He who hangs on hope and is in doubt about the outcome imagines something that excludes the thing he hopes for, and so to that extent is saddened and fears; he who fears imagines something that excludes the thing he hates, and so rejoices and hopes (Def. Aff. 13, Expl.). The point will matter in Part IV, where hope and fear are both judged to be signs of a mind lacking in knowledge.

Repentance and its opposite are traced, in part, to upbringing. It is not surprising, the explanation of Def. 27 says, that acts which custom calls wrong are followed by sadness and those it calls right by joy, since “this depends chiefly on education”: parents, by reproaching the one and praising the other, have joined sadness to the first and joy to the second (Def. Aff. 27, Expl.). The observation is brief, but it contains a whole account of moral feeling as learned association.

The explanation of ambition observes that it is “a desire by which all the affects ... are fostered and strengthened; and for this reason this affect can hardly be overcome,” and quotes Cicero: “Every best man … is led most by glory. Even the philosophers inscribe their names on the books which they write on despising glory” (Def. Aff. 44, Expl.). And the last explanation notes that the five affects of immoderate appetite, ambition, gluttony, drunkenness, greed and lust, have no contraries among the affects: temperance, sobriety and chastity “indicate a power of the mind, not a passion,” and a greedy or timid man who abstains from food or drink is not on that account moved by an affect contrary to gluttony (Def. Aff. 48, Expl.).

The General Definition

The General Definition gathers the account into a single formula: “An affect, which is called a passion of the mind, is a confused idea by which the mind affirms of its body, or of some part of it, a greater or lesser force of existing than before, and by which, when it is given, the mind itself is determined to think of this rather than of that” (General Definition). The Latin for “passion of the mind” is animi pathema, a Greek word in Latin dress. Each phrase is explained. It is a confused idea, because the mind is acted on only insofar as it has inadequate ideas (Prop. 3). It affirms of the body a greater or lesser force of existing, because the mind is the idea of the body and the affect expresses a change in the body’s power of acting. “Than before” does not mean that the mind compares its present state with its past one; it means that the idea which constitutes the affect affirms something of the body that involves more or less reality than before. And the last clause adds the desire that every affect brings with it.

The bridge to Part IV

The catalogue is the bridge to Part IV. There Spinoza will ask how strong the affects are, and which of them agree with reason and which are opposed to it: pity is bad in one who lives by reason (Part IV, Prop. 50), humility and repentance are not virtues (Props. 53–54), cheerfulness cannot be excessive (Prop. 42), hatred can never be good (Prop. 45). That inquiry presupposes what the definitions provide, a complete map of the affects reduced to three, each with its definite cause. The derivations of Part III were offered as explanations of human life as it is; the definitions fix the terms in which Part IV will judge it.

As the text continues, the definitions can be read straight through or kept at hand for reference. The explanations attached to them are where the corrections and the new distinctions are made, and they repay closer attention than the definitions themselves.

I pass over in silence the definitions of jealousy and the other fluctuations of mind, both because they arise from the composition of affects we have already defined and because most of them have no names, which shows that for the conduct of life it is enough to know them only in general. Affectuum Generalis Definitio

General Definition of the Affects

[G II 203] Affectus, qui animi pathema dicitur, est confusa idea, qua mens maiorem vel minorem sui corporis vel alicuius eius partis existendi vim, quam antea, affirmat, et qua data ipsa mens ad hoc potius, quam ad illud cogitandum determinatur.
An affect, which is called a passion of the mind,1 is a confused idea by which the mind affirms of its body, or of some part of it, a greater or lesser force of existing than before, and by which, when it is given, the mind itself is determined to think of this rather than of that.
  1. 1 animi pathema: from the Greek πάθημα (‘something undergone’). The General Definition defines only the affect that is a passion, from the side of the mind; the active affects (Props. 58–59) arise from adequate ideas. Its first part gives the nature of joy and sadness, the last clause that of desire (Explanation, last sentence).

[G II 204] Explicatio. Dico primo affectum seu passionem animi esse “confusam ideam”. Nam mentem eatenus tantum pati ostendimus (vide prop. 3 huius), quatenus ideas inadaequatas sive confusas habet. Dico deinde “qua mens maiorem vel minorem sui corporis vel alicuius eius partis existendi vim quam antea affirmat”. Omnes enim corporum ideae, quas habemus, magis nostri corporis actualem constitutionem (per coroll. 2. prop. 16. P. 2.), quam corporis externi naturam indicant; at haec, quae affectus formam constituit, corporis vel alicuius eius partis constitutionem indicare vel exprimere debet, quam ipsum corpus vel aliqua eius pars habet, ex eo, quod ipsius agendi potentia sive existendi vis augetur vel minuitur, iuvatur vel coercetur.
Explanation. I say first that an affect, or passion of the mind, is ‘a confused idea’. For we have shown that the mind is acted on only insofar as it has inadequate, or confused, ideas (see Prop. 3 of this Part). I say next ‘by which the mind affirms of its body, or of some part of it, a greater or lesser force of existing than before’. For all the ideas of bodies which we have indicate the actual constitution of our body (by Cor. 2 of Prop. 16 of Part II) more than the nature of the external body; but this idea, which constitutes the form of the affect, must indicate or express the constitution of the body, or of some part of it, which the body itself, or some part of it, has because its power of acting, or force of existing, is increased or diminished, aided or restrained.
Sed notandum cum dico “maiorem vel minorem existendi vim, quam antea”, me non intelligere, quod mens praesentem corporis constitutionem cum praeterita comparat, sed quod idea, quae affectus formam constituit, aliquid de corpore affirmat, quod plus minusve realitatis revera involvit, quam antea. Et quia essentia mentis in hoc consistit (per prop. 11. et 13. P. 2.), quod sui corporis actualem existentiam affirmat, et nos per perfectionem ipsam rei essentiam intelligimus; sequitur ergo, quod mens ad maiorem minoremve perfectionem transit, quando ei aliquid de suo corpore vel aliqua eius parte affirmare contingit, quod plus minusve realitatis involvit, quam antea. Cum igitur supra dixerim mentis cogitandi potentiam augeri vel minui, nihil aliud intelligere volui, quam quod mens ideam sui corporis vel alicuius eius partis formaverit, quae plus minusve realitatis exprimit, quam de suo corpore affirmaverat. Nam idearum praestantia et actualis cogitandi potentia ex obiecti praestantia aestimatur. Addidi denique “et qua data ipsa mens ad hoc potius quam ad aliud cogitandum determinatur”,a ut praeter laetitiae et tristitiae naturam, quam prima definitionis pars explicat, cupiditatis etiam naturam exprimerem.
But it should be noted that when I say ‘a greater or lesser force of existing than before’, I do not understand that the mind compares the present constitution of the body with a past one,1 but that the idea which constitutes the form of the affect affirms something of the body which really involves more or less reality than before. And because the essence of the mind consists in this (by Props. 11 and 13 of Part II), that it affirms the actual existence of its body, and we understand by perfection the very essence of a thing, it follows that the mind passes to a greater or lesser perfection when it happens to affirm something of its body, or of some part of it, which involves more or less reality than before. When, therefore, I said above that the mind’s power of thinking is increased or diminished, I wanted to understand nothing but that the mind has formed an idea of its body, or of some part of it, which expresses more or less reality than it had affirmed of its body. For the excellence of ideas and the actual power of thinking are estimated from the excellence of the object.2 I added, finally, ‘and by which, when it is given, the mind itself is determined to think of this rather than of anything else’, so as to express, besides the nature of joy and sadness, which the first part of the definition explains, the nature of desire as well.
