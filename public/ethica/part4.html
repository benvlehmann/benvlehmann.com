Ethices Pars Quarta
De Servitute Humana seu de Affectuum Viribus

Fourth Part of the Ethics
Of Human Bondage, or of the Powers of the Affects

Introduction to the Fourth Part

The fourth Part is called De servitute humana, seu de affectuum viribus, “Of Human Bondage, or of the Powers of the Affects.” Its first sentence defines the bondage: “Man’s lack of power in moderating and restraining the affects I call bondage.” A man subject to the affects is not under his own control (sui iuris) but under that of fortune, so that he is often compelled, “although he sees the better for himself, still to follow the worse” (Preface). The words are Medea’s in Ovid, already quoted in the scholium to Part III, Prop. 2, and they state the problem of the Part. Part III explained how the affects arise; Part IV asks how strong they are, why knowledge of the good is so often powerless against them, and what in them is good or bad. Part V will then show what the mind can do against them.

Part IV is the longest Part of the Ethics, and in some ways the most varied. It contains a theory of value, an analysis of the weakness of reason, an ethics of self-preservation that turns out to be an ethics of community, a brief political philosophy, a judgment on each of the principal affects, the portrait of a free man, and an Appendix that restates the whole in continuous prose. Readers who come to it looking for the ethics promised by the title of the book find much of it here. But they should be prepared for an ethics unlike most others: one without free will, without obligation in the usual sense, without any good independent of human striving, and yet with a definite and demanding picture of how to live.

The Preface: perfection, good and evil

Before beginning, the Preface does something that might seem to undo the Appendix to Part I. There Spinoza had argued that good and bad, perfect and imperfect, order and confusion are notions men form from their own appetites and project onto nature. The Preface agrees, and gives a history of the words. “If someone has decided to make something and has finished it, he will call his thing perfect”; a house is called imperfect while it is still being built, and perfect when it has been brought to the end its author intended. That seems to have been the “first meaning” of the words. But once men began to form universal ideas and to devise models of houses, buildings and towers, each called perfect what agreed with the universal idea he had formed, and imperfect what agreed less with it, even when the builder thought it finished. Men then applied the same models to natural things, believing that nature, “which they think does nothing except for the sake of some end,” looks to these models and sets them before itself (Preface).

But nature does not act for an end. “That eternal and infinite being which we call God, or nature, acts by the same necessity by which it exists”; as it exists for the sake of no end, so it acts for the sake of no end. What is called a final cause is nothing but a human appetite whose causes are unknown, as habitation is the final cause of a house only in the sense that a man, imagining the advantages of domestic life, had an appetite to build one. Perfection and imperfection are therefore “only modes of thinking,” notions we form by comparing individuals of the same kind with one another. Good and evil likewise “indicate nothing positive in things, considered in themselves, and are nothing but modes of thinking, or notions, which we form from the fact that we compare things with one another.” One and the same thing can be good, bad and indifferent at once: “music is good for one who is melancholy, bad for one who mourns, and neither good nor bad for one who is deaf” (Preface).

Yet Spinoza decides to keep the words. “For because we desire to form an idea of man, as a model of human nature which we may look to, it will be useful to us to retain these same words in the sense I have indicated.” Good will mean “that which we certainly know to be a means of approaching nearer and nearer to the model of human nature which we set before ourselves,” and evil what we certainly know prevents us from resembling it; and men will be called more or less perfect as they come nearer to the model or are further from it. Perfection, finally, is not a change from one essence to another, “for a horse, for example, is destroyed as much if it is changed into a man as if into an insect,” but an increase or decrease in a thing’s power of acting. By perfection in general Spinoza understands reality, “the essence of any thing insofar as it exists and operates in a certain way, without any regard to its duration”; no thing is more perfect because it lasts longer.

The Preface is short, but it resolves a tension the reader may have felt since Part I. If good and evil are only modes of thinking, how can there be an ethics? Spinoza’s answer is that ethics does not need goods that exist in nature independently of us. It needs only a model of human nature, an idea of a human being whose power of acting is greater than ours, and the knowledge of what brings us nearer to it. The model is not an end that nature pursues. It is an idea we form, and use, because we compare, and because the comparison is useful.

Definitions and the single axiom

The definitions translate these notions into the vocabulary of utility. “By good I shall understand that which we certainly know to be useful to us” (Def. 1), and by evil “that which we certainly know prevents us from possessing some good” (Def. 2). The word “certainly” matters: Part IV is concerned with what reason knows to be good, not with what the passions take to be so. Two definitions bring back, with explicit epistemic definitions, the words “contingent” and “possible” that Part I had banished from nature. Singular things are called contingent “insofar as, when we attend only to their essence, we find nothing which necessarily posits their existence or which necessarily excludes it,” and possible “insofar as, when we attend to the causes from which they must be produced, we do not know whether these are determined to produce them” (Defs. 3–4). The definitions do not reintroduce contingency into things; they name ways in which a mind ignorant of the whole order of causes must think of them. “By the end for the sake of which we do something I understand appetite” (Def. 7). And “by virtue and power I understand the same thing,” so that virtue, insofar as it is related to man, is “the very essence, or nature, of man insofar as he has the power of bringing about certain things which can be understood through the laws of his nature alone” (Def. 8). Virtue is not obedience, and not a disposition to follow rules; it is power, the power of acting from one’s own nature.

The Part has a single axiom, and it is grim: “There is no singular thing in the nature of things than which there is not another more powerful and stronger. But given any one, there is another more powerful by which the given one can be destroyed” (Axiom). Everything finite can be destroyed by something else, and the first group of propositions draws the consequences for human beings.

Human weakness

“We are acted on insofar as we are a part of nature which cannot be conceived through itself without others” (Prop. 2). “The force by which a man perseveres in existing is limited, and is infinitely surpassed by the power of external causes” (Prop. 3). It cannot happen that a man should not be a part of nature and should undergo no changes except those of which he is the adequate cause (Prop. 4), and so “man is necessarily always subject to passions” (Prop. 4, Cor.). The force of a passion is defined not by our own power but by the power of its external cause compared with ours (Prop. 5), and it can surpass all our other actions, “so that the affect stubbornly clings to the man” (Prop. 6). From this comes the principle on which the rest of the argument turns: “An affect can be neither restrained nor removed except by an affect contrary to it and stronger than the affect to be restrained” (Prop. 7).

The next propositions explain why knowledge is so often defeated. “The knowledge of good and evil is nothing but the affect of joy or sadness, insofar as we are conscious of it” (Prop. 8). It follows that “the true knowledge of good and evil, insofar as it is true, cannot restrain any affect, but only insofar as it is considered as an affect” (Prop. 14). Knowledge does not stand outside the affects and command them; it can act on them only by being itself an affect, and then it is subject to the same laws of strength and weakness as any other. A desire that arises from true knowledge of good and evil can be extinguished by many other desires (Prop. 15), especially by desires for things pleasant in the present when the knowledge concerns the future (Prop. 16), and still more easily when it concerns things we regard as contingent (Prop. 17). The intermediate propositions on affects toward future, past, necessary and contingent things (Props. 9–13) supply the mechanism: an image of something present is stronger than an image of something future, and an image of something necessary stronger than one of something contingent.

The scholium to Prop. 17 draws the moral and quotes two witnesses. “With these I believe I have shown the cause why men are moved more by opinion than by true reason.” Hence the poet’s saying, “I see and approve the better, I follow the worse,” and Ecclesiastes: “He who increases knowledge increases sorrow.” Spinoza adds at once that he does not say this in order to conclude that ignorance is better than knowledge. “It is necessary to know both the power and the lack of power of our nature, so that we can determine what reason can do in moderating the affects and what it cannot do” (Prop. 17, Schol.). The analysis of weakness is the first step toward strength.

The dictates of reason

Then the tone changes. The scholium to Prop. 18 sets out “the dictates of reason” in advance of their demonstration, “so that what I think may be more easily grasped by everyone.” “Since reason demands nothing contrary to nature, it demands, therefore, that each man love himself, seek his own advantage, what is really useful to him, and want everything which really leads man to a greater perfection, and, absolutely, that each strive to preserve his own being as far as it is in him.” Since virtue is acting from the laws of one’s own nature, “the foundation of virtue is the very striving to preserve one’s own being, and ... happiness consists in a man’s being able to preserve his being.” Virtue is to be wanted for its own sake, and there is nothing more excellent for whose sake it ought to be wanted. Those who kill themselves “lack power of mind and are completely conquered by external causes opposed to their nature.”

The scholium then turns from the individual to others. We can never bring it about that we need nothing outside ourselves, and of the things outside us none is more useful than those that agree entirely with our nature. “To man, therefore, nothing is more useful than man; nothing, I say, can men wish for that is more excellent for preserving their being than that all should so agree in all things that the minds and bodies of all should compose, as it were, one mind and one body.” From this it follows that men who are governed by reason “want nothing for themselves which they do not desire for other men.” Spinoza explains that he has set out these dictates in advance because some believe that the principle that everyone is bound to seek his own advantage is the foundation of impiety, not of virtue and piety, and he wants to show briefly that the contrary is true.

Props. 19–37 demonstrate these claims. “The more each one strives, and is able, to seek his own advantage, that is, to preserve his being, the more he is endowed with virtue” (Prop. 20). “The striving to preserve oneself is the first and only foundation of virtue” (Prop. 22, Cor.). To act absolutely from virtue is “to act, to live, to preserve our being (these three signify the same) from the guidance of reason, and that from the foundation of seeking one’s own advantage” (Prop. 24). Reason then defines what our advantage really is. “Whatever we strive for from reason is nothing but understanding” (Prop. 26), and “we know nothing to be certainly good or evil except what really leads to understanding, or what can prevent us from understanding” (Prop. 27). And so: “The highest good of the mind is the knowledge of God, and the highest virtue of the mind is to know God” (Prop. 28).

Agreement and the state

The ethics of self-preservation becomes an ethics of community through the notion of agreement in nature. “Insofar as a thing agrees with our nature, to that extent it is necessarily good” (Prop. 31). Insofar as men are subject to passions they do not agree in nature, and can be contrary to one another (Props. 32–34). “Only insofar as men live from the guidance of reason do they always necessarily agree in nature” (Prop. 35), and so “there is no singular thing in the nature of things which is more useful to man than a man who lives from the guidance of reason” (Prop. 35, Cor. 1). The paradox is stated in the second corollary: “When each man most seeks his own advantage for himself, then men are most useful to one another” (Prop. 35, Cor. 2). The scholium appeals to experience, which testifies so often to this truth “that it is in almost everyone’s mouth that man is a God to man.” Men rarely live by reason, and are for the most part envious and troublesome to one another; but they can hardly lead a solitary life, and far more benefits than harms arise from their common society. “Let the satirists, then, laugh at human affairs as much as they like, let the theologians detest them, and let the melancholy praise as much as they can an uncultivated and rustic life, and despise men and admire the beasts”; men will still find that they can provide what they need much more easily by mutual help (Prop. 35, Schol.).

“The highest good of those who follow virtue is common to all, and all can enjoy it equally” (Prop. 36), since the knowledge of God is not diminished by being shared. “The good which each one who follows virtue wants for himself he will also desire for other men, and the more so, the greater the knowledge of God he has” (Prop. 37). The first scholium to Prop. 37 defines religion, piety and honourableness in these terms: whatever we desire and do insofar as we have the idea of God is religion; “the desire to do good which is generated in us by our living from the guidance of reason I call piety”; the desire to join others to oneself in friendship is honourableness. It also contains one of the most uncomfortable sentences in the book: the principle of seeking our own advantage teaches us to form close ties with men, but not with beasts, so that “the law against slaughtering beasts is founded more on empty superstition and womanish compassion than on sound reason” (Prop. 37, Schol. 1).

The second scholium sketches a political philosophy. “Each one exists by the highest right of nature,” and so by the highest right of nature each judges what is good and evil, looks to his own advantage, and avenges himself. If men lived by reason, each could exercise this right without harm to others. But because they are subject to affects that far surpass their power, they are drawn in different directions while they need one another’s help, and so, in order to live harmoniously, they must give up their natural right and make one another confident that they will do nothing to harm another (Prop. 37, Schol. 2). This is done by the principle of Prop. 7: an affect is restrained only by a stronger affect, and so society can be established if it claims for itself the right of avenging injuries and of judging good and evil, and has the power to make laws backed by threats. In such a civil state, and only there, sin, merit, justice and injustice have a meaning: sin is disobedience punished by the right of the state, merit is obedience, and justice and injustice concern what common consent has decided belongs to each. “From which it is evident that just and unjust, sin and merit, are extrinsic notions, and not attributes which explain the nature of the mind.” The theory is close to Hobbes’s in its starting point, the natural right of each and the fear that makes men give it up. Its conclusion, which the Theological-Political Treatise develops, is different: “the end of the state, therefore, is really freedom” (Finis ergo Reipublicae revera libertas est; TTP ch. 20).

The evaluation of the affects

The rest of the Part evaluates. What disposes the human body to be affected in many ways and to affect other bodies in many ways is useful (Prop. 38), and what preserves the ratio of motion and rest among its parts is good (Prop. 39). “Joy is not directly evil, but good; sadness, on the other hand, is directly evil” (Prop. 41). “Cheerfulness cannot be excessive, but is always good” (Prop. 42); pleasure, which affects one part of the body more than the rest, can be excessive (Prop. 43). Love and desire can be excessive too, when they are attached to one object to the exclusion of others. “Hatred can never be good” (Prop. 45), and “he who lives from the guidance of reason strives as far as he can to repay another’s hatred, anger, contempt, etc., towards him with love in return, or nobility” (Prop. 46).

The judgments on the affects that ordinary morality admires are severe. “The affects of hope and fear cannot be good in themselves” (Prop. 47). “Pity, in a man who lives from the guidance of reason, is in itself evil and useless” (Prop. 50), though Spinoza adds that “he who is moved neither by reason nor by pity to help others is rightly called inhuman” (Prop. 50, Schol.). “Humility is not a virtue, or does not arise from reason” (Prop. 53). “Repentance is not a virtue, or does not arise from reason; but he who repents of what he has done is twice wretched, or lacking in power” (Prop. 54). Against these stands the self-contentment that arises from reason, which “is the greatest there can be” (Prop. 52), and its scholium calls it “the highest thing we can hope for.”

The judgments are tempered by a realism that is easy to miss. The scholium to Prop. 54 grants that since men rarely live by reason, humility and repentance, and hope and fear as well, “bring more advantage than harm; and so, since men must err, it is better that they err in that direction. For if men lacking in power of mind were all equally proud, were ashamed of nothing and feared nothing, by what bonds could they be united and held together? The mob is a terror, unless it is afraid.” It is no wonder, Spinoza adds, that the prophets, who looked to the common advantage and not to that of a few, commended humility, repentance and reverence so greatly (Prop. 54, Schol.). The ethics of reason is for those who can live by it; for the rest, Spinoza allows, the passions that ordinary morality cultivates do useful work.

Reason as a guide to action

The last group of propositions before the free man shows that reason can do everything the passions do, and better. “To all the actions to which we are determined from an affect which is a passion, we can be determined by reason without it” (Prop. 59). “He who is led by fear, and does good in order to avoid evil, is not led by reason” (Prop. 63), and “by a desire which arises from reason we follow the good directly and flee evil indirectly” (Prop. 63, Cor.). “The knowledge of evil is inadequate knowledge” (Prop. 64). Reason prefers a greater future good to a lesser present one, and a lesser present evil to a greater future one (Prop. 66), since it regards things under a species of eternity and is not affected more by the image of a present thing than by that of a future one. The scholium to Prop. 66 contrasts the man led by affect or opinion, who “whether he will or not, does the things of which he is most ignorant,” with the man led by reason, who “complies with no one’s wishes but his own”; Spinoza calls the first a slave, the second free.

The free man and the Appendix

The last seven propositions describe the free man, and an essay before them introduces his portrait. An Appendix of thirty-two chapters follows, in which Spinoza collects what the Part has taught about the right way of living, which had been demonstrated piecemeal, and brings it under main headings in continuous prose.

Questions the reader will meet

Part IV raises more objections from first-time readers than any other part of the book, and some of them deserve an answer in advance.

The first is the charge of egoism. Spinoza says that virtue is seeking one’s own advantage, and that no one strives to preserve his being for the sake of another. But the advantage reason seeks is understanding, and the highest good is the knowledge of God, which is common to all and diminished by no one’s possession of it. The man who lives by reason wants for others what he wants for himself, not as a sacrifice but because the reason that guides him is the same in them. Whether this is an ethics of enlightened self-interest or a genuine overcoming of the opposition between self and others is a question readers have answered differently, but it is not the crude egoism the first sentences of the scholium to Prop. 18 may suggest.

The second concerns obligation. There is no free will, and good and evil are modes of thinking; how then can Spinoza say what we “ought” to do? When Spinoza speaks of the “dictates” of reason he means what reason prescribes in the sense of what follows from the nature of a being that understands. The ethics of Part IV describes what a more powerful human being does, and what brings us nearer to being one. It addresses readers as beings who can understand, and whose understanding, once acquired, will act in them as an affect.

The third concerns the model of human nature. The Preface introduces it as an idea we form because we compare things, and Part I attacked the projection of models onto nature. Is the model of Part IV a relapse into the teleology of the Appendix? Spinoza’s answer is that the model is a human idea, used by human beings, and that he never claims that nature aims at it. It is useful because the mind that has it is moved by it, and because it measures what really increases our power.

The fourth concerns the strength of reason. Part IV says that true knowledge as such cannot restrain any affect, and that men are necessarily always subject to passions. How then can reason guide life at all? The answer is that knowledge is not only true but also, in us, an affect, and that affects arising from reason are related to what is common to things and so are always present to the mind, while the passions depend on what is changing and absent. Part V will develop this answer into its remedies. Part IV prepares it by showing what reason would choose if it were strong enough.

The place of Part IV in the whole

Part IV is the practical centre of the Ethics. It takes the theory of the affects from Part III and turns it into an account of how to live; it takes the theory of reason from Part II and turns it into a picture of the free man; and it takes the metaphysics of Part I, with its denial of final causes and of free will, and shows that an ethics can be built without either. Part V will add what Part IV leaves open: what the mind can actually do against the passions, and in what the freedom and blessedness of the mind consist. The closing scholium of Part IV already points there, referring everything said about the true freedom of man to strength of character, to tenacity and nobility.

The text begins with the Preface on perfection and imperfection, good and evil. It is one of Spinoza’s clearest pieces of prose, and the definitions and the single axiom that follow it should be read in its light.

So habitation, insofar as it is considered as a final cause, is nothing but this singular appetite, which is really an efficient cause, which is considered as first because men are commonly ignorant of the causes of their appetites. For they are, as I have often said, conscious of their actions and appetites, but ignorant of the causes by which they are determined to want something. As for what they commonly say, that nature sometimes fails or errs and produces imperfect things, I number this among the fictions which I treated in the Appendix of Part I.

Perfectio igitur et imperfectio revera modi solummodo cogitandi sunt, nempe notiones, quas fingere solemus ex eo, quod eiusdem speciei aut generis individua ad invicem comparamus. Et hac de causa supra (def. 6. Quatenus itaque naturae individua ad hoc genus revocamus, et ad invicem comparamus, et alia plus entitatis seu realitatis, quam alia habere comperimus, eatenus alia aliis perfectiora esse dicimus; et quatenus iisdem aliquid tribuimus, quod negationem involvit, ut terminus, finis, impotentia etc., eatenus ipsa imperfecta appellamus,c quia nostram mentem non aeque afficiunt, ac illa, quae perfecta vocamus, et non quod ipsis aliquid, quod suum sit, deficiat vel quod natura peccaverit. Nihil enim naturae alicuius rei competit, nisi id quod ex necessitate naturae causae efficientis sequitur; et quicquid ex necessitate naturae causae efficientis sequitur, id necessario fit.
Perfection and imperfection, therefore, are in truth only modes of thinking, namely notions which we are accustomed to feign from the fact that we compare individuals of the same species or genus with one another. And for this reason I said above (Def. 6 of Part II) that by reality and perfection I understand the same thing; for we are accustomed to refer all the individuals of nature to one genus, which is called the most general, namely to the notion of being,3 which pertains absolutely to all the individuals of nature. Insofar, therefore, as we refer the individuals of nature to this genus, and compare them with one another, and find that some have more being, or reality, than others, to that extent we say that some are more perfect than others; and insofar as we attribute to them something which involves negation, such as limit, end, lack of power, etc., to that extent we call them imperfect, because they do not affect our mind as much as those which we call perfect, and not because they lack something which is theirs, or because nature has erred. For nothing belongs to the nature of any thing except what follows from the necessity of the nature of the efficient cause; and whatever follows from the necessity of the nature of the efficient cause necessarily happens.

For it should be especially noted that when I say that someone passes from a lesser to a greater perfection, and the contrary, I do not understand that he is changed from one essence, or form, into another (for a horse, for example, is destroyed as much if it is changed into a man as if into an insect); but that we conceive his power of acting, insofar as it is understood through his nature, to be increased or diminished.2 Finally, by perfection in general I shall understand reality, as I have said, that is, the essence of any thing insofar as it exists and operates in a certain way, without any regard to its duration. For no singular thing can be called more perfect because it has persevered in existing for a longer time; for the duration of things cannot be determined from their essence, since the essence of things involves no certain and determinate time of existing; but any thing whatever, whether it is more perfect or less, will always be able to persevere in existing by the same force by which it begins to exist, so that in this respect all are equal.

Propositio I

Proposition I

[G II 211] Nihil, quod idea falsa positivum habet, tollitur praesentia veri, quatenus verum.
Nothing positive which a false idea has is removed by the presence of the true, insofar as it is true.

Cited in 4P14D

Demonstratio. Falsitas in sola privatione cognitionis, quam ideae inadaequatae involvunt, consistit (per prop. 35. P. 2.), nec ipsae aliquid habent positivum, propter quod falsae dicuntur (per prop. 33. P. 2.); sed contra, quatenus ad Deum referuntur, verae sunt (per prop. 32. P. 2.). Si igitur id quod idea falsa positivum habet, praesentia veri, quatenus verum est, tolleretur, tolleretur ergo idea vera a se ipsa, quod (per prop. 4. P. 3.) est absurdum. Ergo nihil, quod idea etc. Q. E. D.
Demonstration. Falsity consists only in the privation of knowledge which inadequate ideas involve (by Prop. 35 of Part II), nor do they have anything positive on account of which they are called false (by Prop. 33 of Part II); on the contrary, insofar as they are related to God, they are true (by Prop. 32 of Part II). If, therefore, what a false idea has that is positive were removed by the presence of the true, insofar as it is true, a true idea would be removed by itself, which (by Prop. 4 of Part III) is absurd. Therefore nothing positive which a false idea, etc. Q.E.D.
Scholium. Intelligitur haec propositio clarius ex coroll. 2. prop. 16. P. 2. Nam imaginatio idea est, quae magisa corporis humani praesentem constitutionem, quam corporis externi naturam indicat, non quidem distincte, sed confuse; unde fit, ut mens errare dicatur. Scholium. This Proposition is understood more clearly from Cor. 2 of Prop. 16 of Part II. For an imagination is an idea which indicates the present constitution of the human body more than the nature of the external body, not distinctly, indeed, but confusedly; from which it comes about that the mind is said to err. For example, when we look at the sun, we imagine it to be about two hundred feet away from us; in which we are deceived as long as we are ignorant of its true distance; but when its distance is known, the error is indeed removed, but not the imagination, that is, the idea of the sun, which explains its nature only insofar as the body is affected by it; and so, although we know its true distance, we shall nevertheless imagine it as near us.

Fit quidem, cum falso aliquod malum timemus, ut timor evanescat audito vero nuntio; sed contra etiam fit, cum malum quod certe venturum est, timemus, ut timor etiam evanescat audito falso nuntio. Atque adeo imaginationes non praesentia veri, quatenus verum, evanescunt; sed quia aliae occurrunt iis fortiores, quae rerum, quas imaginamur, praesentem existentiam secludunt, ut prop. 17. P. 2. ostendimus.
For as we said in the Schol. of Prop. 35 of Part II, we do not imagine the sun so near because we are ignorant of its true distance, but because the mind conceives the size of the sun insofar as the body is affected by it. So when the rays of the sun, falling on the surface of water, are reflected to our eyes, we imagine it as if it were in the water, even though we know its true place. And so the other imaginations by which the mind is deceived, whether they indicate the natural constitution of the body or that its power of acting is increased or diminished, are not contrary to the true, nor do they vanish by its presence. It does indeed happen, when we falsely fear some evil, that the fear vanishes on hearing true news;1 but on the contrary it also happens, when we fear an evil which is certainly going to come, that the fear also vanishes on hearing false news. And so imaginations do not vanish by the presence of the true, insofar as it is true, but because others occur which are stronger than them and exclude the present existence of the things we imagine,2 as we showed in Prop. 17 of Part II.

Proposition II

[G II 212] Nos eatenus patimur, quatenus naturae sumus pars, quae per se absque aliis non potest concipi.
We are acted on insofar as we are a part of nature which cannot be conceived through itself without others.
Demonstratio. Nos tum pati dicimur, cum aliquid in nobis oritur, cuius non nisi partialis sumus causa (per defin. 2. P. 3.), hoc est (per defin. 1. Demonstration. We are said to be acted on when something arises in us of which we are only a partial cause (by Def. 2 of Part III), that is (by Def. 1 of Part III), something which cannot be deduced from the laws of our nature alone. We are acted on, therefore, insofar as we are a part of nature which cannot be conceived through itself without others. Q.E.D.

Propositio III

Proposition III

Vis, qua homo in existendo perseverat, limitata est et a potentia causarum externarum infinite superatur.
The force by which a man perseveres in existing is limited, and is infinitely surpassed by the power of external causes.

Cited in 4P4D, 4P6D, 4P15D, 4P43D, 4P69D

Demonstratio. Patet ex axiom. huius.a Nam dato homine datur aliquid aliud, puta A potentius, et dato A datur deinde aliud, puta B, ipso A potentius et hoc in infinitum. Ac proinde potentia hominis potentia alterius rei definitur, et a potentia causarum externarum infinite superatur. Q. E. D.
Demonstration. This is evident from the Axiom of this Part. For given a man, there is something else, say A, more powerful; and given A, there is then something else, say B, more powerful than A; and so to infinity. And therefore the power of a man is defined by the power of another thing, and is infinitely surpassed by the power of external causes. Q.E.D.

Proposition IX

Propositio XVIII

Proposition XVIII

Proposition XIX

Propositio XXXV

Proposition XXXV

Quatenus homines ex ductu rationis vivunt, eatenus tantum natura semper necessario conveniunt.
Only insofar as men live from the guidance of reason do they always necessarily agree in nature.

Demonstration. Insofar as men are troubled by affects which are passions, they can be different in nature (by Prop. 33 of this Part) and contrary to one another (by the preceding Prop.). But men are said to act only insofar as they live from the guidance of reason (by Prop. 3 of Part III); and so whatever follows from human nature insofar as it is defined by reason must (by Def. 2 of Part III) be understood through human nature alone, as through its proximate cause.

And so men also necessarily always agree with one another insofar as they live from the guidance of reason. Q.E.D.

Propositio XXXVI

Proposition XXXVI

Summum bonum eorum, qui virtutem sectantur, omnibus commune est, eoque omnes aeque gaudere possunt.
The highest good of those who follow virtue is common to all, and all can enjoy it equally.1
  1. 1 Unlike the goods men fight over, this one is not diminished by being shared. Demonstratio. Ex virtute agere est ex ductu rationis agere (per prop. 24. huius), et quicquid ex ratione conamur agere, est intelligere (per prop. 26. huius). Atque adeo (per prop. 28. huius) summum bonum eorum, qui virtutem sectantur, est Deum cognoscere, hoc est (per prop. 47. P. 2. et eiusdem schol.), bonum quod omnibus hominibus commune est, et ab omnibus hominibus, quatenus eiusdem sunt naturae, possideri aeque potest. Q. E. D.
Demonstration. To act from virtue is to act from the guidance of reason (by Prop. 24 of this Part), and whatever we strive to do from reason is to understand (by Prop. 26 of this Part). And so (by Prop. 28 of this Part) the highest good of those who follow virtue is to know God, that is (by Prop. 47 of Part II and its Schol.), a good which is common to all men and can be possessed equally by all men insofar as they are of the same nature. Q.E.D.
Scholium. Si quis autem roget: quid si summum bonum eorum, qui virtutem sectantur, non esset omnibus commune? an non inde, ut supra (vide prop. 34. huius) sequeretur, quod homines, qui ex ductu rationis vivunt, hoc est (per prop. 35. huius) homines, quatenus natura conveniunt, essent invicem contrarii? Pertinet namque (per prop. 47. P. 2.) ad mentis humanae essentiam, adaequatam habere cognitionem aeternae et infinitae essentiae Dei.
Scholium. But if someone asks: what if the highest good of those who follow virtue were not common to all? Would it not follow from this, as above (see Prop. 34 of this Part), that men who live from the guidance of reason, that is (by Prop. 35 of this Part), men insofar as they agree in nature, would be contrary to one another? For it pertains (by Prop. 47 of Part II) to the essence of the human mind to have an adequate knowledge of the eternal and infinite essence of God.

But in truth greed, ambition, lust, etc., are species of madness, although they are not numbered among diseases.

Proposition LVI

Maxima superbia vel abiectio maximam animi impotentiam indicat.

[G II 251] Demonstratio. Primum virtutis fundamentum est suum esse conservare (per coroll. prop. 22. huius) idque ex ductu rationis (per prop. 24. huius). Qui igitur se ipsum ignorat, omnium virtutum fundamentum, et consequenter omnes virtutes ignorat.
Demonstration. The first foundation of virtue is to preserve one’s being (by Cor. of Prop. 22 of this Part), and that from the guidance of reason (by Prop. 24 of this Part). He, therefore, who is ignorant of himself is ignorant of the foundation of all the virtues, and consequently of all the virtues.
Deinde ex virtute agere nihil aliud est, quam ex ductu rationis agere (per prop. 24. huius), et qui ex ductu rationis agit, scire necessario debet se ex ductu rationis agere (per prop. 43. P. 2.). Propositio LVII

Proposition LVII

Superbus parasitorum seu adulatorum praesentiam amat, generosorum autem odit.
The proud man loves the presence of parasites, or flatterers, but hates that of the noble.
Demonstratio. Superbia est laetitia orta ex eo, quod homo de se plus iusto sentit (per aff. defin. 28. et 6.), quam opinionem homo superbus, quantum potest, fovere conabitur (vide schol. prop. 13. Demonstration. Pride is a joy arising from the fact that a man thinks more highly of himself than is just (by Defs. Aff. 28 and 6), an opinion which the proud man will strive to encourage as far as he can (see Schol. of Prop. 13 of Part III). And so the proud will love the presence of parasites or flatterers (I have omitted their definitions because they are too well known), and will flee that of the noble, who think of them as is right. Q.E.D.

Et abiectio huic superbiae contraria definienda esset tristitia orta ex falsa opinione, quod homo se infra reliquos esse credit.
Scholium. It would take too long to enumerate here all the evils of pride, since the proud are subject to all affects, but to none less than the affects of love and compassion. But here it must by no means be passed over in silence that he too is called proud who thinks less of others than is just; and so in this sense pride is to be defined as a joy arising from the false opinion by which a man thinks he is above others. And the self-abasement contrary to this pride would be defined as a sadness arising from the false opinion by which a man believes he is below others.
At hoc posito facile concipimus, superbum necessario esse invidum (vide schol. prop. 55. P. 3.) et eos maxime odio habere, qui maxime ob virtutes laudantur, nec facile eorum odium amore aut beneficio vinci (vide schol. prop. 41. P. 3.), et eorum tantummodo praesentia delectari, qui animo eius impotenti morem gerunt et ex stulto insanum faciunt.
But on this supposition we easily conceive that the proud man is necessarily envious (see Schol. of Prop. 55 of Part III), that he hates most those who are most praised for their virtues, that his hatred of them is not easily conquered by love or benefit (see Schol. of Prop. 41 of Part III), and that he takes pleasure only in the presence of those who humour his mind lacking in power and make a madman out of a fool.
Abiectio quamvis superbiae sit contraria, est tamen abiectus superbo proximus. Nam quandoquidem eius tristitia ex eo oritur, quod suam impotentiam ex aliorum potentia seu virtute iudicat, levabitur ergo eius tristitia, hoc est, laetabitur si eius imaginatio in alienis vitiis contemplandis occupetur, unde illud proverbium natum: Solamen miseris socios habuisse malorum; et contra eo magis contristabitur, quo se magis infra reliquos esse crediderit. Unde fit, ut nulli magis ad invidiam sint proni,a quam abiecti, et ut isti maxime hominum facta observare conentur ad carpendum magis, quam ad eadem corrigendum, et ut tandem solam abiectionem laudent eaque glorientur, sed ita, ut tamen abiecti videantur.
Although self-abasement is contrary to pride, the self-abased man is still very near to the proud. For since his sadness arises from the fact that he judges his lack of power from the power, or virtue, of others, his sadness will therefore be relieved, that is, he will rejoice, if his imagination is occupied in contemplating the vices of others; hence the proverb: It is a comfort to the wretched to have had companions in misfortune; and on the contrary he will be the more saddened, the more he believes himself to be below others. Hence it comes about that no one is more prone to envy than the self-abased, that they strive most to observe men’s deeds, to find fault with them rather than to correct them, and that finally they praise only self-abasement and glory in it, but in such a way that they still seem self-abased.

Atque haec ex hoc affectu tam necessario sequuntur, quam ex natura trianguli, quod eius tres anguli aequales sint duobus rectis; et iam dixi me hos et similes affectus malos vocare, quatenus ad solam humanam utilitatem attendo. But the laws of nature concern the common order of nature, of which man is a part; I wished to point this out here in passing, so that no one should think that I wanted here to tell of the vices and absurd deeds of men, rather than to demonstrate the nature and properties of things.

Sed pergo de affectibus ea notare, quae hominibus utilitatem adferunt, vel quae iisdem damnum inferunt.
For, as I said in the Preface of Part III, I consider human affects and their properties just like other natural things.1 And human affects certainly indicate, if not human power, at least the power and art of nature no less than many other things which we admire and in the contemplation of which we take pleasure. But I go on to note those things about the affects which bring advantage to men, or which inflict harm on them.

Propositio LVIII

Proposition LVIII

[G II 253] Gloria rationi non repugnat, sed ab ea oriri potest.
Glory is not opposed to reason, but can arise from it.
Demonstratio. Patet ex aff. defin. 30. et ex defin. honesti, quam vide in schol. 1. prop. 37. huius.
Demonstration. This is evident from Def. Aff. 30 and from the definition of the honourable, which see in Schol. 1 of Prop. 37 of this Part.
Scholium. Vana quae dicitur gloria est acquiescentia in se ipso, quae sola vulgi opinione fovetur, eaque cessante cessat ipsa acquiescentia, hoc est (per schol. prop. 52. huius) summum bonum, quod unusquisque amat. Unde fit, ut qui vulgi opinione gloriatur, quotidiana cura anxius nitatur, faciat, experiatur, ut famam conservet.
Scholium. What is called empty glory is a self-contentment which is encouraged only by the opinion of the multitude, and when that ceases, the self-contentment itself ceases, that is (by Schol. of Prop. 52 of this Part), the highest good which each one loves. Hence it comes about that he who glories in the opinion of the multitude, anxious with daily care, strives, acts and tries in order to preserve his reputation.
Est namque vulgus varius et inconstans, atque adeo, nisi conservetur fama,a cito abolescit; imo quia omnes vulgi captare applausus cupiunt, facile unusquisque alterius famam reprimit, ex quo, quandoquidem de summo, quod aestimatur, bono certatur, ingens libido oritur se invicem quocumque modo opprimendi, et qui tandem victor evadit, gloriatur magis, quod alteri obfuit, quam quod sibi profuit. Est igitur haec gloria seu acquiescentia revera vana, quia nulla est.
For the multitude is changeable and inconstant, and so, unless a reputation is preserved, it quickly vanishes; indeed, because all desire to capture the applause of the multitude, each one readily represses the reputation of another; and from this, since the contest is over what is esteemed the highest good, there arises an immense lust to crush one another in any way whatever, and he who finally emerges victorious glories more in having harmed another than in having benefited himself. This glory, or self-contentment, is therefore truly empty, because it is nothing.

Quae de pudore notanda sunt, colliguntur facile ex iis, quae de misericordia et poenitentia diximus. What should be noted about shame is easily gathered from what we have said about compassion and repentance. I add only this, that, like pity, shame, although it is not a virtue, is still good insofar as it indicates that the man who is suffused with shame has a desire to live honourably, just as pain is called good insofar as it indicates that the injured part is not yet putrefied. So although a man who is ashamed of some deed is in truth sad, he is still more perfect than the shameless man, who has no desire to live honourably.1

Sed omnes revera, quatenus ex affectibus, qui passiones sunt, in nobis ingenerantur, caecae sunt (ut facile colligitur ex iis, quae in schol. prop. 44. huius diximus), nec ullius usus essent, si homines facile duci possent, ut ex solo rationis dictamine viverent, ut iam paucis ostendam.
These are the things I had undertaken to note about the affects of joy and sadness. As for desires, they are certainly good or evil insofar as they arise from good or evil affects. But all of them, insofar as they are generated in us from affects which are passions, are in truth blind (as is easily gathered from what we said in Schol. of Prop. 44 of this Part), nor would they be of any use if men could easily be led to live from the dictate of reason alone, as I shall now show in a few words.

Propositio LIX

Proposition LIX

Ad omnes actiones, ad quas ex affectu, qui passio est, determinamur, possumus absque eo a ratione determinari.
To all the actions to which we are determined from an affect which is a passion, we can be determined by reason without it.1
  1. 1 The same action can follow from a passion or from reason; what differs is its cause. Part V gives an example: the appetite by which each wants others to live according to his own temperament is, in a man not led by reason, the passion called ambition, and in one who lives from the dictate of reason, an action or virtue called piety, pietas (Part V, Prop. 4, Schol.).

Demonstratio. Ex ratione agere nihil aliud est (per prop. 3. et defin. 2. P. 3.) quam ea agere, quae ex necessitate nostrae naturae in se sola consideratae sequuntur. At tristitia eatenus mala est, quatenus hanc agendi potentiam minuit vel coercet (per prop. 41. huius). Ergo ex hoc affectu ad nullam actionem possumus determinari, quam non possemus agere, si ratione duceremur.
Demonstration. To act from reason is nothing but (by Prop. 3 and Def. 2 of Part III) to do those things which follow from the necessity of our nature considered in itself alone. But sadness is evil insofar as it diminishes or restrains this power of acting (by Prop. 41 of this Part). Therefore from this affect we can be determined to no action which we could not do if we were led by reason.
Praeterea laetitia eatenus mala est,a quatenus impedit, quominus homo ad agendum sit aptus (per prop. 41. et 43. huius) atque adeo eatenus etiam ad nullam actionem determinari possumus, quam non possemus agere, si ratione duceremur. Denique quatenus laetitia bona est, eatenus cum ratione convenit (consistit enim in eo, quod hominis agendi potentia augetur vel iuvatur), nec passio est nisi quatenus hominis agendi potentia non eo usque augetur, ut se suasque actiones adaequate concipiat (per prop. 3. P. 3. cum eius schol.). Quare si homo laetitia affectus ad tantam perfectionem duceretur, ut se suasque actiones adaequate conciperet, ad easdem actiones, ad quas iam ex affectibus, qui passiones sunt, determinatur, aptus, imo aptior esset.
Further, joy is evil insofar as it prevents a man from being capable of acting (by Props. 41 and 43 of this Part); and so to that extent too we can be determined to no action which we could not do if we were led by reason. Finally, insofar as joy is good, to that extent it agrees with reason (for it consists in this, that a man’s power of acting is increased or aided), and it is a passion only insofar as a man’s power of acting is not increased to the point where he conceives himself and his actions adequately (by Prop. 3 of Part III with its Schol.). So if a man affected with joy were led to such perfection that he conceived himself and his actions adequately, he would be capable, indeed more capable, of the same actions to which he is now determined from affects which are passions.

Propositio LX

Proposition LX

Cupiditas, quae oritur ex laetitia vel tristitia, quae ad unam vel ad aliquot, non autem ad omnes corporis partes refertur, rationem utilitatis totius hominis non habet.
A desire which arises from a joy or sadness that is related to one or several, but not to all, of the parts of the body has no regard for the advantage of the whole man.
[G II 256] Demonstratio. Ponatur ex. gr. corporis pars A vi alicuius causae externae ita corroborari, ut reliquis praevaleat (per prop. 6. huius); haec pars vires suas amittere propterea non conabitur, ut reliquae corporis partes suo fungantur officio. Deberet enim vim seu potentiam habere vires suas amittendi; quod (per prop. 6. P. 3.) est absurdum.
Demonstration. Suppose, for example, that part A of the body is so strengthened by the force of some external cause that it prevails over the others (by Prop. 6 of this Part); this part will not for that reason strive to lose its powers so that the other parts of the body may perform their function. For it would have to have a force, or power, of losing its powers, which (by Prop. 6 of Part III) is absurd.
Conabitur itaque illa pars, et consequenter (per prop. 7. et 12. P. 3.) mens etiam illum statum conservare; adeoque cupiditas, quae ex tali affectu laetitiae oritur, rationem totius non habet. Quod si contra supponatur pars A coerceri, ut reliquae praevaleant, eodem modo demonstratur, quod nec cupiditas, quae ex tristitia oritur, rationem totius habeat. Q. E. D.
That part will therefore strive, and consequently (by Props. 7 and 12 of Part III) the mind also will strive, to preserve that state; and so the desire which arises from such an affect of joy has no regard for the whole. But if, on the contrary, part A is supposed to be restrained, so that the others prevail, it is demonstrated in the same way that the desire which arises from sadness has no regard for the whole either. Q.E.D.

The Free Man

The last seven propositions of Part IV describe a figure the Preface had announced: the “model of human nature” which we may look to (Preface). They are written differently from the rest of the Part. The preceding propositions measured the force of the affects and set out the dictates of reason one by one; these gather the results into the portrait of a single man, and it is worth being clear in advance about what kind of figure he is and in what sense he is called free.

Slave and free

The Scholium to Prop. 66 introduces him by contrast. The man led by affect alone, or opinion, “whether he will or not, does the things of which he is most ignorant”; the man led by reason “complies with no one’s wishes but his own, and does only those things which he knows to be most important in life, and which he therefore desires most.” Spinoza calls the first a slave and the second free. The word answers to the title of the Part, De servitute humana, and to the definition of freedom in Part I, Def. 7: a thing is free which exists from the necessity of its own nature alone and is determined to act by itself alone. Only God is free in the full sense. A man is free to the extent that what he does follows from his own nature, that is, to the extent that he is the adequate cause of his actions, and Part III has shown that this is the case insofar as he has adequate ideas (Part III, Prop. 3). Freedom, for Spinoza, is a matter of degree, and it is the same thing as understanding.

This sense of freedom is easily misunderstood. It is not the absence of causes, since everything has a cause; it is not the ability to have done otherwise, since nothing could have been otherwise; and it is not doing whatever one wants, since the man led by his passions does what he wants and is a slave. The free man is free because his actions express his own nature rather than the pressure of external things on it, and because he knows what he is doing and why. The rest of the portrait spells out what such a man would be like.

Death

The portrait begins with death. “A free man thinks of nothing less than of death, and his wisdom is a meditation not on death but on life” (Prop. 67). The sentence answers a long tradition. Cicero had repeated the saying of Plato’s Socrates that “tota enim philosophorum vita … commentatio mortis est” (“the whole life of philosophers is a preparation for death,” Tusculan Disputations I.74). Spinoza’s demonstration is short: the free man is not led by fear (Prop. 63), but desires the good directly (Prop. 63, Cor.), that is, to act, to live and to preserve his being. The thought of death belongs to fear, and the free man is not led by fear.

It may seem strange that a book which ends with an account of the eternity of the mind should forbid the thought of death. But there is no conflict. Part V will not console the reader with the prospect of a life after death; it will argue that something of the mind is eternal now, in the sense of Part I, Def. 8, and that “the more things the mind understands by the second and third kinds of knowledge, the less it is acted on by affects which are evil, and the less it fears death” (Part V, Prop. 38). The free man thinks of life, not because he avoids an unpleasant subject, but because what he understands is the power by which things exist and act, not their destruction, which comes from outside.

Born free: the story of the first man

Prop. 68 then supposes men born free and concludes that they would form no concept of good and evil so long as they remained free, since the knowledge of evil is inadequate (Prop. 64) and good and evil are correlative. The Scholium at once declares the hypothesis false: it can be conceived only if we attend to human nature alone, or to God not as infinite but only as the cause of man’s existence, and Prop. 4 showed that a man cannot but be a part of nature.

The Scholium then reads the story of the first man as an allegory of the doctrine. In it, Spinoza says, no other power of God is conceived than that by which he created man, a power that consulted only man’s advantage. God forbade the free man to eat of the tree of the knowledge of good and evil, and as soon as he ate of it, “he would at once fear death rather than desire to live.” Finding a wife who agreed entirely with his nature, he knew that nothing in nature could be more useful to him than she; “but that after he believed the beasts to be like himself, he at once began to imitate their affects ... and to lose his freedom, which the patriarchs afterwards recovered, led by the spirit of Christ, that is, by the idea of God, on which alone it depends that man is free” (Prop. 68, Schol.). The Theological-Political Treatise had read the command to Adam in the same way: God commanded him to do good from the love of good and not from fear of evil (ch. 4).

The reading is remarkable for what it does with each element of the story. The knowledge of good and evil is not a forbidden wisdom but the inadequate knowledge that comes with fear; the fall is not disobedience but the imitation of affects proper to another nature; and redemption is not a supernatural grace but the idea of God, which the reader of Part II has already learned that every human mind possesses (Part II, Prop. 47). The phrase “led by the spirit of Christ, that is, by the idea of God” is characteristic of the way Spinoza uses religious language: he does not reject it, but tells the reader what, in his view, it really means.

Among others

The remaining propositions show the free man among others. “The virtue of a free man is seen to be as great in declining dangers as in overcoming them” (Prop. 69), and “to a free man, therefore, timely flight is ascribed to as great a tenacity as battle; or, the free man chooses flight with the same tenacity, or presence of mind, as combat” (Prop. 69, Cor.). The point is not that cowardice is acceptable, but that courage is not recklessness: the same strength of character that makes a man stand firm when that is reasonable makes him withdraw when withdrawal is reasonable.

“A free man who lives among the ignorant strives as far as he can to decline their favours” (Prop. 70), so as to follow his own reason rather than their appetite, and not to be hated for repaying less than they expect. But the scholium adds a qualification that shows how far Spinoza is from a lofty indifference to ordinary people: “although men are ignorant, they are still men, who in times of need can bring human help, than which nothing is more excellent,” and so it is often necessary to accept their favours and thank them in their own way; and “caution must be exercised even in declining favours, so that we do not seem to despise them” (Prop. 70, Schol.). “Only free men are most grateful to one another” (Prop. 71); the gratitude of men led by blind desire “is for the most part a bargain or a hunt for gain rather than thankfulness” (Prop. 71, Schol.).

Good faith

“A free man never acts deceptively, but always in good faith” (Prop. 72). The scholium raises the hardest case. “What if a man could free himself by perfidy from a present danger of death? Would not reason, for preserving his being, entirely advise him to be perfidious?” The answer is that “if reason advises this, it therefore advises it to all men, and so reason entirely advises men to make no agreements to join their forces and to have common rights except deceptively, that is, not really to have common rights, which is absurd” (Prop. 72, Schol.).

The scholium has puzzled readers, because it seems to sit uneasily with the ethics of self-preservation. If virtue is seeking one’s own advantage and preserving one’s being, why should the free man not lie to save his life? Spinoza’s answer turns on the universality of reason. What reason advises one man it advises to all, since reason is the same in all, and a policy of deceit when convenient, adopted by all, would destroy the trust on which common life depends. The argument resembles later arguments for the universalizability of moral principles, and readers have asked whether it is consistent with the rest of Part IV. But the scholium also says something about what the free man is. He does not calculate whether a lie would serve him on this occasion, because the reason that guides him does not take the point of view of this occasion. His good faith is not a rule he obeys but an expression of the kind of understanding he has.

Freedom in the state

“A man who is led by reason is more free in a state, where he lives according to the common decree, than in solitude, where he obeys himself alone” (Prop. 73). The proposition completes the movement of the Part from the individual to the community. It may seem paradoxical that a man should be freer under laws than without them, but it follows from everything Spinoza has said. The free man is not led by fear to obey the laws, and he desires to live by the rule of common life and common advantage, which the laws express; the state supplies the security and the help of others without which he could not live by reason at all. Solitude is not independence. It is exposure to the power of external causes without the help of those whose nature agrees most with one’s own.

Strength of character

The closing Scholium refers everything said about “the true freedom of man” to strength of character, that is, to tenacity and nobility (Prop. 73, Schol.), and declines to demonstrate separately all its properties, “and much less that a strong man hates no one, is angry with no one, envies no one, is indignant with no one, despises no one, and is least of all proud.” These follow easily from the propositions that hatred is to be conquered by love and that each one led by reason desires for others the good he wants for himself. The strong man considers above all “that all things follow from the necessity of the divine nature,” and that whatever he finds troublesome and evil, impious, horrible, unjust and base arises from his conceiving things in a disturbed, mutilated and confused way; and so he strives above all to conceive things as they are in themselves, and to remove the obstacles to true knowledge, hatred, anger, envy, mockery and pride. The ethics of Part IV ends where Part V will begin, with the improvement of understanding.

Reading the portrait

Two things should be kept in view. First, the free man is a model, not a description of anyone. Men are necessarily always subject to passions (Prop. 4, Cor.), and the Scholium to Prop. 68 calls the hypothesis of a man born free false. What the propositions describe is the direction in which a man becomes more perfect, that is, more powerful, as he comes to understand; the Preface had said that men are called more or less perfect insofar as they come nearer to the model or are further from it. The portrait is meant to be useful, as the Preface promised, not to be matched.

Second, the portrait is of a social being. The free man’s freedom is not independence from others but agreement with them: he seeks his own advantage from the guidance of reason, and nothing is more useful to him than other men who live by reason (Prop. 35, Cor. 1). That is why the series ends with the state, and why the virtues it describes are almost all virtues of common life: courage, gratitude, good faith, obedience to common law. The solitary sage of some ancient traditions has no place here.

The Appendix

An Appendix follows in which Spinoza collects what the Part has taught about the right way of living, which had been demonstrated piecemeal, and brings it under main headings in thirty-two short chapters of continuous prose. Its fourth chapter states the final end of a man led by reason: “In life, therefore, it is above all useful to perfect the intellect, or reason, as far as we can, and in this one thing man’s highest happiness, or blessedness, consists” (Appendix, ch. 4). Other chapters treat friendship, the education of others to live by reason (ch. 9), flattery, money and marriage, which “agrees with reason if the desire for bodily union is generated not from beauty alone but also from love of begetting children and educating them wisely,” and if the love of each has as its cause “chiefly freedom of mind” (ch. 20). The last chapter returns to the theme of human weakness with which the Part began, and turns it into a source of contentment: human power is very limited and infinitely surpassed by external causes, but if we understand clearly that we are a part of the whole of nature whose order we follow, “that part of us which is defined by understanding, that is, the better part of us, will be entirely content with it,” since “insofar as we understand, we can want nothing but what is necessary” (ch. 32).

As the text continues, the portrait runs from Prop. 67 to Prop. 73, and the Appendix follows. How far human power can go in attaining the freedom described here is the subject of Part V.

Propositio LXIX

Proposition LXIX

[G II 262] Hominis liberi virtus aeque magna cernitur in declinandis, quam in superandis periculis.
The virtue of a free man is seen to be as great in declining dangers as in overcoming them.

Proposition LXX

Homo liber qui inter ignaros vivit,a eorum, quantum potest, beneficia declinare studet.
A free man who lives among the ignorant strives as far as he can to decline their favours.

Demonstration. Each one judges from his own temperament what is good (see Schol. of Prop. 39 of Part III). An ignorant man, therefore, who has conferred a favour on someone, will value it according to his own temperament, and if he sees that it is valued less by the one to whom it was given, he will be saddened (by Prop. 42 of Part III).
At homo liber reliquos homines amicitia sibi iungere (per prop. 37. huius), nec paria hominibus beneficia ex eorum affectu referre, sed se et reliquos libero rationis iudicio ducere et ea tantum agere studet, quae ipse prima esse novit. Scholium. Dico quantum potest. Nam quamvis homines ignari sint, sunt tamen homines, qui in necessitatibus humanum auxilium, quo nullum praestabilius est, adferre queunt. Atque adeo saepe fit, ut necesse sit ab iisdem beneficium accipere, et consequenter iisdem contra ex eorum ingenio congratulari. Ad quod accedit, quod etiam in declinandis beneficiis cautio esse debet, ne videamur eosdem contemnere, vel prae avaritia remunerationem timere, atque ita, dum eorum odium fugimus, eo ipso in eorum offensionem incurramus. Quare in declinandis beneficiis ratio utilis et honesti habenda est.
Scholium. I say as far as he can. For although men are ignorant, they are still men, who in times of need can bring human help, than which nothing is more excellent. And so it often happens that it is necessary to accept a favour from them, and consequently to thank them in return according to their temperament. Add to this that caution must be exercised even in declining favours, so that we do not seem to despise them, or to fear repayment out of greed, and so, while we flee their hatred, by that very fact incur their offence. So in declining favours account must be taken of what is useful and honourable.

Proposition LXXI

Soli homines liberi erga invicem gratissimi sunt.
Only free men are most grateful to one another.
Demonstratio. Soli homines liberi sibi invicem utilissimi sunt et maxima amicitiae necessitudine invicem iunguntur (per prop. 35. huius et eius coroll. 1.), parique amoris studio sibi invicem benefacere conantur (per prop. 37. huius). Propositio LXXII

Proposition LXXII

[G II 264] Homo liber nunquam dolo malo, sed semper cum fide agit.
A free man never acts deceptively, but always in good faith.
Demonstratio. Si liber homo quicquam dolo malo, quatenus liber est, ageret, id ex dictamine rationis ageret (nam eatenus tantum liber a nobis appellatur); atque adeo dolo malo agere virtus esset (per prop. 24. huius), et consequenter (per eandem prop.) unicuique ad suum esse conservandum consultius esset, dolo malo agere, hoc est (ut per se notum) hominibus consultius esset verbis solummodo convenire, re autem invicem esse contrarios; quod (per coroll. prop. 31. huius) est absurdum. Ergo homo liber etc. Q. E. D.
Demonstration. If a free man, insofar as he is free, did anything deceptively, he would do it from the dictate of reason (for only to that extent do we call him free); and so to act deceptively would be a virtue (by Prop. 24 of this Part), and consequently (by the same Prop.) it would be more advisable for everyone, for preserving his being, to act deceptively, that is (as is self-evident), it would be more advisable for men to agree only in words, but in fact to be contrary to one another, which (by Cor. of Prop. 31 of this Part) is absurd. Therefore a free man, etc. Q.E.D.

Propositio LXXIII

Proposition LXXIII

Chapter XXXII. But human power is very limited and is infinitely surpassed by the power of external causes; and so we do not have an absolute power of adapting things outside us to our use. Nevertheless, we shall bear with equanimity whatever happens to us contrary to what the principle of our advantage requires, if we are conscious that we have done our duty, that the power we have could not have extended so far as to avoid those things, and that we are a part of the whole of nature, whose order we follow.
Quod si clare et distincte intelligamus, pars illa nostri, quae intelligentia definitur, hoc est, pars melior nostri, in eo plane acquiescet et in ea acquiescentia perseverare conabitur. Nam quatenus intelligimus, nihil appetere nisi id quod necessarium est, nec absolute, nisi in veris acquiescere possumus; adeoque quatenus haec recte intelligimus, eatenus conatus melioris partis nostri cum ordine totius naturae convenit.
If we understand this clearly and distinctly, that part of us which is defined by understanding, that is, the better part of us, will be entirely content with it, and will strive to persevere in that contentment. For insofar as we understand, we can want nothing but what is necessary, nor be content absolutely with anything but what is true; and so, insofar as we understand these things rightly, to that extent the striving of the better part of us agrees with the order of the whole of nature.1
  1. 1 The Part closes where it began, with man as a part of nature (Prop. 4, Cor.), but now seen from the side of understanding. ‘The better part of us’ is the intellect; Part V calls it the eternal part of the mind (Part V, Prop. 40, Cor.).
