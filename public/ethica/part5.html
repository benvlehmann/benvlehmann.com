Ethices Pars Quinta
De Potentia Intellectus seu de Libertate Humana

Fifth Part of the Ethics
Of the Power of the Intellect, or of Human Freedom

Introduction to the Fifth Part

The last Part is called De potentia intellectus, seu de libertate humana, “Of the Power of the Intellect, or of Human Freedom.” Part IV ended by describing a free man and admitting that no one is born free; Part V asks how far the mind can make itself free, and in what that freedom consists. Its Preface states both questions: Spinoza will treat of the power of reason, “showing what reason itself can do against the affects, and then what freedom of mind, or blessedness, is; from which we shall see how much more powerful the wise man is than the ignorant” (Preface). The answer to the first question occupies Props. 1–20; the answer to the second, Props. 21–42.

Part V is the shortest Part of the Ethics, less than half the length of Part IV, and it is the most disputed. Its first half is a practical psychology, sober and close to experience, and most readers find it among the most useful things in the book. Its second half, on the eternity of the mind and the intellectual love of God, is the culmination toward which Spinoza says everything has been leading, and it has been read as the summit of his philosophy, as a mystical appendage to it, and as a failure. This introduction describes both halves, the essay before Prop. 21 introduces the second more fully, and Appendix E describes how modern interpreters have read it.

The Preface: the Stoics and Descartes

The Preface begins by marking the limits of the Part. How the intellect is to be perfected belongs to logic, and how the body is to be cared for belongs to medicine; neither is treated here. Spinoza will treat only of the power of the mind, “and above all I shall show how great and of what kind its dominion over the affects is, for restraining and moderating them. For we have already demonstrated above that we do not have absolute dominion over them.”

Two answers are then set aside. The Stoics thought that the affects depend absolutely on our will and that we can command them absolutely, yet they were compelled “by the protests of experience and not by their own principles” to admit that no little practice is needed to restrain them; someone, Spinoza recalls, tried to show this with two dogs, a house dog trained to hunt and a hunting dog trained to leave hares alone. Descartes inclines to the same opinion. Spinoza summarizes the doctrine of the Passions of the Soul at length and with evident care: the mind is united chiefly to the pineal gland, which it can move by willing; each motion of the gland is joined by nature to a certain thought, but can be joined to others by habit; and so, by determining the will through firm judgments and joining to them the motions of the passions we want to have, “we shall acquire an absolute dominion over our passions.” From this Descartes concluded “that there is no soul so weak that it cannot, when it is well directed, acquire an absolute power over its passions” (Preface).

Spinoza’s reply is one of the sharpest passages he wrote. He can hardly believe that so great a man should have put the doctrine forward, “if it had been less acute.” A philosopher who had resolved to deduce nothing except from self-evident principles, and who had so often reproached the scholastics for explaining obscure things by occult qualities, assumed “a hypothesis more occult than any occult quality.” “What clear and distinct concept, I say, has he of a thought most closely united to a certain small portion of quantity?” How many degrees of motion can the mind give the gland, and with what force can it hold it? Might not the motions joined to firm judgments be disjoined again by corporeal causes, so that a man who had resolved to face danger would, at the sight of it, think only of flight? “And indeed, since there is no ratio of will to motion, there is also no comparison between the power, or forces, of the mind and those of the body.” The anatomy is wrong as well: the gland is not so placed that it can be moved so easily, and not all the nerves reach the cavities of the brain. “Therefore, since the power of the mind, as I have shown above, is defined by understanding alone, we shall determine the remedies for the affects ... by the knowledge of the mind alone, and from that same knowledge we shall deduce all that concerns its blessedness.”

The Preface thus states the program of the whole Part. There is no will that can master the passions by moving the body; there is only understanding, and whatever power the mind has over the affects must come from it.

The axioms and the first propositions

The Part has two axioms. If two contrary actions are aroused in the same subject, a change must take place in both or in one until they cease to be contrary (Axiom 1); and the power of an effect is defined by the power of its cause (Axiom 2). The first propositions lay the foundation for the remedies. “In just the way that thoughts and the ideas of things are ordered and connected in the mind, so the affections of the body, or the images of things, are ordered and connected exactly in the body” (Prop. 1), which is Part II’s parallelism applied to the affects: if the mind orders its thoughts, the body’s affections are ordered correspondingly. “If we separate a commotion of the mind, or affect, from the thought of an external cause, and join it to other thoughts, then the love or hatred towards the external cause ... will be destroyed” (Prop. 2), since love and hatred are joy and sadness accompanied by the idea of an external cause.

Then comes the central proposition of the first half: “An affect which is a passion ceases to be a passion as soon as we form a clear and distinct idea of it” (Prop. 3). The demonstration is short: a passion is a confused idea, and when we form a clear and distinct idea of it, it will be distinguished from the affect only by reason, and so will cease to be a passion. “An affect, therefore, is the more in our power, and the mind is acted on by it the less, the better it is known to us” (Prop. 3, Cor.). And “there is no affection of the body of which we cannot form some clear and distinct concept” (Prop. 4), since whatever is common to all things can only be conceived adequately. The scholium concludes that “no remedy for the affects which depends on our power can be thought of that is more excellent than this one, which consists in a true knowledge of them, since there is no other power of the mind than that of thinking and forming adequate ideas” (Prop. 4, Schol.).

The remedies

The next propositions describe the ways in which understanding changes the force of the affects. An affect toward a thing imagined simply, not as necessary, possible or contingent, is the greatest (Prop. 5); and “insofar as the mind understands all things as necessary, to that extent it has a greater power over the affects” (Prop. 6). The scholium appeals to experience. The sadness over a good that has been lost is eased as soon as a man considers that it could not have been kept; and “no one pities an infant because it does not know how to speak, walk or reason,” but if most men were born adults and only a few infants, everyone would pity infants, regarding infancy as a fault of nature rather than as natural and necessary (Prop. 6, Schol.).

Affects that arise from reason are, over time, more powerful than those related to singular things contemplated as absent, since reason is concerned with the common properties of things, which are always present (Prop. 7). An affect is greater the more causes concur in arousing it (Prop. 8), and an affect related to many causes is less harmful than an equally great affect related to one (Prop. 9). “So long as we are not troubled by affects which are contrary to our nature, we have the power of ordering and connecting the affections of the body according to the order of the intellect” (Prop. 10).

The scholium to Prop. 10 turns this into practical advice, and it is the passage of the Ethics that comes closest to a manual of conduct. “The best thing, therefore, that we can do, so long as we do not have perfect knowledge of our affects, is to conceive a right way of living, or sure principles of life, to commit them to memory, and to apply them continually to the particular things frequently met with in life, so that in this way our imagination may be widely affected by them, and they may always be ready to hand for us.” The example is the precept of Part IV that hatred is to be conquered by love, or nobility, and not repaid with hatred: to have it ready when needed, we must often meditate on the common injuries of men and on how they are best warded off by nobility, so that the image of an injury is joined to the image of the precept (Prop. 10, Schol.). The same scholium applies the method to fear, by imagining the common dangers of life and how presence of mind and strength of character overcome them, and advises that in ordering our thoughts we attend to what is good in each thing, so as to be moved by joy: a man who pursues glory too much should think of its right use, and not of its abuse and emptiness and the inconstancy of men, “about which no one thinks except from sickness of mind.” The remedies of the Ethics are not acts of will; they are habits of thought, formed by understanding and maintained by practice.

The love of God

The first half of the Part ends with a new affect. “The mind can bring it about that all the affections of the body, or images of things, are related to the idea of God” (Prop. 14), since there is no affection of the body of which it cannot form a clear and distinct concept. “He who understands himself and his affects clearly and distinctly loves God, and the more so the more he understands himself and his affects” (Prop. 15), since such understanding is accompanied by joy and by the idea of God as its cause. “This love towards God must occupy the mind most” (Prop. 16), because it is joined to all the affections of the body and encouraged by all of them.

The love Spinoza describes is not reciprocal. “God is without passions, and is not affected with any affect of joy or sadness” (Prop. 17), and so, “strictly speaking, God loves no one and hates no one” (Prop. 17, Cor.). No one can hate God (Prop. 18). “He who loves God cannot strive that God should love him in return” (Prop. 19), since he would then desire that God, whom he loves, should not be God. And this love “cannot be tainted by the affect of envy or of jealousy; but it is the more encouraged, the more men we imagine to be joined to God by the same bond of love” (Prop. 20). Unlike the loves analysed in Part III, which are troubled by rivals, loss and change, the love of God is shared without diminution and directed at an object that cannot be lost.

The scholium to Prop. 20 gathers the results into five remedies. The power of the mind over the affects consists “1. In the knowledge itself of the affects ... 2. In its separating the affects from the thought of an external cause, which we imagine confusedly ... 3. In time, by which the affections related to things which we understand surpass those related to things which we conceive confusedly, or in a mutilated way ... 4. In the multitude of causes by which affections related to the common properties of things, or to God, are encouraged ... 5. Finally, in the order in which the mind can order its affects and connect them with one another” (Prop. 20, Schol.). The scholium ends with a sentence that divides the Part in two: “And with these I have completed everything which concerns this present life. ... It is time, therefore, to pass to those things which pertain to the duration of the mind without relation to the body.”

The eternity of the mind

The second half begins with the denial of what most readers expect. “The mind can imagine nothing, nor recollect past things, except while the body endures” (Prop. 21). But there is in God an idea which expresses the essence of this or that human body under a species of eternity (Prop. 22), and so “the human mind cannot be absolutely destroyed with the body, but something of it remains which is eternal” (Prop. 23). The eternity in question is not endless duration but the eternity defined in Part I, and the essay before Prop. 21 explains the doctrine and its difficulties.

From it Spinoza derives an account of the third kind of knowledge and its fruits. “The more we understand singular things, the more we understand God” (Prop. 24). “The highest striving of the mind, and its highest virtue, is to understand things by the third kind of knowledge” (Prop. 25), and “from this third kind of knowledge arises the greatest contentment of mind there can be” (Prop. 27). The desire to know things in this way cannot arise from the first kind of knowledge, but it can from the second (Prop. 28): reason prepares the way for intuition, and imagination does not.

“Whatever we understand by the third kind of knowledge we take delight in, and our delight is accompanied by the idea of God as its cause” (Prop. 32). From this “there necessarily arises the intellectual love of God,” the love of God not insofar as we imagine him as present, “but insofar as we understand God to be eternal” (Prop. 32, Cor.). This love is eternal (Prop. 33), though the scholium explains that Spinoza speaks of it as if it had come into being, for ease of exposition. “God loves himself with an infinite intellectual love” (Prop. 35), and “the intellectual love of the mind towards God is the very love of God by which God loves himself ... that is, the intellectual love of the mind towards God is part of the infinite love by which God loves himself” (Prop. 36). Hence “God, insofar as he loves himself, loves men, and consequently ... the love of God towards men and the intellectual love of the mind towards God are one and the same” (Prop. 36, Cor.).

The scholium to Prop. 36 draws the conclusion toward which the whole book has moved. “From these things we understand clearly in what our salvation, or blessedness, or freedom consists: namely, in a constant and eternal love towards God, or in the love of God towards men.” This love “is called glory in the sacred books, and not without reason.” And Spinoza adds a remark about the third kind of knowledge itself: he has shown generally in Part I that all things depend on God according to essence and existence, but that demonstration, though certain, does not affect the mind as much as when the same conclusion is drawn from the essence of a singular thing that we say depends on God (Prop. 36, Schol.). The difference between the second and third kinds of knowledge is the difference between knowing that everything follows from God and seeing how this thing, oneself, does.

Body, death and degrees of eternity

The last propositions draw consequences for the life of the body. “There is nothing in nature which is contrary to this intellectual love, or which can take it away” (Prop. 37). “The more things the mind understands by the second and third kinds of knowledge, the less it is acted on by affects which are evil, and the less it fears death” (Prop. 38). “He who has a body capable of a great many things has a mind whose greatest part is eternal” (Prop. 39), so that eternity is not given to every mind in the same measure, and the scholium returns to the infant and the corpse, the changes of life by which we are called happy or unhappy. “The more perfection each thing has, the more it acts and the less it is acted on” (Prop. 40), and the part of the mind that remains, however great it may be, is more perfect than the rest (Prop. 40, Cor.).

An ethics that does not depend on immortality

Then Spinoza does something surprising. Having argued that something of the mind is eternal, he insists that the ethics of Part IV does not depend on it. “Even if we did not know that our mind is eternal, we would still regard as of first importance piety and religion, and absolutely all the things which we showed in Part IV to be related to tenacity and nobility” (Prop. 41). The scholium attacks the common view that piety and religion are burdens to be laid down after death, borne only in hope of reward and fear of punishment, and calls it no less absurd than if someone, because he did not believe he could nourish his body with good food for eternity, should prefer to fill himself with poisons (Prop. 41, Schol.).

The last proposition answers that view directly. “Blessedness is not the reward of virtue, but virtue itself; nor do we enjoy it because we restrain our lusts, but on the contrary, because we enjoy it, we can restrain our lusts” (Prop. 42). The final scholium contrasts the ignorant man, driven about by external causes and never possessing true contentment of mind, who “as soon as he ceases to be acted on, he also ceases to be,” with the wise man, who, “being conscious of himself, and of God, and of things by a certain eternal necessity, never ceases to be, but always possesses true contentment of mind.” And it ends with the most famous sentences of the book: “If the way which I have shown to lead to these things now seems very hard, it can nevertheless be found. And indeed what is so rarely found must be hard. For how could it happen, if salvation were ready to hand and could be found without great labour, that it should be neglected by almost everyone? But all things excellent are as difficult as they are rare” (Prop. 42, Schol.).

Questions the reader will meet

The first concerns God’s love. Prop. 17, Cor. says that strictly speaking God loves no one; Prop. 36, Cor. says that God, insofar as he loves himself, loves men. The contradiction is apparent. The first denies that God has passions, joy or sadness that arise from external causes; the second describes an intellectual love that is not a passion and has no external object, the love by which God, conceived through the essence of the human mind, enjoys his own perfection. The phrase “strictly speaking” in the corollary to Prop. 17 is a warning that the word “love” will be used again in another sense.

The second concerns the relation between the two halves of the Part. The remedies of the first half concern “this present life”; the second half concerns the mind “without relation to the body.” Yet the second half speaks of what the mind can do now, and its account of the intellectual love of God continues the account of the love of God in Props. 14–20. Readers have asked whether the two halves describe two kinds of freedom, a practical one and a contemplative one, or two aspects of one.

The third concerns the value of the second half. Jonathan Bennett judged that “the final three doctrines” of the Ethics, the mind’s eternity, intuitive knowledge and the intellectual love of God, cannot be rescued, and that this part of the Ethics “has nothing to teach us” (A Study of Spinoza’s Ethics, § 85, p. 372). Others, among them Stuart Hampshire, whom Bennett quotes, have read the doctrine sympathetically as an account of what it is to understand things completely and to know oneself, in such moments, as part of the eternal order of nature. The reader will have to decide; the essay before Prop. 21 tries to make clear what the doctrine claims and what it does not.

A last question concerns how hard the way is. Spinoza ends by saying that it is very hard and rarely found, and the remedies of Part V are modest: knowledge of the affects, separation of them from their external causes, time, the multitude of causes, and order. None of them promises a sudden liberation. The freedom of Part V is a matter of degree, and so is its blessedness, and the book’s last word is not a promise but an encouragement.

The place of Part V in the whole

Part V gathers the threads of the whole Ethics. Its God is the God of Part I, who acts from the necessity of his nature and loves himself with an intellectual love; its theory of knowledge is that of Part II, with the third kind of knowledge at last put to work; its remedies apply the theory of the affects of Part III; and its blessedness is the end toward which the free man of Part IV was striving. The Preface to Part II promised that the work would explain only what could lead us, as if by the hand, to the knowledge of the human mind and its highest blessedness. Part V is where that promise is kept.

The text begins with the Preface, with its criticism of the Stoics and of Descartes, and then with the two axioms. The first propositions are short and depend closely on Parts II and III; the reader who has followed those Parts will find the opening of Part V easy going.

This is the opinion of this most distinguished man (so far as I can conjecture from his words); and I would hardly have believed it to have been put forward by so great a man, if it had been less acute. Indeed, I cannot wonder enough that a philosopher who had firmly resolved to deduce nothing except from self-evident principles, and to affirm nothing except what he perceived clearly and distinctly, and who had so often reproached the scholastics for wanting to explain obscure things by occult qualities, should assume a hypothesis more occult than any occult quality.
Quid, quaeso, per mentis et corporis unionem intelligit? Quem, inquam, clarum et distinctum conceptum habet cogitationis arctissime unitae cuidam quantitatis portiunculae? Vellem sane, ut hanc unionem per proximam suam causam explicuisset. Sed ille mentem a corpore adeo distinctam conceperat, ut nec huius unionis, nec ipsius mentis ullam singularem causam assignare potuerit, sed necesse ipsi fuerit, ad causam totius universi, hoc est, ad Deum recurrere.
What, I ask, does he understand by the union of mind and body? What clear and distinct concept, I say, has he of a thought most closely united to a certain small portion of quantity? I should indeed have liked him to explain this union through its proximate cause. But he had conceived the mind to be so distinct from the body that he could assign no singular cause either of this union or of the mind itself, but had to have recourse to the cause of the whole universe, that is, to God.7
  1. 7 Descartes explains the correspondence of particular movements of the gland with particular thoughts by ‘the institution of nature’ (arts. 36 and 50), and by nature, ‘generaliter spectatam’, he understands ‘vel Deum ipsum, vel rerum creatarum coordinationem a Deo institutam’ (‘either God himself, or the order of created things instituted by God’, Meditations VI, AT VII 80). For Spinoza mind and body are one and the same thing conceived under different attributes (Part II, Prop. 7, Schol.; Part III, Prop. 2, Schol.).
Deinde pervelim scire: quot motus gradus potest glandulae isti pineali mens tribuere, et quanta cum vi eandem suspensam tenere potest? Nam nescio an haec glans tardius vel celerius a mente circumagatur, quam a spiritibus animalibus, et an motus passionum, quos firmis iudiciis arcte iunximus, non possint ab iisdem iterum a causis corporeis disiungi; ex quo sequeretur, ut, quamvis mens firmiter proposuerit contra pericula ire, atque huic decreto motus audaciae iunxerit, viso tamen periculo glans ita suspendatur, ut mens non nisi de fuga possit cogitare.
Next, I should very much like to know how many degrees of motion the mind can give to this pineal gland, and with how much force it can hold it suspended. For I do not know whether this gland is driven about more slowly or more quickly by the mind than by the animal spirits, and whether the motions of the passions which we have closely joined to firm judgments cannot be disjoined from them again by corporeal causes; from which it would follow that, although the mind had firmly resolved to go against dangers, and had joined the motions of daring to this decree, nevertheless, at the sight of the danger, the gland would be so suspended that the mind could think only of flight.
Et sane cum nulla detur ratio voluntatis ad motum, nulla etiam datur comparatio inter mentis et corporis potentiam seu vires; et consequenter huius vires nequaquam viribus illius determinari possunt. His adde, quod nec haec glans ita in medio cerebro sita reperiatur, ut tam facile totque modis circumagi possit, et quod non omnes nervi ad cavitates usque cerebri protendantur. Denique omnia, quae de voluntate eiusque libertate asserit, omitto, quandoquidem haec falsa esse satis superque ostenderim. Igitur quia mentis potentia, ut supra ostendi, sola intelligentia definitur, affectuum remedia, quae omnes experiri quidem, sed non accurate observare, nec distincte videre credo, sola mentis cognitione determinabimus, et ex eadem illa omnia, quae ad ipsius beatitudinem spectant, deducemus.
And indeed, since there is no ratio of will to motion,8 there is also no comparison between the power, or forces, of the mind and those of the body; and consequently the forces of the latter can by no means be determined by the forces of the former. Add to this that this gland is not found to be so situated in the middle of the brain that it can be driven about so easily and in so many ways, and that not all the nerves extend as far as the cavities of the brain. Finally, I pass over everything he asserts about the will and its freedom, since I have shown more than enough that it is false.9 Therefore, since the power of the mind, as I have shown above, is defined by understanding alone, we shall determine the remedies for the affects, which I believe all experience but do not observe accurately or see distinctly, by the knowledge of the mind alone, and from that same knowledge we shall deduce all that concerns its blessedness.

Propositio I

Proposition I

In just the way that thoughts and the ideas of things are ordered and connected in the mind, so the affections of the body, or the images of things, are ordered and connected exactly in the body.1

Propositio II

Proposition II

Si animi commotionem seu affectum a causae externae cogitatione amoveamus et aliis iungamus cogitationibus, tum amor seu odium erga causam externam, ut et animi fluctuationes, quae ex his affectibus oriuntur, destruentur.
If we separate a commotion of the mind, or affect, from the thought of an external cause, and join it to other thoughts, then the love or hatred towards the external cause, as well as the fluctuations of mind which arise from these affects, will be destroyed.

Cited in 5P4S, 5P20S

[G II 282] Demonstratio. Id enim, quod formam amoris vel odii constituit, est laetitia vel tristitia concomitante idea causae externae (per aff. defin. 6. et 7.). Hac igitur sublata amoris vel odii forma simul tollitur; adeoque hi affectus, et qui ex his oriuntur, destruuntur. Q. E. D.
Demonstration. For what constitutes the form of love or hatred is joy or sadness accompanied by the idea of an external cause (by Defs. Aff. 6 and 7). When this is taken away, therefore, the form of love or hatred is taken away at the same time; and so these affects, and those which arise from them, are destroyed. Q.E.D.

Propositio III

Proposition III

Affectus, qui passio est, desinit esse passio, simulatque eius claram et distinctam formamus ideam.
An affect which is a passion ceases to be a passion as soon as we form a clear and distinct idea of it.1
  1. 1 The idea of an affect is not a second thing beside it but the affect itself conceived as an idea (Part II, Prop. 21, Schol.). Cited in 5P18S, 5P20S

Si itaque ipsius affectus claram et distinctam formemus ideam, haec idea ab ipso affectu, quatenus ad solam mentem refertur, non nisi ratione distinguetur (per prop. 21. P. 2. cum eiusdem schol.a); adeoque (per prop. 3. P. 3.) affectus desinet esse passio. Q. E. D.
Demonstration. An affect which is a passion is a confused idea (by the General Definition of the Affects). If, therefore, we form a clear and distinct idea of the affect itself, this idea will be distinguished from the affect itself, insofar as it is related to the mind alone, only by reason (by Prop. 21 of Part II with its Schol.); and so (by Prop. 3 of Part III) the affect will cease to be a passion. Q.E.D.

Propositio V

Proposition V

[G II 284] Affectus erga rem, quam simpliciter et non ut necessariam neque ut possibilem, neque ut contingentem imaginamur, ceteris paribus omnium est maximus.
An affect towards a thing which we imagine simply, and not as necessary, or as possible, or as contingent, is, other things being equal, the greatest of all.

Cited in 5P6D

Demonstratio. Affectus erga rem, quam liberam esse imaginamur, maior est, quam erga necessariam (per prop. 49. P. 3.), et consequenter adhuc maior, quam erga illam, quam ut possibilem vel contingentem imaginamur (per prop. 11. P. 4.). At rem aliquam ut liberam imaginari nihil aliud esse potest, quam quod rem simpliciter imaginamur, dum causas, a quibus ipsa ad agendum determinata fuit, ignoramus (per illa quae in schol. prop. 35. P. 2. ostendimus). Ergo affectus erga rem, quam simpliciter imaginamur, ceteris paribus maior est, quam erga necessariam, possibilem vel contingentem, et consequenter maximus. Q. E. D.
Demonstration. An affect towards a thing which we imagine to be free is greater than towards a necessary thing (by Prop. 49 of Part III), and consequently still greater than towards one which we imagine as possible or contingent (by Prop. 11 of Part IV). But to imagine a thing as free can be nothing other than to imagine the thing simply, while we are ignorant of the causes by which it was determined to act (by what we showed in Schol. of Prop. 35 of Part II). Therefore an affect towards a thing which we imagine simply is, other things being equal, greater than towards a necessary, possible or contingent thing, and consequently the greatest. Q.E.D.

Propositio VI

Proposition VI

Quatenus mens res omnes ut necessarias intelligit, eatenus maiorem in affectus potentiam habet, seu minus ab iisdem patitur.
Insofar as the mind understands all things as necessary, to that extent it has a greater power over the affects, or is acted on less by them.

Cited in 5P10S

Demonstratio. Mens res omnes necessarias esse intelligit (per prop. 29. P. 1.), et infinito causarum nexu determinari ad existendum et operandum (per prop. 28. P. 1.). Adeoque (per prop. praeced.) eatenus efficit, ut ab affectibus, qui ex iis oriuntur, minus patiatur et (per prop. 48. P. 3.) minus erga ipsas afficiatur. Q. E. D.
Demonstration. The mind understands all things to be necessary (by Prop. 29 of Part I) and to be determined to exist and to operate by an infinite connection of causes (by Prop. 28 of Part I). And so (by the preceding Prop.) to that extent it brings it about that it is acted on less by the affects which arise from them, and (by Prop. 48 of Part III) that it is less affected towards them. Q.E.D.
Scholium. Quo haec cognitio, quod scilicet res necessariae sint, magis circa res singulares, quas distinctius et magis vivide imaginamur, versatur, eo haec mentis in affectus potentia maior est, quod ipsa etiam experientia testatur. Videmus enim tristitiam boni alicuius, quod periit, mitigari simulac homo, qui id perdidit, considerat bonum illud servari nulla ratione potuisse. Sic etiam videmus, quod nemo miseretur infantis, propterea quod nescit loqui, ambulare, ratiocinari, et quod denique tot annos quasi sui inscius vivat. At si plerique adulti, et unus aut alter infans nascerentur, tum unumquemque misereret infantum, quia tum ipsam infantiam non ut rem naturalem et necessariam, sed ut naturae vitium seu peccatum consideraret. Et ad hunc modum plura alia notare possemus.
Scholium. The more this knowledge, that things are necessary, concerns singular things which we imagine more distinctly and vividly, the greater is this power of the mind over the affects, as experience itself also testifies. For we see that the sadness over a good which has perished is eased as soon as the man who has lost it considers that the good could not have been kept in any way. So also we see that no one pities an infant because it does not know how to speak, walk or reason, and because, finally, it lives so many years as if unconscious of itself. But if most men were born adults, and only one or another an infant, then everyone would pity infants, because he would then consider infancy itself not as a natural and necessary thing, but as a vice or fault of nature. And we could note many other things of this kind.

Propositio VII

Proposition VII

[G II 285] Affectus, qui ex ratione oriuntur vel excitantur, si ratio temporis habeatur, potentiores sunt iis, qui ad res singulares referuntur, quas ut absentes contemplamur.
Affects which arise from or are aroused by reason are, if account is taken of time,1 more powerful than those which are related to singular things which we contemplate as absent.
  1. 1 This qualifies Part IV, Props. 14–17: in the moment, desires from knowledge of the good are easily overcome; over time, affects related to what is always present, the common properties of things, prevail over affects whose objects are imagined as absent.

Cited in 5P10S, 5P20S

Demonstratio. Rem aliquam ut absentem non contemplamur ex affectu, quo eandem imaginamur, sed ex eo, quod corpus alio afficitur affectu, qui eiusdem rei existentiam secludit (per prop. 17. P. 2.). Quare affectus, qui ad rem, quam ut absentem contemplamur, refertur, eius naturae non est, ut reliquas hominis actiones et potentiam superet (de quibus vide prop. 6. P. 4.), sed contra eius naturae est, ut ab iis affectionibus, quae existentiam externae eius causae secludunt, coerceri aliquo modo possit (per prop. 9. P. 4.).
Demonstration. We contemplate a thing as absent not from the affect by which we imagine it, but from the fact that the body is affected with another affect which excludes the existence of the thing (by Prop. 17 of Part II). Hence the affect which is related to a thing which we contemplate as absent is not of such a nature that it surpasses the other actions and power of a man (on which see Prop. 6 of Part IV), but on the contrary is of such a nature that it can be restrained in some way by those affections which exclude the existence of its external cause (by Prop. 9 of Part IV).
At affectus, qui ex ratione oritur, refertur necessario ad communes rerum proprietates (vide rationis definitionem in schol. 2. prop. 40. P. 2.), quas semper ut praesentes contemplamur (nam nihil dari potest, quod earum praesentem existentiam secludat), et quas semper eodem modo imaginamur (per prop. 38. P. 2.). Quare talis affectus idem semper manet, et consequenter (per axiom. 1. huius) affectus, qui eidem sunt contrarii, quique a suis causis externis non foventur, eidem magis magisque sese accommodare debebunt, donec non amplius sint contrarii, et eatenus affectus, qui ex ratione oritur, est potentior. Q. E. D.
But an affect which arises from reason is necessarily related to the common properties of things (see the definition of reason in Schol. 2 of Prop. 40 of Part II), which we always contemplate as present (for there can be nothing which excludes their present existence), and which we always imagine in the same way (by Prop. 38 of Part II). Hence such an affect always remains the same, and consequently (by Axiom 1 of this Part) the affects which are contrary to it, and which are not encouraged by their external causes, will have to accommodate themselves to it more and more, until they are no longer contrary; and to that extent an affect which arises from reason is more powerful. Q.E.D.

Proposition IX

Proposition X

Proposition XI

[G II 289] Quo imago aliqua ad plures res refertur, eo frequentior est seu saepius viget, et mentem magis occupat.

The Eternity of the Mind

The Scholium to Prop. 20 closes the account of the remedies with the words: “with these I have completed everything which concerns this present life. ... It is time, therefore, to pass to those things which pertain to the duration of the mind without relation to the body.” The phrase is paradoxical on purpose, since what follows denies that this part of the mind has any duration at all, and the Scholium to Prop. 40 closes the account with a more exact one: “the mind insofar as it is considered without relation to the existence of the body.” The propositions between them are the most disputed in the Ethics. They come here because they keep earlier promises: Part II had deferred to Part V an account of the excellence and usefulness of the third kind of knowledge (Part II, Prop. 47, Schol.), Part IV the question how far the death of the body can harm the mind (Part IV, Prop. 39, Schol.), and the Scholium to Prop. 20 itself the nature of the love of God “insofar as it is related to the mind alone.” The reader should know in advance what they claim and how the argument is built.

What the doctrine denies

They begin by taking away what the reader might expect. “The mind can imagine nothing, nor recollect past things, except while the body endures” (Prop. 21). The demonstration rests entirely on Part II: the mind expresses the actual existence of its body only while the body endures (Part II, Prop. 8, Cor.), and imagination and memory are defined by the affections of the body (Part II, Prop. 17, Schol.; Prop. 18, Schol.). They belong to the mind as the idea of an actually existing body, and they end with it.

Whatever is eternal in the mind, therefore, remembers nothing, and Spinoza says so: “it cannot happen that we should remember that we existed before the body, since there can be no traces of this in the body, nor can eternity be defined by time or have any relation to time” (Prop. 23, Schol.). Men, he says, are indeed conscious of the eternity of their mind, but “they confuse it with duration and attribute it to the imagination, or memory, which they believe remains after death” (Prop. 34, Schol.). The common opinion is the confused form of something true, but the life after death of popular religion, in which a person remembers his earthly life and is rewarded or punished for it, is precisely what the doctrine excludes. The eternal part will prove to be the idea of this body and of no other, but it has no memory, no imagination and no relation to time, and Spinoza does not try to show that it is a self in the sense in which we care about our own survival. Steven Nadler argues that on Spinoza’s principles it could not be (Spinoza’s Heresy, 2001, pp. 123–129), and Appendix E takes up the question.

The idea of the body’s essence

What remains? In God there is necessarily an idea which expresses the essence of this or that human body under a species of eternity (Prop. 22). The demonstration returns to Part I: God is the cause of the essence of things as well as of their existence (Part I, Prop. 25), so the essence of this or that human body must be conceived through the essence of God, by a certain eternal necessity, and this concept must be in God (Part II, Prop. 3). Part II had prepared the doctrine: the ideas of things which do not exist are comprehended in God’s infinite idea as their formal essences are contained in his attributes (Part II, Prop. 8). Its corollary distinguishes two ways in which the ideas of things are in God: as comprehended in God’s infinite idea, and, when the things endure, as involving the existence “through which they are said to endure” (Part II, Prop. 8, Cor.). The demonstrations of Props. 21 and 23 cite it for the second; Prop. 22 asserts of the human body what the first says of every thing.

The demonstration of Prop. 23 then takes the step on which everything turns. The idea in God which expresses the essence of the human body “is necessarily something that pertains to the essence of the human mind,” since the mind is the idea of the body (Part II, Prop. 13); duration belongs to the mind only insofar as it expresses the body’s actual existence; and since this idea is conceived by an eternal necessity through God’s essence, “this something, which pertains to the essence of the mind, will necessarily be eternal” (Prop. 23, Dem.). Hence “the human mind cannot be absolutely destroyed with the body, but something of it remains which is eternal” (Prop. 23).

The argument is asymmetrical. Its premise concerns the essence of the body, its conclusion the mind, and Spinoza never says that something of the body remains. Yet nothing in the demonstration of Prop. 22 is peculiar to human bodies, and Part II had said that what holds of the idea of the human body “must necessarily be said of the idea of any thing” (Part II, Prop. 13, Schol.). Whether the doctrine then reduces to the truism that every essence is eternal, as Jonathan Bennett thought Edwin Curley’s “symmetrical” reading made it (Bennett, A Study of Spinoza’s Ethics, 1984, pp. 358–359), is one of the questions Appendix E takes up. Nadler distinguishes two eternities of the mind: one that the idea of every body has as the idea of its essence, no more personal in a man than in a tree, and one acquired by rational minds alone, which consists in their adequate ideas (Nadler, pp. 111–122). On this reading, the rest of the argument is about the second, of which a mind can have more or less.

Eternity, not duration

The word “remains” is misleading if it is taken to mean “continues afterwards.” Eternity here has the sense of Part I, Def. 8: existence itself, insofar as it is conceived to follow necessarily from the definition of an eternal thing, which “cannot be explained through duration or time, even if the duration is conceived to lack a beginning and an end” (Part I, Def. 8, Expl.). On the strict reading, which Appendix E sets beside more temporal ones, the eternal part of the mind does not exist at all times, or after the body’s death; it exists in a way that has no relation to time. So “our mind ... can be said to endure, and its existence can be defined by a certain time, only insofar as it involves the actual existence of the body,” and insofar as it involves the essence of the body under a species of eternity, its existence “cannot be defined by time or explained through duration” (Prop. 23, Schol.).

The claim is not only deduced but felt. “But nevertheless we feel and experience that we are eternal. For the mind feels those things which it conceives in understanding no less than those which it has in memory. For the eyes of the mind, by which it sees and observes things, are the demonstrations themselves” (Prop. 23, Schol.). The passage is often quoted, and it is easily misread as an appeal to mystical experience. Read with the sentence that follows it, it seems to point the other way. The experience of eternity is the experience of understanding something by demonstration, of seeing that it must be so; when we understand in this way, we are aware of a kind of knowing that is not tied to the moment at which it occurs, as “he who has a true idea knows at the same time that he has a true idea” (Part II, Prop. 43). The demonstrations are the eyes with which the mind sees, and what it sees, when it sees anything necessary, is the eternal.

The phrase “under a species of eternity” (sub specie aeternitatis) first appeared, as “under a certain species of eternity,” in Part II, Prop. 44, Cor. 2, where it is said to be of the nature of reason to perceive things in this way, because the necessity it perceives “is the very necessity of God’s eternal nature,” and because the common notions on which it rests “must therefore be conceived without any relation to time” (Part II, Prop. 44, Cor. 2, Dem.). Part V joins this strand to Props. 22–23. Whatever the mind understands under a species of eternity, it understands “not from the fact that it conceives the present actual existence of the body, but from the fact that it conceives the essence of the body under a species of eternity” (Prop. 29). The demonstration shows how Spinoza builds: it is of the nature of reason to conceive things under a species of eternity, it pertains to the mind to conceive the essence of the body so, “and besides these two nothing else pertains to the essence of the mind” (Prop. 29, Dem.). The power which Part II ascribed to reason belongs to the mind, then, only insofar as the mind is eternal. The scholium explains the phrase: “Things are conceived by us as actual in two ways: either insofar as we conceive them to exist in relation to a certain time and place, or insofar as we conceive them to be contained in God and to follow from the necessity of the divine nature” (Prop. 29, Schol.). To conceive the essence of one’s body under a species of eternity is not to imagine it lasting forever, but to understand it as following from the nature of God, and so to know that one “is in God and is conceived through God” (Prop. 30).

The third kind of knowledge

Props. 24–28 turn from what the mind is to what it strives for. “The more we understand singular things, the more we understand God” (Prop. 24), since singular things are modes by which God’s attributes are expressed (Part I, Prop. 25, Cor.). The Dutch version of 1677 adds “or have more understanding of God”; the note to the proposition gives Gebhardt’s account of that reading. The demonstration of Prop. 25 joins this to earlier results: the third kind of knowledge proceeds from an adequate idea of certain attributes of God to the adequate knowledge of the essence of things (Part II, Prop. 40, Schol. 2), and the highest virtue of the mind, that is, its power and its striving, is to know God (Part IV, Prop. 28). Hence “the highest striving of the mind, and its highest virtue, is to understand things by the third kind of knowledge” (Prop. 25). The more capable of this the mind is, the more it desires it (Prop. 26); from it arises the greatest contentment of mind there can be (Prop. 27); and the desire for it “cannot arise from the first kind of knowledge, but it can from the second” (Prop. 28). Don Garrett notes that human beings generally reach the third kind only after the second (Nature and Necessity in Spinoza’s Philosophy, 2018, p. 211).

What distinguishes the third kind from the second? Bennett observed that Part II gives little help, since it contrasts the first kind with the other two, never the second with the third (Bennett, p. 364). In the Scholium to Prop. 36 Spinoza says that he has noted how our mind follows from the divine nature in order to show “how much the knowledge of singular things which I have called intuitive, or of the third kind ..., can do”: the general demonstration of Part I that all things depend on God is certain, but it “does not affect our mind as much as when this same thing is concluded from the very essence of any singular thing which we say depends on God” (Prop. 36, Schol.). On Garrett’s reading, the demonstration of Part I gives knowledge of the second kind because it rests on a property common to everything, not on the mind’s own essence (p. 208); the third kind is more powerful because it runs from essences to what follows from them, as from causes to effects, in the order in which God himself knows (pp. 212–213). Spinoza’s conclusion is that the third kind of knowledge “depends on the mind as on its formal cause, insofar as the mind itself is eternal” (Prop. 31): insofar as it is eternal, the mind has the adequate knowledge of God which every idea involves (Part II, Prop. 46), and can know whatever follows from it.

The intellectual love of God

From here on, Spinoza warns, he will speak of the mind, as he has done so far, “as if it were now beginning to be, and were now beginning to understand things under a species of eternity,” although we are certain that it is eternal; this may be done “without any danger of error, provided we take care to conclude nothing except from clear premises” (Prop. 31, Schol.). The language of growth and acquisition that follows describes, in temporal terms, relations that are not temporal. Bennett saw in the device an attempt to hide a split in the doctrine: how much of my mind is eternal seems fixed by eternal truths, yet the same pages speak of self-improvement (p. 363).

Whatever we understand by the third kind of knowledge we take delight in, with the idea of God as its cause (Prop. 32); by the definition of love (Part III, Def. Aff. 6) this is a love of God, “not insofar as we imagine him as present ..., but insofar as we understand God to be eternal; and this is what I call the intellectual love of God” (Prop. 32, Cor.). It is not the love of God of Props. 15–16 as related to the body: that love arose as the mind related the affections of the body to the idea of God, and in that relation, the Scholium to Prop. 20 said, it cannot be destroyed except with the body. This love arises from the third kind of knowledge, and since that knowledge is eternal, so is the love (Prop. 33). The scholium corrects the corollary: this love has had no beginning, but “it nevertheless has all the perfections of love, just as if it had come into being” (Prop. 33, Schol.). It also resolves a difficulty left by Part III, which defined joy as a passage to a greater perfection and warned that “joy is not perfection itself” (Part III, Def. Aff. 3, Expl.). The scholium concludes: “if joy consists in the passage to a greater perfection, blessedness must surely consist in this, that the mind is endowed with perfection itself” (Prop. 33, Schol.). H. A. Wolfson noted that the difficulty was old: Crescas had held that the delight of knowing lies in passing from potentiality to actuality, and found the answer in a pleasure of constant possession (The Philosophy of Spinoza, 1934, II 307–308).

Only while the body endures is the mind subject to passions, and so “no love except intellectual love is eternal” (Prop. 34, Cor.). But “God loves himself with an infinite intellectual love” (Prop. 35), and the mind’s intellectual love of God “is part of the infinite love by which God loves himself” (Prop. 36). The demonstration, by way of Part II, Prop. 11, Cor., makes the mind’s contemplation of itself “an action by which God, insofar as he can be explained through the human mind, contemplates himself, accompanied by the idea of himself” (Prop. 36, Dem.). The essay before Part V explains why this does not contradict the denial that God loves anyone (Prop. 17, Cor.). The demonstration of Prop. 17 gives the reason: what is denied of God is joy as a passage, and what is affirmed here is perfection possessed, which the Scholium to Prop. 36 calls joy only “if we may still use this word” (Prop. 36, Schol.). Nothing in nature can take this love away (Prop. 37), for the axiom of Part IV, that every singular thing can be destroyed by a stronger one, “concerns singular things insofar as they are considered in relation to a certain time and place” (Prop. 37, Schol.).

The measure of the eternal part

The argument returns to the body. “The more things the mind understands by the second and third kinds of knowledge, the less it is acted on by affects which are evil, and the less it fears death” (Prop. 38), because the more it knows in these ways, “the greater the part of it that remains” (Prop. 38, Dem.). The scholium names the promise of Part IV which this keeps, “that death is the less harmful, the greater the mind’s clear and distinct knowledge” (Prop. 38, Schol.).

Then comes the most troubling proposition: “He who has a body capable of a great many things has a mind whose greatest part is eternal” (Prop. 39). Its demonstration runs through the first half of the Part. Such a man is least troubled by evil affects, and so he can order the affections of the body according to the order of the intellect (Prop. 10), relate them all to the idea of God (Prop. 14), and love God (Prop. 15) with a love that occupies the greatest part of the mind (Prop. 16); “and accordingly (by Prop. 33 of this Part) he has a mind whose greatest part is eternal” (Prop. 39, Dem.). The scholium explains why the body matters. An infant, whose body is capable of very few things, has a mind which “is conscious of almost nothing of itself, or of God, or of things”; and so “in this life” we strive that the body of infancy may change into one capable of a great many things (Prop. 39, Schol.). The principle is Part II’s: the more a body can do and undergo, the more its mind can perceive (Part II, Prop. 13, Schol.).

The part that remains is the intellect, the part that perishes the imagination (Prop. 40, Cor.). The intellect, the corollary says, is that “through which alone we are said to act,” so that, as Bennett and Garrett both note, the mind’s eternity is tied to reason as well as to intuition (Bennett, p. 369; Garrett, p. 212). The vocabulary is old. Aristotle had said of the intellect that makes all things: Χωρισθεὶς δ’ ἐστὶ μόνον τοῦθ’ ὅπερ ἐστί, καὶ τοῦτο μόνον ἀθάνατον καὶ ἀΐδιον (“when separated it is only what it is, and this alone is immortal and eternal”); and he added, in a remark that invites comparison with Spinoza’s, that we do not remember: Οὐ μνημονεύομεν δέ, ὅτι τοῦτο μὲν ἀπαθές, ὁ δὲ παθητικὸς νοῦς φθαρτός (“but we do not remember, because this is not acted on, while the passive intellect is perishable”; De anima III.5, 430a22–25). Appendix E describes the medieval doctrine of the acquired intellect, which, as Wolfson notes, is not found in Aristotle but grew out of medieval expositions of his account of the intellect (II 54). Spinoza’s last word is the Scholium to Prop. 40: our mind, insofar as it understands, is an eternal mode of thinking determined by another, and that by another, and so to infinity, “so that all together constitute the eternal and infinite intellect of God” (Prop. 40, Schol.). Part II had called the human mind “part of the infinite intellect of God” (Part II, Prop. 11, Cor.); the end of Part V shows which part, and why it can be greater or smaller.

The remedies seen from eternity

Prop. 39 shows how closely the two halves of Part V are joined. On the reading offered here, the second adds no new remedy, but regards the remedies of the first from the side of eternity. The Scholium to Prop. 20 had already said that clear and distinct knowledge, “and especially that third kind of knowledge ..., whose foundation is the knowledge of God itself,” begets “love towards an immutable and eternal thing ..., which we really possess” (Prop. 20, Schol.). The second half explains how we possess that thing, and why the love of it is eternal, and its demonstrations draw on the first: Prop. 39 runs through Props. 10 and 14–16, and the last demonstration of the book cites the corollary of Prop. 3, that an affect is the more in our power the better it is known (Prop. 42, Dem.). What the first half describes as a process in time, in which adequate ideas come to make up more of the mind and the passions less, the second describes as a fact about those ideas: they are in God, they are eternal, and the more of the mind they constitute, the less of it can be destroyed. The essay before Part V asks whether the two halves describe two kinds of freedom or two aspects of one. The demonstrations suggest one freedom, conceived in the two ways the Scholium to Prop. 29 distinguished; Bennett, as noted above, saw instead a split that cannot be healed (p. 363).

An ethics that does not wait for it

Spinoza adds at once that the ethics of Part IV does not depend on any of this. “Even if we did not know that our mind is eternal, we would still regard as of first importance piety and religion, and absolutely all the things which we showed in Part IV to be related to tenacity and nobility” (Prop. 41). In determining what reason dictates, he recalls, “we took no account of the eternity of the mind, which we have come to know only in this fifth Part” (Prop. 41, Dem.). The doctrine is not offered as a reward for virtue, or as a motive for it, but as a further truth about what the mind is. The scholium, which attacks the belief that piety is a burden to be repaid after death, contains the only occurrence of the word “immortal” in the Ethics, in the absurd reasoning of someone who “sees that the mind is not eternal, or immortal,” and so prefers to live without reason (Prop. 41, Schol.). Nadler notes how carefully the book avoids the traditional vocabulary (pp. 108–111). The early Short Treatise still had a chapter “Of the Immortality of the Soul,” Van des Ziels Onsterfelykheid, in which the soul’s fate depends on whether it is united with the body alone or with God (KV II.23; G I 102–103); the Ethics speaks of eternity instead. Whatever the reader concludes about the doctrine, the remedies of the first half of Part V and the ethics of Part IV stand on their own.

The text resumes with Prop. 21, whose demonstration rests on Part II, as that of Prop. 22 rests on Parts I and II; the main claim comes in Prop. 23 and its scholium. Props. 24–31 treat the third kind of knowledge, Props. 32–37 the intellectual love of God, and Props. 38–40 the measure of the eternal part. After Prop. 40 the last two propositions of the Ethics turn from the eternity of the mind back to the life of virtue, and conclude that “blessedness is not the reward of virtue, but virtue itself” (Prop. 42).

Demonstration. God is the cause not only of the existence of this or that human body, but also of its essence (by Prop. 25 of Part I), which therefore must necessarily be conceived through the very essence of God (by Axiom 4 of Part I), and that by a certain eternal necessity (by Prop. 16 of Part I); and this concept must necessarily be in God (by Prop. 3 of Part II). Q.E.D.

Propositio XXIII

Proposition XXIII

Mens humana non potest cum corpore absolute destrui, sed eius aliquid remanet quod aeternum est.
The human mind cannot be absolutely destroyed with the body, but something of it remains which is eternal.

Cited in 5P29D, 5P31D, 5P38D, 5P40C

Demonstratio. In Deo datur necessario conceptus seu idea, quae corporis humani essentiam exprimit (per prop. praeced.), quae propterea aliquid necessario est, quod ad essentiam mentis humanae pertinet (per prop. 13. P. 2.). Sed menti humanae nullam durationem, quae tempore definiri potest, tribuimus, nisi quatenus corporis actualem existentiam, quae per durationem explicatur et tempore definiri potest, exprimit, hoc est (per coroll. prop. 8. P. 2.) ipsi durationem non tribuimus nisi durante corpore. Cum tamen aliquid nihilominus sit id, quod aeterna quadam necessitate per ipsam Dei essentiam concipitur (per prop. praeced.), erit necessario hoc aliquid, quod ad mentis essentiam pertinet, aeternum. Q. E. D.
Demonstration. In God there is necessarily a concept, or idea, which expresses the essence of the human body (by the preceding Prop.), and which therefore is necessarily something that pertains to the essence of the human mind (by Prop. 13 of Part II). But we attribute to the human mind no duration which can be defined by time, except insofar as it expresses the actual existence of the body, which is explained by duration and can be defined by time; that is (by Cor. of Prop. 8 of Part II), we do not attribute duration to it except while the body endures. Since, however, there is nevertheless something which is conceived by a certain eternal necessity through the very essence of God (by the preceding Prop.), this something, which pertains to the essence of the mind, will necessarily be eternal. Q.E.D.
Scholium. Est, uti diximus, haec idea, quae corporis essentiam sub specie aeternitatis exprimit, certus cogitandi modus, qui ad mentis essentiam pertinet, quique necessario aeternus est. Nec tamen fieri potest, ut recordemur nos ante corpus exstitisse, quandoquidem nec in corpore ulla eius vestigia dari,a nec aeternitas tempore definiri, nec ullam ad tempus relationem habere potest.
Scholium. This idea, which expresses the essence of the body under a species of eternity, is, as we have said, a certain mode of thinking which pertains to the essence of the mind and which is necessarily eternal. And yet it cannot happen that we should remember that we existed before the body, since there can be no traces of this in the body, nor can eternity be defined by time or have any relation to time.
  1. a From the following potest a possunt must be understood after dari; the Dutch seems to have the same inconcinnity. W. Meijer (second edition), followed by Appuhn, would put possunt into the text. Gebhardt (G II 296; Textgestaltung II 390–391) leaves the text, and rejects Halpern’s view that Spinoza meant only that there are no such traces (dantur), not that there can be none.
At nihilominus sentimus experimurque, nos aeternos esse. Nam mens non minus res illas sentit, quas intelligendo concipit, quam quas in memoria habet. Mentis enim oculi, quibus res videt observatque, sunt ipsae demonstrationes.
But nevertheless we feel and experience that we are eternal. For the mind feels those things which it conceives in understanding no less than those which it has in memory. For the eyes of the mind, by which it sees and observes things, are the demonstrations themselves.
Quamvis itaque non recordemur nos ante corpus exstitisse, sentimus tamen mentem nostram, quatenus corporis essentiam sub aeternitatis specie involvit, aeternam esse, et hanc eius existentiam tempore definiri sive per durationem explicari non posse. Mens igitur nostra eatenus tantum potest dici durare, eiusque existentia certo tempore definiri potest, quatenus actualem corporis existentiam involvit, et eatenus tantum potentiam habet rerum existentiam tempore determinandi, easque sub duratione concipiendi.
Although, therefore, we do not remember that we existed before the body, we nevertheless feel that our mind, insofar as it involves the essence of the body under a species of eternity, is eternal, and that this existence of it cannot be defined by time or explained through duration. Our mind, therefore, can be said to endure, and its existence can be defined by a certain time, only insofar as it involves the actual existence of the body, and only to that extent does it have the power of determining the existence of things by time and of conceiving them under duration.

Propositio XXV

Propositio XXVIII

Proposition XXVIII

Conatus seu cupiditas cognoscendi res tertio cognitionis genere oriri non potest ex primo, at quidem ex secundo cognitionis genere.
The striving, or desire, to know things by the third kind of knowledge cannot arise from the first kind of knowledge, but it can from the second.
Demonstratio. Haec propositio per se patet. Nam quicquid clare et distincte intelligimus, id vel per se vel per aliud, quod per se concipitur, intelligimus; hoc est, ideae, quae in nobis clarae et distinctae sunt, sive quae ad tertium cognitionis genus referuntur (vide schol. 2. prop. 40. P. 2.), non possunt sequi ex ideis mutilatis et confusis, quae (per idem schol.) ad primum cognitionis genus referuntur, sed ex ideis adaequatis, sive (per idem schol.) ex secundo et tertio cognitionis genere. Ac proinde (per aff. defin. 1.) cupiditas cognoscendi res tertio cognitionis genere non potest oriri ex primo, at quidem ex secundo. Q. E. D.
Demonstration. This proposition is evident through itself. For whatever we understand clearly and distinctly, we understand either through itself or through something else which is conceived through itself; that is, ideas which are clear and distinct in us, or which are related to the third kind of knowledge (see Schol. 2 of Prop. 40 of Part II), cannot follow from mutilated and confused ideas, which (by the same Schol.) are related to the first kind of knowledge, but from adequate ideas, or (by the same Schol.) from the second and third kind of knowledge. And accordingly (by Def. Aff. 1) the desire to know things by the third kind of knowledge cannot arise from the first, but it can from the second. Q.E.D.

Propositio XXIX

Proposition XXIX

[G II 298] Quicquid mens sub specie aeternitatis intelligit, id ex eo non intelligit, quod corporis praesentem actualem existentiam concipit; sed ex eo, quod corporis essentiam concipit sub specie aeternitatis.
Whatever the mind understands under a species of eternity, it understands not from the fact that it conceives the present actual existence of the body, but from the fact that it conceives the essence of the body under a species of eternity.

Cited in 5P31D, 5P32C, 5P37D, 5P38D, 5P40C

Demonstratio. Quatenus mens praesentem sui corporis existentiam concipit, eatenus durationem concipit, quae tempore determinari potest, et eatenus tantum potentiam habet concipiendi res cum relatione ad tempus (per prop. 21. huius et prop. 26. P. 2.). At aeternitas per durationem explicari nequit (per defin. 8. P. 1. et ipsius explic.).
Demonstration. Insofar as the mind conceives the present existence of its body, to that extent it conceives duration, which can be determined by time, and to that extent only does it have the power of conceiving things in relation to time (by Prop. 21 of this Part and Prop. 26 of Part II). But eternity cannot be explained by duration (by Def. 8 of Part I and its Explanation).
Ergo mens eatenus potestatem non habet concipiendi res sub specie aeternitatis; sed quia de natura rationis est, res sub specie aeternitatis concipere (per coroll. 2. prop. 44. P. 2.), et ad mentis naturam etiam pertinet, corporis essentiam sub specie aeternitatis concipere (per prop. 23. huius), et praeter haec duo nihil aliud ad mentis essentiam pertinet (per prop. 13. P. 2.), ergoa haec potentia concipiendi res sub specie aeternitatis ad mentem non pertinet, nisi quatenus corporis essentiam sub specie aeternitatis concipit. Q. E. D.
Therefore to that extent the mind does not have the power of conceiving things under a species of eternity; but because it is of the nature of reason to conceive things under a species of eternity (by Cor. 2 of Prop. 44 of Part II), and it also pertains to the nature of the mind to conceive the essence of the body under a species of eternity (by Prop. 23 of this Part), and besides these two nothing else pertains to the essence of the mind (by Prop. 13 of Part II), therefore this power of conceiving things under a species of eternity does not pertain to the mind except insofar as it conceives the essence of the body under a species of eternity. Q.E.D.
  1. a The 1677 edition puts a full stop after pertinet and begins a new sentence with Ergo; the Dutch (behoort; … zo) shows that the sentence runs on, and Gebhardt (G II 298; Textgestaltung II 391) corrects the punctuation, as Leopold asked.
Scholium. Res duobus modis a nobis ut actuales concipiuntur, vel quatenus easdem cum relatione ad certum tempus et locum existere, vel quatenus ipsas in Deo contineri et ex naturae divinae necessitate consequi concipimus. Quae autem hoc secundo modo ut verae seu reales concipiuntur, eas sub aeternitatis specie concipimus, et earum ideae aeternam et infinitam Dei essentiam involvunt, ut prop. 45. P. 2. ostendimus, cuius etiam schol. vide.
Scholium. Things are conceived by us as actual in two ways: either insofar as we conceive them to exist in relation to a certain time and place, or insofar as we conceive them to be contained in God and to follow from the necessity of the divine nature.1 But those which are conceived as true, or real, in this second way, we conceive under a species of eternity, and their ideas involve the eternal and infinite essence of God, as we showed in Prop. 45 of Part II; see also its Schol..
  1. 1 The two ways correspond to existence as duration and existence as it follows from God’s nature. Part II, Prop. 45, Schol. had already drawn the line: ‘by existence I do not here understand duration, that is, existence insofar as it is conceived abstractly and as a certain species of quantity’, but ‘the very nature of existence which is attributed to singular things because infinitely many things follow in infinitely many ways from the eternal necessity of God’s nature’.

Propositio XXX

Proposition XXX

[G II 299] Mens nostra quatenus se et corpus sub aeternitatis specie cognoscit, eatenus Dei cognitionem necessario habet, scitque se in Deo esse et per Deum concipi.
Our mind, insofar as it knows itself and the body under a species of eternity, to that extent necessarily has knowledge of God, and knows that it is in God and is conceived through God.

Cited in 5P31D, 5P32D

Demonstratio. Aeternitas est ipsa Dei essentia, quatenus haec necessariam involvit existentiam (per defin. 8. P. 1.). Res igitur sub specie aeternitatis concipere est res concipere, quatenus per Dei essentiam ut entia realia concipiuntur, sive quatenus per Dei essentiam involvunt existentiam. Adeoque mens nostra quatenus se et corpus sub specie aeternitatis concipit, eatenus Dei cognitionem necessario habet, scitque etc. Q. E. D.
Demonstration. Eternity is the very essence of God insofar as this involves necessary existence (by Def. 8 of Part I). To conceive things under a species of eternity, therefore, is to conceive things insofar as they are conceived as real beings through the essence of God, or insofar as through the essence of God they involve existence. And so our mind, insofar as it conceives itself and the body under a species of eternity, to that extent necessarily has knowledge of God, and knows, etc. Q.E.D.

Propositio XXXI

Proposition XXXI

Tertium cognitionis genus pendet a mente, tamquam a formali causa, quatenus mens ipsa aeterna est.
The third kind of knowledge depends on the mind as on its formal cause, insofar as the mind itself is eternal.

Cited in 5P33D

Demonstratio. Mens nihil sub aeternitatis specie concipit, nisi quatenus sui corporis essentiam sub aeternitatis specie concipit (per prop. 29. huius), hoc est (per prop. 21. et 23. huius) nisi quatenus aeterna est. Adeoque (per prop. praeced.) quatenus aeterna est, Dei habet cognitionem, quae quidem cognitio est necessario adaequata (per prop. 46. P. 2.); ac proinde mens quatenus aeterna est, ad illa omnia cognoscendum est apta, quae ex data hac Dei cognitione consequi possunt (per prop. 40. P. 2.), hoc est, ad res tertio cognitionis genere cognoscendum (vide huius definitionem in schol. 2. prop. 40. P. 2.), cuius propterea mens (per defin. 1. P. 3.) quatenus aeterna est, causa est adaequata seu formalis. Q. E. D.
Demonstration. The mind conceives nothing under a species of eternity except insofar as it conceives the essence of its body under a species of eternity (by Prop. 29 of this Part), that is (by Props. 21 and 23 of this Part), except insofar as it is eternal. And so (by the preceding Prop.) insofar as it is eternal it has knowledge of God, which is necessarily adequate (by Prop. 46 of Part II); and accordingly the mind, insofar as it is eternal, is capable of knowing all those things which can follow from this given knowledge of God (by Prop. 40 of Part II), that is, of knowing things by the third kind of knowledge (see its definition in Schol. 2 of Prop. 40 of Part II), of which the mind, insofar as it is eternal, is therefore (by Def. 1 of Part III) the adequate, or formal, cause.1 Q.E.D.
  1. 1 causa formalis is here equated with the adequate cause of Part III, Def. 1: a cause whose effect can be perceived clearly and distinctly through it. The third kind of knowledge follows from the mind’s own nature insofar as that nature is eternal.
[G II 300] Scholium. Quo igitur unusquisque hoc cognitionis genere plus pollet, eo melius sui et Dei conscius est, hoc est, eo est perfectior et beatior, quod adhuc clarius ex seqq. patebit. Sed hic notandum, quod, tametsi iam certi sumus, mentem aeternam esse, quatenus res sub aeternitatis specie concipit, nos tamen, ut ea, quae ostendere volumus, facilius explicentur et melius intelligantur, ipsam, tamquam iam inciperet esse, et res sub aeternitatis specie intelligere iam inciperet, considerabimus, ut hucusque fecimus; quod nobis absque ullo erroris periculo facere licet, modo nobis cautio sit nihil concludere, nisi ex perspicuis praemissis.
Scholium. The more, therefore, each one is capable in this kind of knowledge, the better he is conscious of himself and of God, that is, the more perfect and blessed he is, as will be evident still more clearly from what follows. But it should be noted here that, although we are already certain that the mind is eternal insofar as it conceives things under a species of eternity, nevertheless, so that what we wish to show may be explained more easily and better understood, we shall consider it, as we have done so far, as if it were now beginning to be, and were now beginning to understand things under a species of eternity; which we may do without any danger of error, provided we take care to conclude nothing except from clear premises.

Propositio XXXIV

Proposition XXXIV

Mens non nisi durante corpore obnoxia est affectibus, qui ad passiones referuntur.
Only while the body endures is the mind subject to affects which are related to passions.
Demonstratio. Imaginatio est idea, qua mens rem aliquam ut praesentem contemplatur (vide eius definitionem in schol. prop. 17. Est igitur affectus (per gener. aff. defin.) imaginatio, quatenus corporis praesentem constitutionem indicat; atque adeo (per prop. 21. huius) mens non nisi durante corpore obnoxia est affectibus, qui ad passiones referuntur. Q. E. D.
Demonstration. An imagination is an idea by which the mind contemplates some thing as present (see its definition in Schol. of Prop. 17 of Part II), but which indicates the present constitution of the human body more than the nature of the external thing (by Cor. 2 of Prop. 16 of Part II). An affect, therefore (by the General Definition of the Affects), is an imagination, insofar as it indicates the present constitution of the body; and so (by Prop. 21 of this Part) only while the body endures is the mind subject to affects which are related to passions. Q.E.D.

Atque adeo ex eo, quod mens hoc amore divino seu beatitudine gaudet, potestatem habet libidines coercendi; et quia humana potentia ad coercendos affectus in solo intellectu consistit, ergo nemo beatitudine gaudet, quia affectus coercuit, sed contra potestas libidines coercendi ex ipsa beatitudine oritur. Q. E. D.
Next, the more the mind enjoys this divine love, or blessedness, the more it understands (by Prop. 32 of this Part), that is (by Cor. of Prop. 3 of this Part), the greater the power it has over the affects, and (by Prop. 38 of this Part) the less it is acted on by affects which are evil. And so, from the fact that the mind enjoys this divine love, or blessedness, it has the power of restraining lusts; and because human power to restrain the affects consists in the intellect alone, no one enjoys blessedness because he has restrained the affects, but on the contrary the power of restraining lusts arises from blessedness itself. Q.E.D.
[G II 308] Scholium. His omnia, quae de mentis in affectus potentia, quaeque de mentis libertate ostendere volueram, absolvi. Ex quibus apparet, quantum sapiens polleat, potiorque sit ignaro, qui sola libidine agitur. Ignarus enim, praeterquam quoda a causis externis multis modis agitatur, nec unquam vera animi acquiescentia potitur, vivit praeterea sui et Dei et rerum quasi inscius, et simulac pati desinit, simul etiam esse desinit. Cum contra sapiens, quatenus ut talis consideratur, vix animo movetur, sed sui et Dei et rerum aeterna quadam necessitate conscius, nunquam esse desinit, sed semper vera animi acquiescentia potitur.
Scholium. With these I have completed all that I wished to show concerning the power of the mind over the affects and concerning the freedom of the mind. From which it is evident how much the wise man is capable of, and how much more powerful he is than the ignorant man, who is driven by lust alone. For the ignorant man, besides being driven about in many ways by external causes and never possessing true contentment of mind, lives as if unconscious of himself, and of God, and of things, and as soon as he ceases to be acted on, he also ceases to be. The wise man, on the other hand, insofar as he is considered as such, is hardly moved in mind, but, being conscious of himself, and of God, and of things by a certain eternal necessity, never ceases to be, but always possesses true contentment of mind.
