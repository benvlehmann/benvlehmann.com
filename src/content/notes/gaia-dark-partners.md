Gaia has spent a decade measuring where the stars are and how they move. One byproduct is an enormous catalog of binaries, and among them are a handful in which an ordinary star orbits something heavy that gives off no light. They are called Gaia BH1, BH2, and BH3; the dark partner is presumed a black hole. In a new paper with Badal Bhalla, David Dunsky, Akshay Ghalsasi, Jackie Lodman, and Kuver Sinha, we argue that such systems probe something specific: whether any part of dark matter can cool.

## Partner swapping

A star does not usually form in orbit around a black hole, but the pair can be assembled after the fact. Take two stars already orbiting each other, and let a lone black hole wander past. If it comes close enough, the three bodies tumble around one another, and sometimes a star is flung out and the black hole stays in its place. This is exchange, a leading explanation for Gaia's systems.

We showed earlier that primordial black holes, if they were the dark matter, would be exchanged into binaries at roughly the rate Gaia implies. Those black holes move at halo speeds, hundreds of kilometers per second. But the exchange rate grows sharply as the intruder slows down, roughly as the inverse square of its speed. For most ways of detecting dark objects, slow is unhelpful. For binaries, slow is exactly what you want.

## Dark matter that cools

Ordinary dark matter cannot radiate, so it never cools, never settles, and never forms anything smaller than a galaxy. Stars exist because ordinary gas *can*.

A dark sector need not be so simple. In *dissipative* dark matter models, a small subcomponent has a dark version of electromagnetism: dark protons, dark electrons, a dark photon. It can form dark atoms and molecules, cool, and sink into a thin disk alongside the visible one. If the disk fragments, it makes compact dark objects, dark stars or black holes; either way, point masses. They inherit the motion of the cold gas that made them: born slow, and living in the disk, among the stellar binaries.

That is the whole idea, and cooling is the only ingredient it needs. Cooling makes compact objects, the same cooling makes them slow, and slow objects get exchanged into binaries.

## What we find

We take a benchmark atomic dark matter model and follow a Milky Way-like halo through its history: when the dark gas cools, how massive a disk it builds, and how heavy the compact objects should be. We compute the exchange cross section for slow intruders, check it against three-body simulations, and integrate over the Galaxy's binaries.

Across a wide range of parameters, with conservative assumptions, the Milky Way should hold on the order of ten thousand star–dark-object binaries, comparable to what Gaia BH1 suggests, and in places well above it. Exchange also favors heavy dark objects, and a heavier partner swings its star around faster, which is what Gaia is built to notice.

Three systems cannot tell us that Gaia's black holes are dark matter, and whether ordinary stellar channels can make them is still debated. Our dark disk's thickness and mass fraction rest on simulations at two parameter points, and we took it to be as thick as the visible disk, which if anything undercounts the exchanges.

## Why I like it

Everything we know about dark matter, we learned from gravity. If it never interacts with us by any other means, we still want to know whether it can cool, because cooling is what turns a diffuse fog into structure. We used atomic dark matter because we trust the calculation there, but the ingredient is cooling, not atoms; any dark sector with a cold subcomponent should behave similarly. Astronomers have long used stars as tracers of dark matter on galactic scales; here they trace it at the scale of a single binary, and a question about microphysics becomes a counting problem. Gaia's next data release should expand the catalog, and given a model of what Gaia can see, parts of this parameter space could be ruled out by the census alone. A dark companion lighter than the Sun would be the cleanest find of all, since stellar evolution makes no black holes that small.
